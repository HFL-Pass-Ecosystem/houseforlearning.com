Childcare Products and Services

Plan a safe, small childcare product or service for families, test it with parents and record the results.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 124 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Plan a safe, small childcare product or service for families, test it with parents and record the results.

  • Understand Explain safety first in childcare.
  • Apply Run a small trial with consent.
  • Analyse Analyse parent feedback and incidents.
  • Create Design a product or service sheet.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design and trial one childcare product or service for 30 days with real families and record feedback and safety checks.

Condition

About 120 hours over 3 to 5 months. Partner setting or families who consent, free forms, a qualified adviser, mentor and peer circle.

Standard

Safety plan reviewed by a qualified adviser; consent forms; 30-day record; parent feedback; at least 70% on the check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (124 h guided)

Module 1 Know the need 8 hUnderstand

Parents choose a childcare product or service because they trust it and it makes their day easier. This module teaches you to ask parents about real problems and to choose one need. You will not design anything until you hear the same problem more than once.

  • Interview ten parents for 10 to 15 minutes each, and ask what was hardest last week, not what they would like you to make.
  • Write each problem on a card and count how many parents mention it, then rank the cards from most to least frequent.
  • Ask what they do now and what it costs in time or money, so you can see whether your idea would be better.
  • Choose one need that at least four of ten parents share and that you can serve safely with your skills.
  • A common mistake is to pick a need because you like the idea, so let the count decide instead.
Worked example. Seven of ten parents, which is 70%, say last-minute school pick-ups are hard. The other three split between sleep and meals, so you choose pick-up help as the need and write it as: working parents need a safe pick-up helper because they cannot leave work early.

Activity. Interview ten parents, count the problems, and write a one-sentence need and user description.

Standard to meet. Ten interviews are recorded, problems are counted, and the chosen need is shared by at least four parents.

Module 2 Safety and law first 16 hApply

Children must be safe before any product or service is designed. Rules on ages, ratios, clearance and product testing differ by place. In this module you write a safety plan and have a qualified adviser review it.

  • Check with your local authority which rules apply to your product or service, including age limits, adult-to-child ratios and any required clearance.
  • List every hazard, such as choking, falls, burns, allergies and getting lost, and write the control for each one.
  • For a product, test small parts with the small-parts test cylinder, which is about 3 centimetres wide, and treat any part that fits inside as a choking risk for children under 3.
  • Never be alone with a child unless you have the clearance that the rules require.
  • Ask a qualified adviser to read the plan, write their comments in the plan and change it before any trial.
Worked example. If a local rule allows 1 adult for up to 8 children aged 6 to 10, then 12 children need 12 / 8 = 1.5, so 2 adults. Your toy for ages 3 and over has a bead that fits in the test cylinder, so you replace it with a larger bead before the trial.

Activity. Write a safety plan with hazards, controls, age limits and rules checked, and get a qualified adviser's written comments.

Standard to meet. Each hazard has a control, the rules checked are named with the date, and the adviser's comments are recorded in the plan.

Module 3 Consent and data 10 hApply

Families trust you with information about their children. You must ask permission first and collect only what you need. This module shows you how to write a clear consent form and keep data safe.

  • Write a consent form in plain words that says what you will do, when, who will be present and how to reach you.
  • Collect the minimum child data, such as first name, age and allergies, and never collect photos, addresses or school names unless essential.
  • Say how long you keep the data, for example delete within 30 days after the trial, and how a parent can ask for deletion.
  • Store paper forms in a locked place and digital forms in a protected file with a password.
  • Tell parents they can withdraw at any time without giving a reason, and stop at once if they do.
Worked example. Your form asks 4 things only: first name, age, allergies and an emergency phone number. For 6 families that is 6 x 4 = 24 data items, kept in one locked folder and deleted 30 days after the trial.

Activity. Draft a one-page consent form and a data note, and ask one parent to explain it back to you in their own words.

Standard to meet. The form covers purpose, data, storage time, deletion and withdrawal, and the parent explains it correctly.

Module 4 Design the offer 16 hCreate

A clear offer tells families what you do, for whom, when and what you will not do. Honest limits protect children and build trust. You produce a one-page sheet that a parent can read in two minutes.

  • State what the product or service is in one sentence, with the age range and the times or places it is available.
  • Say what is included and, just as clearly, what is not, such as medicine, swimming or transport by car.
  • Describe the safety steps in simple words, for example two adults present and an allergy check on arrival.
  • Give the price per visit or per item and how to pay, and avoid claims you cannot prove such as the safest in town.
  • Test the sheet with two parents and change every sentence they find unclear.
Worked example. Your pick-up helper walks children aged 6 to 10 from the school gate to home, 3 days a week. Over a 4-week trial that is 3 x 4 = 12 visits per child, and the sheet says clearly that you do not give medicine or take children by car.

Activity. Write a one-page offer sheet and test it with two parents, recording what they found unclear and what you changed.

Standard to meet. The sheet states age range, times, inclusions, exclusions, safety steps and price, and contains no claim you cannot prove.

Module 5 Price and costs 12 hEvaluate

Childcare costs include more than your time. Insurance, training, travel and materials all belong in the price. You will build a cost sheet and find the number of visits you need to break even.

  • List the cost of each visit: your time at a fair hourly rate, travel, materials and a share of insurance and training.
  • Add monthly fixed costs, such as insurance, first-aid training and background checks, with the currency on each line.
  • Margin per visit equals price minus cost per visit, and break-even visits equal fixed costs divided by margin.
  • Compare your price with similar local services and explain any difference.
  • Ask your mentor to check the sheet before you give any price to a family.
Worked example. Cost per visit is EUR 6.00 for time, 1.50 for travel, 1.50 for an insurance share and 2.00 for materials, which is EUR 11.00. At a price of EUR 15 the margin is 15 - 11 = EUR 4, and with EUR 120 fixed costs a month you need 120 / 4 = 30 visits to break even.

Activity. Build a cost sheet with cost per visit, price, margin and break-even visits, and have it checked by your mentor.

Standard to meet. Every line has a currency, the break-even is correct, and the mentor has signed the sheet.

Module 6 Run 30 days 34 hApply

A trial must be small, supervised and fully recorded. You keep a daily log and report any incident the same day. A weekly review with your adviser keeps safety at the centre.

  • Start with a small number of families, such as 3 to 6, so that you can give each child proper attention.
  • Write a daily log with the date, the children present, what happened and any problem or near miss.
  • Report any accident, injury or serious concern to the parent and your adviser on the same day.
  • Hold a 15-minute weekly review with your adviser and write down one change after each.
  • Check the safety plan each week and change it if something new appears.
Worked example. In week 2 the log shows two late pick-ups at the school gate. You add a reminder text sent 15 minutes before pick-up, and the following week there are 0 late pick-ups.

Activity. Run the 30-day trial with a daily log, same-day incident reports and four weekly reviews with your adviser.

Standard to meet. The log has no missing days, each incident has a same-day report, and each weekly review records one change.

Module 7 Read feedback 12 hAnalyse

Parents will tell you the truth if you ask short, clear questions. Their answers show what to keep and what to change. You summarise the results with simple percentages and choose two changes.

  • Send a short survey of five or six questions, covering trust, price, convenience and one open question.
  • Use a scale of 1 to 5 for the rating questions and add one free-text question such as what would you change.
  • Calculate the share of parents scoring 4 or 5 on each question.
  • Compare each result to the goal you set before the trial, for example 80 percent trust.
  • Pick two changes only, and write how you will check each one worked.
Worked example. Eight of 10 parents say they trust the service, which is 8 / 10 = 80%, and 3 of 10 say price is high, which is 30%. You choose two changes: a lower price for three visits a week and a weekly photo-free message update.

Activity. Run a short survey with all families, summarise it in a table with percentages, and choose two changes.

Standard to meet. The table shows percentages for trust, price and convenience, compared to your goals, and the two changes each have a check.

Module 8 Plan next steps 16 hEvaluate

After the trial you decide whether to keep, change or stop. The decision rests on safety record, trust and cost. You write a short plan for the next 90 days with numbers.

  • Review the safety log first, and stop or pause if there was any serious incident that you cannot control.
  • Compare actual costs and visits with your plan and calculate the margin you really made.
  • List the top risks for growth, such as not enough trained helpers, and one step to reduce each.
  • Set a limit on growth, for example no more than 6 families until a second trained helper is available.
  • Plan dates for the next review with your adviser and the next parent survey.
Worked example. Growing to 6 families with 3 visits a week for 4 weeks is 6 x 3 x 4 = 72 visits. At a margin of EUR 4 per visit that earns 72 x 4 = EUR 288, minus EUR 120 fixed costs, leaving EUR 168.

Activity. Present a 90-day plan with a safety review, a cost check, growth limit and dates for the next review.

Standard to meet. The plan includes the safety record, a margin calculation, a growth limit and dated review points.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: your records, numbers and reflections. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet" and at least 70% on the check.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why ask parents what was hardest instead of what they want?

Model answer: Past problems are real facts, while wishes are guesses. Real problems show which service parents would actually use and pay for.

Q2. What must be done before you design or trial anything with children?

Model answer: Check the safety rules with your local authority, write a hazard plan and have a qualified adviser review it.

Q3. Why collect the minimum child data, and what must a consent form say?

Model answer: Less data means less harm if it leaks. The form must state what you will do, what data you hold, how long, how to delete it and that parents may withdraw.

Q4. Why state what you do not do?

Model answer: Clear limits protect children, avoid misunderstandings and keep you inside the rules and your insurance.

Q5. Why include insurance and training in the cost?

Model answer: They are real costs of running the service safely, and leaving them out makes the price too low to survive a loss.

Q6. What do you do when an incident happens?

Model answer: Tell the parent and your adviser the same day, record it in the log and review the safety plan.

Q7. What three things should the survey ask about?

Model answer: Trust, price and convenience, plus one open question for ideas and concerns.

Q8. What should decide whether you grow?

Model answer: The safety record, parents' trust and whether costs are covered with a margin.

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Design and trial one childcare product or service for 30 days with real families and record feedback and safety checks.

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Safety plan reviewed by a qualified adviser; consent forms; 30-day record; parent feedback; at least 70% on the check.

where next

Ladder L1 to L7, with notes

  1. L1 Basic: First contact: words, tools and safety. Short units, about 2 to 6 hours, no record needed.
  2. L2 Awareness: Explain what the field is and who it serves. Shared with schools and community groups.
  3. L3 Foundation: Do guided tasks with a checklist. Evidence is a small set of records.
  4. L4 Practitioner: Run the full task alone with a mentor. This course sits here.
  5. L5 Specialist: Take one narrow area to depth and teach a peer circle.
  6. L6 Advanced: Lead a team, design a service or a study, and review other people's evidence.
  7. L7 Super expert: Set the standard: write the method, audit others, publish worked examples back to learners.

Notes: credits stack and you can step in and out. This course is L4. After it, L5 takes one narrow area of Childcare Products and Services to depth; L6 leads a team; L7 sets and audits the standard. Aligned with, not accredited by, national credit frameworks.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
