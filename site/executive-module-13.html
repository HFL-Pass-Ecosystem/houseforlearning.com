Module 13: Quantitative decision science and the data pipeline

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 13

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 15 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Written memo 30%
  • Live interactive simulation 35%
  • Boardroom presentation 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

Weights shown follow the shared pattern for modules 3 to 14, and will be confirmed.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Frame a decision, check a small data pipeline and use basic statistics and scenarios to recommend a choice.

Condition

Given a small data set, pipeline counts, option payoffs and a revenue and cost case. All money figures are in one currency of your choice. Figures in worked examples are invented for teaching (worked-example figures).

Standard

Row counts reconcile, rates and statistics are correct, a line is fitted, scenarios and break-even are shown and the recommendation names a range.

Who it is for: Managers and analysts who must back decisions with data.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 Framing a decision with numbers 9 hApply: structure

A good decision starts with a clear question, the options, and a measure of success. Without these, data cannot help.

  • State the choice and the deadline
  • List options and the measure for each
  • Name what you do not know

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. A firm can launch a product (option A) or wait (option B). A: a 60% chance of a 50,000 gain and a 40% chance of a 10,000 loss. B: a sure 20,000. Expected value of A = 0.6 x 50,000 + 0.4 x (-10,000) = 30,000 - 4,000 = 26,000. B is 20,000. A is higher by 6,000, but A can lose 10,000, so the board checks that it can bear that loss.

Activity: Frame a launch decision with two options. Give probabilities and money amounts and work out each expected value.

Standard: Expected values are correct for both options and the worst case is stated.

Part 2 Stages of a data pipeline 10 hUnderstand: describe

A pipeline moves data from source to use in stages: collect, clean, store, check and report. Each stage should leave a count.

  • Collect, clean, store, check, report
  • Count rows in and out of each stage
  • Keep a log of every rule applied

Worked example: A pipeline starts with 10,000 rows. The clean stage removes 150 duplicates, leaving 9,850. It then removes 200 rows with a missing key, leaving 9,650. The store stage loads 9,650. Check: 10,000 - 150 - 200 = 9,650, so the counts reconcile. If the store had loaded 9,600, 50 rows would be unexplained and the run would stop.

Activity: Take a source of 8,000 rows and three clean rules that remove 90, 60 and 25. Compute the rows loaded and write the check.

Standard: The loaded count is 7,825 and the check line matches.

Part 3 Data quality checks 9 hApply: test

Quality checks catch bad data before it reaches a decision. The common checks are completeness, uniqueness, range and timing.

  • Null rate against a limit
  • Duplicate rate
  • Range and date checks

Worked example: A table has 400 rows. 12 rows have no region, so the null rate is 12 / 400 = 3%. The limit is 2%, so it fails. 6 rows are exact duplicates, a rate of 1.5%, and the limit is 1%, so it also fails. All 400 sale amounts fall between 0 and 5,000 except 2 rows that show 50,000, which is a likely extra zero. The run is stopped and the source owner is told.

Activity: A table has 250 rows, 7 with nulls and 4 duplicates. Compute both rates and compare with 2% and 1% limits.

Standard: Rates are 2.8% and 1.6% and both fail the limits.

Part 4 Describing data 10 hApply: calculate

Before any model, describe the data with a centre and a spread. The mean, median and standard deviation tell different parts of the story.

  • Mean and median
  • Spread: standard deviation
  • Spot outliers

Worked example: Seven daily orders are 4, 6, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14. The total is 60 so the mean is 60 / 7 = 8.57. The median is the middle value, 8. Squared gaps from the mean add to 77.71. Dividing by 6 (the 7 values minus 1) gives a variance of 12.95, so the standard deviation is 3.6. The mean is close to the median, so no strong skew is shown.

Activity: Use six values 3, 5, 5, 7, 9 and 11. Find the mean, median and standard deviation.

Standard: Mean is 6.67, median is 6 and the standard deviation is about 2.94, with the working shown.

Part 5 Probability and expected value 9 hApply: calculate

Probability measures how likely something is. Conditional probability shows how new evidence changes the odds.

  • Probabilities add to 1
  • Update with evidence
  • Expected value is probability times amount

Worked example: 2% of parts are faulty. A test flags 90% of faulty parts and wrongly flags 5% of good parts. Per 1,000 parts: 20 are faulty, 18 of them flagged. Of the 980 good parts, 49 are flagged. Total flags are 67, and 18 / 67 = 26.9% are truly faulty. So even after a flag, the part is faulty only about 1 time in 4, and a second test is needed.

Activity: A fault rate is 5%, the test flags 80% of faults and 10% of good parts. Find the chance a flagged part is faulty.

Standard: Per 1,000: 50 faulty, 40 flagged, 95 good flagged, 40 / 135 = 29.6%.

Part 6 Sampling and intervals 10 hApply: estimate

A sample lets you judge a large group without counting all of it. An interval shows how sure the estimate is.

  • Standard error is spread divided by the square root of n
  • A 95% interval is the mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors
  • Larger samples give narrower intervals

Worked example: A sample of 100 customers spends a mean of 52 with a standard deviation of 10. The standard error is 10 / 10 = 1. The 95% interval is 52 plus or minus 1.96, which is 50.04 to 53.96. If the sample was 400, the error would be 10 / 20 = 0.5, and the interval would be 51.02 to 52.98, half as wide.

Activity: A sample of 64 has a mean of 80 and a standard deviation of 16. Give the 95% interval.

Standard: Standard error is 2, the interval is 76.08 to 83.92.

Part 7 Simple regression and forecasts 9 hApply: model

A line through points shows how one measure moves with another. The slope is the change in y for each step in x.

  • Slope equals the co-movement divided by the spread of x
  • The intercept sets the starting level
  • Do not forecast far outside the data

Worked example: Ad spend x is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 (in thousands) and sales y are 3, 5, 6, 9, 12. Mean x is 3 and mean y is 7. The co-movement sum is (-2)(-4) + (-1)(-2) + 0 + (1)(2) + (2)(5) = 8 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 10 = 22. The spread of x is 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10. The slope is 2.2, the intercept is 7 - 2.2 x 3 = 0.4. Forecast for x = 6 is 0.4 + 13.2 = 13.6.

Activity: Fit a line to x = 1, 2, 3 and y = 2, 4, 5 and forecast for x = 4.

Standard: Slope is 1.5, intercept is 0.67 and the forecast is 6.67.

Part 8 Sensitivity, scenarios and clear reporting 9 hEvaluate: judge

A forecast is only a guess unless you test what happens when inputs change. Scenarios show which input matters most.

  • Change one input at a time
  • Find the break-even point
  • Report ranges, not one figure

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. A project has revenue of 200,000 and costs of 150,000, so profit is 50,000. If revenue falls 10% to 180,000, profit is 30,000, down 40%. If costs rise 10% to 165,000, profit is 35,000, down 30%. Break-even revenue is 150,000, so revenue can fall by 50,000 / 200,000 = 25% before a loss. Revenue is the weaker point, so the report leads with it.

Activity: Revenue 120,000, costs 90,000. Test a 10% revenue fall and a 10% cost rise and give the break-even fall.

Standard: Profits are 18,000 and 21,000 and the break-even fall is 25%.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. Option A gives 70% chance of 10,000 and 30% chance of -5,000. What is its expected value?
    7,000 - 1,500 = 5,500. (Covered in part 1: Framing a decision with numbers. To pass that part: Expected values are correct for both options and the worst case is stated.)
  2. A pipeline starts with 5,000 rows, removes 120 and then 80. How many rows load?
    4,800. (Covered in part 2: Stages of a data pipeline. To pass that part: The loaded count is 7,825 and the check line matches.)
  3. 12 of 300 rows are null. What is the null rate?
    4%. (Covered in part 3: Data quality checks. To pass that part: Rates are 2.8% and 1.6% and both fail the limits.)
  4. The values 2, 4, 6 have what mean and what median?
    Mean 4 and median 4. (Covered in part 4: Describing data. To pass that part: Mean is 6.67, median is 6 and the standard deviation is about 2.94, with the working shown.)
  5. 1% of items are faulty. A test flags 95% of faults and 4% of good items. Of 1,000, how many flags are good items?
    990 x 0.04 = 39.6, about 40. (Covered in part 5: Probability and expected value. To pass that part: Per 1,000: 50 faulty, 40 flagged, 95 good flagged, 40 / 135 = 29.6%.)
  6. Sample size 25, standard deviation 10. What is the standard error?
    10 / 5 = 2. (Covered in part 6: Sampling and intervals. To pass that part: Standard error is 2, the interval is 76.08 to 83.92.)
  7. Slope is 1.5 and intercept 2. What is the forecast for x = 4?
    8. (Covered in part 7: Simple regression and forecasts. To pass that part: Slope is 1.5, intercept is 0.67 and the forecast is 6.67.)
  8. Revenue is 100,000, costs 80,000. By what percent can revenue fall before a loss?
    20%. (Covered in part 8: Sensitivity, scenarios and clear reporting. To pass that part: Profits are 18,000 and 21,000 and the break-even fall is 25%.)
  9. Why must the count of rows reconcile between stages?
    A missing row with no rule to explain it shows data was lost or added by mistake. (Covered in part 2: Stages of a data pipeline. To pass that part: The loaded count is 7,825 and the check line matches.)
  10. Why is a larger sample better for an interval?
    The standard error shrinks with the square root of n, so the interval gets narrower. (Covered in part 6: Sampling and intervals. To pass that part: Standard error is 2, the interval is 76.08 to 83.92.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Frame the choice. A slide shows a decision with two options and a number for each. Speaker note: Say: no question, no analysis. Check: ask for the measure of success. Mistake: starting with the data instead of the decision.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Basic: Pipeline stages. Five boxes from collect to report with row counts between them. Speaker note: Say: count at every stage. Check: reconcile 10,000 to 9,650. Mistake: trusting a pipeline that gives no counts.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Core: Quality checks. A table of null rate, duplicate rate and limits with a fail flag. Speaker note: Say: stop on a failed check. Check: calculate a rate. Mistake: fixing bad data silently.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Core: Centre and spread. A set of seven numbers with mean, median and standard deviation worked. Speaker note: Say: describe before modelling. Check: find the median. Mistake: using the mean when outliers are present.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Applied: Odds and evidence. A table of 1,000 parts splits into faulty, good, flagged and not flagged. Speaker note: Say: use counts to see the odds. Check: ask the chance after a flag. Mistake: assuming a 90% test means 90% right.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Applied: Intervals and lines. An interval on a mean and a fitted line with a forecast. Speaker note: Say: show the range. Check: widen the interval for a smaller sample. Mistake: forecasting far past the data.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Advanced: Sensitivity and the report. A scenario table with profit under each change and a break-even line. Speaker note: Say: lead with the weakest input. Check: ask for break-even. Mistake: giving one figure with no range.

jobs this leads to

Where this takes you

  • Data analyst. Cleans data and reports with checks.
  • Decision analyst. Builds expected value and scenario models.
  • Data engineer. Builds and monitors the data pipeline.
  • Operations researcher. Fits models and tests how sensitive they are.
