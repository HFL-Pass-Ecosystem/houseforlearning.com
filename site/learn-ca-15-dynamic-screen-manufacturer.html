Dynamic screen manufacturer

Run a pilot of dynamic screen manufacturer in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small dynamic screen manufacturer offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Understand display technologies and uses 6 hUnderstand

Dynamic screens change what they show: signs, windows, vehicles, wearable and building surfaces.

  • Know the types: LCD, LED, e-paper, transparent and flexible panels.
  • Match to use: bright for outdoors, low power for shelf labels, transparent for windows.
  • Ask buyers about brightness, size, viewing distance, power and life.
  • Pick a first product and customer, such as shelf labels for grocers.
  • Compare panel types on four numbers: brightness in nits, power in watts, thickness in millimetres and cost per square metre.
  • Check each panel's stated operating temperature range; an outdoor sign in a Canadian winter needs a panel and heater rated for the cold, not just for the sun.
  • Interview buyers in three settings, such as grocery, transit and shop windows, and write down each one's viewing distance and hours of use per day.
Worked example. A grocery chain changes 4,000 shelf prices a week by hand at 15 seconds each, which is 60,000 seconds or 16.7 hours. If staff time costs 25 per hour, that is about 417 a week, and e-paper labels update the same prices in minutes. Compare that saving with the label cost before you pitch.

Activity. Write a requirement sheet from three buyer interviews.

Standard to meet. Add units to the requirements.

Module 2 Know product and trade rules 6 hApply

Electronics have safety and import rules.

  • Electrical and radio approvals are required.
  • Hazardous substance limits and recycling duties may apply; check the federal and provincial rule.
  • Import duties and origin rules affect costs.
  • Warranty and consumer law apply.
  • If a sign has wireless links, check the federal radio rule and keep the test report with the product file.
  • Mark every unit with model, rating and serial number so that a buyer, installer or regulator can trace it.
  • Write a one-page import note: tariff class, proof of origin and who pays the duty; confirm the current rule with a licensed customs broker.
Worked example. Row: 'Sell a powered sign | electrical safety approval | lab certificate | before sale | certificate'. A second row: 'Ship panels from abroad | border agency | customs entry and origin proof | each shipment | entry copy'. Re-read each source every six months and update the date.

Activity. Build a rules table.

Standard to meet. Cite a source and a date for every row.

Module 3 Design and assemble a display module 10 hApply

Good design hides complexity.

  • Block diagram: panel, driver, power, controller, link.
  • Heat and brightness testing.
  • Content management: how a user changes the image.
  • Document assembly steps.
  • Write a power budget: a panel drawing 120 W for 16 hours a day uses 1.92 kWh a day (120 x 16 = 1,920 Wh).
  • Choose cables and connectors with strain relief, because field faults often start at a cable rather than at the panel.
  • Write a test script that shows all-white, all-black and colour bars, and record what you see at each step.
Worked example. Brightness spec 700 nits; measured 640 at 40 C. The shortfall is 60 nits, which is 8.6% of the spec. Add a better heat path, retest at the same temperature and record the new reading.

Activity. Assemble a prototype and measure brightness at two temperatures.

Standard to meet. Record measurements.

Module 4 Price and prove the business 8 hEvaluate

Display makers live on yield.

  • Bill of materials: panel is the largest part.
  • Assembly, test, scrap.
  • Warranty provision.
  • Margin and break-even.
  • Split the bill of materials into panel, driver board, enclosure, power supply, labour, test and packaging, and show each as a share of the total.
  • Allow for scrap: if 780 is the build cost per unit attempted and 8% fail, each good unit costs 780 / 0.92 = about 848.
  • Set a warranty provision, such as 2% of price (25 on a 1,250 unit), and review it against real returns.
Worked example. Price 1,250; cost 780; margin 470 (38%); fixed cost 564,000 a year, so break-even is 564,000 / 470 = 1,200 units. With the 8% scrap case the cost is 848, margin 402, and break-even rises to 564,000 / 402 = about 1,403 units. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build cost sheet with 8% scrap case.

Standard to meet. Show the cases.

Module 5 Control quality and yield 8 hApply

Dead pixels and colour drift end sales.

  • Incoming panel test.
  • Burn-in test.
  • Grade units by defects.
  • Track yield.
  • Set an incoming sample, such as 20 panels from each lot of 200, and agree in writing how many defects reject the lot.
  • Photograph each defect type and keep a reference board so that every tester grades the same way.
  • Run burn-in at raised temperature for a set number of hours and record failures per hundred units.
Worked example. Yield 91%: of 200 units built, 182 pass and 18 fail (200 x 0.91 = 182). If 9 of the 18 failures are bonding bubbles, that single defect costs 4.5% of output, so it is the first process to fix.

Activity. Design test sheet with grades.

Standard to meet. State limits.

Module 6 Sell, install and support 6 hCreate

Installers decide what they recommend.

  • Train installers.
  • Offer pilots.
  • Remote update service.
  • Spare stock.
  • Write a one-page install guide with photos, fixing steps and a power checklist, and test it on someone who has not seen the product.
  • Offer a service plan that states the remote update schedule, the replacement time and a spare stock of about 5% of installed units.
  • Quote five-year cost of ownership: unit price, power, content service and repairs, so the buyer compares like with like.
Worked example. Funnel: 18 retailers, 5 pilots, 2 orders. That is 5 / 18 = 28% of retailers to pilot, 2 / 5 = 40% of pilots to order, and 2 / 18 = 11% overall. Use these rates to plan how many retailers to contact for ten orders.

Activity. Pitch three buyers.

Standard to meet. Pitch has numbers.

Module 7 Manage supply, energy and end-of-life risk 6 hEvaluate

Panels depend on a global chain.

  • Second sources.
  • Power use targets.
  • Recycling plan.
  • Cyber security for networked signs.
  • Hold several weeks of panel stock or name a second supplier, and record each supplier's lead time in writing.
  • Write a take-back plan: who collects old units, where they are sorted, and which provincial electronics recycling rule applies; check the provincial rule.
  • Change default passwords and set a firmware update schedule for networked signs.
Worked example. Risk: 'Panel shortage | 4 x 4 | second source | purchasing | monthly'. The score is likelihood 4 times impact 4 = 16 out of 25, which is high. Action: qualify a second panel source within 90 days and review it monthly.

Activity. Write a risk register.

Standard to meet. Name an owner for each item.

Module 8 Pilot and plan scale 8 hCreate

Prove in a store.

  • 60 days in 3 stores.
  • Measure failures and update time.
  • Stop rule: failure over 3% means fix.
  • Plan production.
  • Choose three stores with different conditions: a bright window, a shaded aisle and a cold-room.
  • Log every fault with date, unit, cause and time to fix, and time how long a price change takes to appear on a label.
  • At the end, ask store staff what annoyed them and write three product changes.
Worked example. Failure 1.5%: with 200 units installed, 3 failed (3 / 200 = 1.5%). The stop line at 3% is 6 failures (200 x 0.03), so the pilot passes and you proceed to plan production.

Activity. Run pilot review.

Standard to meet. Review has decision.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name three display types.

Model answer: LCD, LED and e-paper (also transparent, flexible). (Covered in module 1: Understand display technologies and uses. To pass that module: Add units to the requirements.)

Q2. What is a nit?

Model answer: A unit of brightness. (Covered in module 1: Understand display technologies and uses. To pass that module: Add units to the requirements.)

Q3. Why burn-in test?

Model answer: To find early failures. (Covered in module 5: Control quality and yield. To pass that module: State limits.)

Q4. What drives yield loss?

Model answer: Defects such as dead pixels or bonding bubbles. (Covered in module 5: Control quality and yield. To pass that module: State limits.)

Q5. Why a second panel source?

Model answer: Panels are the largest, scarcest part. (Covered in module 7: Manage supply, energy and end-of-life risk. To pass that module: Name an owner for each item.)

Q6. What approval do powered signs need?

Model answer: Electrical safety approval. (Covered in module 2: Know product and trade rules. To pass that module: Cite a source and a date for every row.)

Q7. What is break-even?

Model answer: Fixed cost over margin per unit. (Covered in module 4: Price and prove the business. To pass that module: Show the cases.)

Q8. Why train installers?

Model answer: They influence what is recommended. (Covered in module 6: Sell, install and support. To pass that module: Pitch has numbers.)

Q9. What triggers a fix in the pilot?

Model answer: Failure over 3%. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and plan scale. To pass that module: Review has decision.)

Q10. Why plan recycling?

Model answer: End-of-life duties apply. (Covered in module 7: Manage supply, energy and end-of-life risk. To pass that module: Name an owner for each item.)

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
