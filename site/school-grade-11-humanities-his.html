Grade 11 Humanities: History

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5Post-secondary4 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Understand, Analyse
  • Understand Describe early human societies.
  • Analyse Compare empires and their legacies.
  • Understand Explain religion, trade and learning across regions.
  • Understand Describe revolutions and industrialisation.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 11 Humanities History, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 From the beginning of time to early societies Weeks 1 to 9Understand

In this unit you work on 'From the beginning of time to early societies', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe early human societies. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'From the beginning of time to early societies' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe early human societies. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'From the beginning of time to early societies'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe the timescale first. Current evidence suggests modern humans appeared in Africa roughly 300,000 years ago, while farming began only about 10,000 years ago. So farming covers about 3 percent of our species' time, and for the rest people lived by hunting and gathering. Draw a line and mark both dates.
  2. Step 2, Describe hunter-gatherer societies. Small bands of perhaps 20 to 50 people moved with the seasons, gathered plants, hunted animals, made stone tools and used fire for warmth, cooking and protection. Our evidence comes from tools, bones and cave paintings, not from written words.
  3. Step 3, Describe the shift to farming. From about 9500 BCE in West Asia people grew wheat and barley and kept sheep and goats. In South Asia the farming village of Mehrgarh dates from about 7000 BCE. Settling brought storage, surplus food, larger villages and new ideas of property.
  4. Step 4, Describe the first cities. Uruk in Mesopotamia had grown into a city by about 3500 BCE, with temples, officials and traders, and cuneiform writing appeared by about 3200 BCE. Surplus food freed some people to become potters, priests and scribes. Name three signs of a city: size, specialist work and record keeping.
  5. Step 5, Explain how we know. Archaeologists dig in layers, with older objects lower down, and date organic material by radiocarbon, which reaches back around 50,000 years. A stone scraper suggests hunting, while a grinding stone suggests farming. Always state what the evidence cannot show, such as beliefs.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: describe two differences between a hunter-gatherer band and a farming village. Answer: a band moved with food and owned few things, while a village stayed in one place, stored grain and grew larger, so farming villages could develop surplus food and specialist work.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe early human societies: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Empires and cultures Weeks 10 to 18Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Empires and cultures', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: compare empires and their legacies. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Empires and cultures' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: compare empires and their legacies. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Empires and cultures'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Choose criteria before you compare: size, government, economy and legacy. Take three empires, Rome, Han China and the Mauryan empire of India. A comparison works only when each empire is judged on the same points, so write them as column headings in a table.
  2. Step 2, Compare lifespans using dates. Han China lasted from 202 BCE to 220 CE, which is 202 + 220 - 1 = 421 years, since there is no year zero, counting a short break in the middle. The Mauryan empire, about 321 to 185 BCE, lasted about 136 years. The Roman Empire in the West, 27 BCE to 476 CE, lasted 502 years.
  3. Step 3, Compare government. Rome ran provinces under governors, spread citizenship and used written law. Han emperors relied on salaried officials chosen for talent and loyalty. The Mauryan capital at Pataliputra controlled provinces through royal officials. Write one sentence on each, noting how it kept control.
  4. Step 4, Compare economy and links. Roman paved roads and sea lanes joined the Mediterranean world. Han China sent silk westward along the routes later called the Silk Road. Mauryan roads linked the northern plains to the coasts, and punch-marked silver coins helped trade. Note which empire relied most on roads.
  5. Step 5, Compare legacies. Roman law and Latin shaped later European law and languages. The Han left the model of a unified state run by trained officials. Mauryan inscribed pillar edicts, in local scripts, are among the earliest public writing in South Asia. Rank which legacy lasted longest and explain why.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: compare how Han China and Rome held their territory. Answer: Han China used salaried officials answerable to the emperor, while Rome used provincial governors, legions, roads and the spread of citizenship; both depended on taxes and a strong army to keep control.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Compare empires and their legacies: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Changing traditions Weeks 19 to 27Understand

In this unit you work on 'Changing traditions', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain religion, trade and learning across regions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Changing traditions' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain religion, trade and learning across regions. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Changing traditions'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain the routes first. Goods and ideas moved on overland Silk Roads and by sea across the Indian Ocean. Sailors used the monsoon: the southwest wind in summer carried ships from Arabia to India, and the northeast wind in winter brought them back, so a round trip fitted in one year.
  2. Step 2, Explain how religion travelled with trade. Merchants carried silk, spices and cotton, and also their beliefs. Buddhism spread from India along oasis towns of Central Asia and reached China by about the first century CE. Monasteries beside the routes gave travellers rest, so trade and religion supported each other.
  3. Step 3, Explain the spread of learning. Indian numerals and the zero were taken up by scholars writing in Arabic in the eighth and ninth centuries, and later reached Europe. The word algebra comes from the Arabic al-jabr. A translation movement in Baghdad turned Greek, Persian and Sanskrit works into Arabic.
  4. Step 4, Explain the spread of technology. Paper came from China and was being made at Samarkand by the mid-eighth century, then spread west. The earliest dated printed book, a Buddhist scroll from China, is from 868 CE. Cheaper paper and printing made books easier to copy and share.
  5. Step 5, Explain causes and effects in a chain. Cause: rulers wanted trade taxes and protected roads. Effect: merchants travelled widely. Result: religions, numbers, paper and crops moved across regions and mixed with local cultures. Write each link with because or so, and avoid claiming one cause explains everything.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: explain why religions often spread along trade routes. Answer: merchants and travellers moved along the routes, stopped in towns and monasteries, and shared their beliefs with the people they met, while rulers and traders who valued safe trade supported those centres.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain religion, trade and learning across regions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 The modern world Weeks 28 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'The modern world', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe revolutions and industrialisation. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'The modern world' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe revolutions and industrialisation. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'The modern world'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe the age of revolutions with a timeline. The American Declaration was in 1776, the French Revolution began in 1789 and the Russian Revolution came in 1917. The gaps are 1789 - 1776 = 13 years and 1917 - 1789 = 128 years. Mark each on a line and say what changed in each.
  2. Step 2, Describe the French Revolution. Causes were royal debt, high bread prices and an unequal system of three estates. On 14 July 1789 a crowd stormed the Bastille prison in Paris. The 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen stated that men are born free and equal in rights.
  3. Step 3, Describe industrialisation. It began in Britain from about the 1760s, where textile machines, coal, iron and the steam engine moved work from homes into factories. Early public railways opened in 1825 and 1830. Say why Britain led: coal and iron nearby, capital, and overseas markets.
  4. Step 4, Describe the social effects. Factory towns grew fast with crowded housing, long hours and child labour. The Factory Act of 1833 banned children under nine from working in textile mills. Describe one gain, cheaper cloth, and one cost, poor living conditions, to give a balanced answer.
  5. Step 5, Link industrialisation to the wider world. Mills needed raw cotton and new markets. Britain imported raw cotton from its colonies and the United States, and sold machine-made cloth abroad. In India this harmed hand-loom weavers, which shows how industry in one region changed life elsewhere.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: describe two effects of steam power on transport. Answer: steam locomotives made rail travel faster and cheaper, with passenger lines from 1825 and 1830, and steamships allowed regular sea crossings that did not depend on wind, so goods and people moved farther in less time.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe revolutions and industrialisation: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: My own timeline

On the slide: L1 Basic · My own timeline

Notes: Put three events from your own life in order. Notes: ask learners to draw a baby picture, a first day at school and today, then order them on a string line and use the words before, after and now in a spoken sentence.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Then and now

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Then and now

Notes: Tell how a long-ago object differs from a modern one and use a simple timeline. Notes: compare an oil lamp with an electric bulb; place both on a line marked in 100-year steps and ask learners to say one way daily life changed and one way it stayed the same.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Early civilisations and evidence

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Early civilisations and evidence

Notes: Describe an early river-valley civilisation and say how we know about it. Notes: sort sources into written and object, primary and secondary. Practice: a clay tablet about 4,500 years old, counted back from 2026, dates to roughly 2500 BCE; learners say what it can and cannot tell us.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Cause, effect and change

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Cause, effect and change

Notes: Explain the causes and effects of one major change, such as the spread of printing. Notes: sort causes into short term and long term. Movable-type printing reached Europe around 1450, which is 576 years before 2026; learners list two effects on reading and learning.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Sources and balanced argument (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Sources and balanced argument

Notes: Evaluate two sources on one event for reliability and write a balanced paragraph. Notes: take the Second World War, 1939 to 1945, and compare a diary with an official report; check who wrote it, when and why, then give one point each way before judging.

This course: Grade 11 Humanities History is at this level. Its units include From the beginning of time to early societies; Empires and cultures; Changing traditions; The modern world.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Historiography and archives

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Historiography and archives

Notes: Analyse competing interpretations and build an argument from primary evidence. Notes: compare two accounts of the causes of one reform or war and note what each leaves out. Practice: in a ledger of 1,200 entries, 15 per cent are damaged, so 180 are unusable; learners state what that does to the claim.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Original research design

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Original research design

Notes: Design an original research question, justify method and place the findings in the wider debate. Notes: write a 300-word proposal naming sources, gaps and bias; if a sample is 80 letters from a collection of 640, that is 12.5 per cent, and learners argue whether it is representative.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
