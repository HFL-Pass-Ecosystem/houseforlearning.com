Module 2: Cultural intelligence and business-to-business negotiation

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 2

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 15 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Written memo 30%
  • Live interactive simulation 35%
  • Boardroom presentation 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

Weights shown follow the shared pattern for modules 3 to 14, and will be confirmed.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Prepare for and run a business-to-business negotiation with a partner from a different culture and reach a deal that holds.

Condition

Given a supplier or buyer case with price, delivery and payment figures and a short profile of the other side's working style.

Standard

The preparation sheet has correct limits and a deal zone, and the final deal lies inside the zone with reasons recorded.

Who it is for: Managers who buy, sell or partner across borders and need deals that survive after the handshake.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 The four parts of cultural intelligence 9 hUnderstand: describe and rate

Cultural intelligence is the skill of working well with people whose habits differ from yours. It has four parts: drive, knowledge, strategy and action.

  • Drive: wish to learn
  • Knowledge: facts about the other culture
  • Strategy: planning and checking
  • Action: changing what you do

Worked example: A manager rates herself 1 to 7 on four parts: drive 6, knowledge 3, strategy 4, action 5. Average = (6+3+4+5)/4 = 4.5. The lowest is knowledge at 3, so she spends her first 4 prep hours reading about the partner's business customs rather than rehearsing her pitch.

Activity: Rate yourself on each of the four parts with one real example each, and choose the lowest part to improve.

Standard: Four ratings with four examples, a correct average, and one named improvement action.

Part 2 Reading direct and indirect messages 9 hAnalyse: interpret

Some people say what they mean in words; others let the situation carry the meaning. Misreading this costs deals.

  • Direct style: the words carry the meaning
  • Indirect style: tone, pauses and setting carry it
  • Check meaning by asking neutral questions

Worked example: A supplier writes: 'This will be difficult to arrange in that time.' A direct reader hears 'maybe'. An indirect reader hears 'no'. Test: the supplier offers no new date. Count of signals for 'no': no date offered, hedged wording, copy to a senior person = 3 of 3. Reading: treat as no and ask 'What time would be easier?'.

Activity: Read five short messages and mark each as likely yes, maybe or no with the signals that support the mark.

Standard: At least four of five readings match the model answers and each has two signals named.

Part 3 Time, trust and relationship building 9 hApply: plan

In some places trust comes first and a deal follows. In others the deal is the way to build trust. Plan time for both.

  • Estimate meetings needed to reach trust
  • Count the cost of relationship time
  • Fix the deal clock to match

Worked example: Home market: 3 meetings of 2 hours to a deal = 6 hours. Partner market: 9 meetings of 3 hours on average, 4 of them meals = 27 hours. Extra time = 27 - 6 = 21 hours. At a manager cost of 90 an hour (worked-example figures, in your currency) that is 1,890. If the deal is worth 400,000 the cost is under 0.5%, so the relationship time is easily justified.

Activity: Estimate the hours to close a deal in two different markets and compare the cost with deal value.

Standard: Both time estimates are shown with working and the percentage cost is correct.

Part 4 Preparing limits and the deal zone 10 hApply: calculate

Before talking, know your walk-away point, your best alternative and the other side's likely limit. The overlap is the deal zone.

  • Reservation point: the worst deal you accept
  • Best alternative: what you do if no deal
  • Deal zone: where both sides could say yes

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency: a buyer will pay at most 120 a unit; the best alternative costs 115 after extra shipping, so the real limit is 115. The seller needs at least 95. The zone is 95 to 115, width 20. If the buyer's guess of the seller's limit is wrong and the seller needs 118, there is no zone, and the buyer must change the alternative or the specification.

Activity: Fill in a preparation sheet with both sides' limits, alternatives and the deal zone for a case you are given.

Standard: Both limits, both alternatives and the zone width are correct and the sheet names one assumption to test.

Part 5 Opening offers and the pattern of concessions 9 hApply: sequence

Where you start and how you move tells the other side what to expect. Cultures read concessions differently.

  • First offers anchor the talk
  • Concessions should shrink as you near your limit
  • Never concede without asking for something

Worked example: A seller wants a target price of 110 and a floor of 100. Opens at 130. Planned steps: 130, 120, 114, 110, 108. Gaps: 10, 6, 4, 2. Shrinking gaps signal the floor is near. Total moved = 130 - 108 = 22. In a market where haggling is a sign of respect, a first move of only 3 would seem rude.

Activity: Plan a five-step concession ladder for a case, with a trade requested at each step.

Standard: Steps shrink, none passes the limit and each step lists what is asked in return.

Part 6 Multi-issue trades 9 hAnalyse: value and trade

Most deals have more than price. Trading issues each side values differently can make both better off.

  • List issues and give each a value to each side
  • Trade what is cheap for you and dear to them
  • Check the total value, not each issue

Worked example: Value of fast delivery: 6 points to the buyer, 2 points of cost to the seller. Value of a 3-point price cut: 3 points to the buyer, 3 points of cost to the seller. Trade: the seller gives fast delivery and the buyer gives up the price cut. Buyer gets 6 and gives up 3, so gains 3. Seller spends 2 and saves 3, so gains 1. Both are ahead, and the pair gains 4 in total.

Activity: Rank four issues for each side in a case and propose two trades that leave both sides better off.

Standard: Two trades with the gain to each side shown in numbers and both gains above zero.

Part 7 Hierarchy and decision making 10 hAnalyse: map

Who decides, and how, differs by culture. Some decide alone at the top; others agree in groups before anyone speaks.

  • Map the real decision path
  • Estimate days per level
  • Find the person who can say no

Worked example: Partner firm: 4 approval levels at 5 working days each = 20 days. Add a month-end freeze of 6 days. Expected decision = 26 working days. If your deadline is in 15 days, you are 11 days short. Option: ask for a pre-approval meeting with the second level, which could remove two levels and save 10 days.

Activity: Draw the decision path for a partner and calculate the expected time to decision against your deadline.

Standard: A path with levels, a correct total and one action that shortens it.

Part 8 Closing, writing the contract and keeping the bond 10 hCreate: write and review

A contract is read differently in different places. Close the deal with clear terms and keep the relationship working afterwards.

  • Write clear measurable terms
  • Agree how disputes are raised
  • Plan a first-90-day review

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency: a contract for 2,000,000 over two years, paid in eight quarterly payments of 250,000. Unclear delivery wording leads to a dispute costing an estimated 3% = 60,000 plus 4 weeks delay. Clear wording: 'delivered to the named port by the 15th of each month, 5% fine per week late'. A 90-day review meeting at day 90 catches issues before the later quarterly payments of 250,000.

Activity: Write five measurable terms and a dispute step for a deal, then plan the 90-day review.

Standard: Five terms with number, date or test, one dispute step, and a dated review.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. A manager rates drive 6, knowledge 3, strategy 4, action 5. What is her average and which part gets work first?
    Average 4.5; knowledge at 3 is lowest. (Covered in part 1: The four parts of cultural intelligence. To pass that part: Four ratings with four examples, a correct average, and one named improvement action.)
  2. A supplier says 'This will be difficult' and offers no date. How might an indirect reader take it?
    As a polite no, and the next step is to ask what time would be easier. (Covered in part 2: Reading direct and indirect messages. To pass that part: At least four of five readings match the model answers and each has two signals named.)
  3. A deal takes 6 hours at home and 27 hours in the partner market. What is the extra cost at 90 an hour?
    21 hours x 90 = 1,890. (Covered in part 3: Time, trust and relationship building. To pass that part: Both time estimates are shown with working and the percentage cost is correct.)
  4. A buyer's stated limit is 120 but the alternative costs 115. What is the real limit?
    115, because paying more than the alternative makes no sense. (Covered in part 4: Preparing limits and the deal zone. To pass that part: Both limits, both alternatives and the zone width are correct and the sheet names one assumption to test.)
  5. The buyer's real limit is 115 and the seller's floor is 95. How wide is the zone?
    20. (Covered in part 4: Preparing limits and the deal zone. To pass that part: Both limits, both alternatives and the zone width are correct and the sheet names one assumption to test.)
  6. A seller steps 130, 120, 114, 110, 108. What are the gaps and what do they tell the buyer?
    10, 6, 4, 2; shrinking gaps signal the floor is near. (Covered in part 5: Opening offers and the pattern of concessions. To pass that part: Steps shrink, none passes the limit and each step lists what is asked in return.)
  7. Why should every concession in a ladder be paired with an ask?
    Giving without asking teaches the other side that pushing works and leaves value unused. (Covered in part 5: Opening offers and the pattern of concessions. To pass that part: Steps shrink, none passes the limit and each step lists what is asked in return.)
  8. Delivery is worth 6 to the buyer and costs the seller 2. What is the net value of trading it?
    Buyer gains 6, seller loses 2, so the pair gains 4 in total. (Covered in part 6: Multi-issue trades. To pass that part: Two trades with the gain to each side shown in numbers and both gains above zero.)
  9. Four levels at 5 days each plus 6 freeze days against a 15-day deadline. By how many days do you miss it?
    26 - 15 = 11 days. (Covered in part 7: Hierarchy and decision making. To pass that part: A path with levels, a correct total and one action that shortens it.)
  10. A 3% dispute cost on a 2,000,000 contract is how much?
    60,000. (Covered in part 8: Closing, writing the contract and keeping the bond. To pass that part: Five terms with number, date or test, one dispute step, and a dated review.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Why culture changes a deal. Two honest people can read the same sentence differently. A deal fails when each side assumes the other reads it their way. Speaker note: Say: tell a short story of a misread yes. Check: ask learners for one case. Mistake: blaming bad faith.
  2. Slide 2: L2 The four parts of cultural intelligence. Drive, knowledge, strategy and action. We rate ourselves and pick the lowest part. Speaker note: Say: all four are skills, not talent. Check: each learner names their lowest. Mistake: rating only drive.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Reading messages. Direct words or indirect signals. Count the signals before you decide what was meant. Speaker note: Say: use the supplier example. Check: mark three messages. Mistake: reading one signal alone.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Time and trust. Relationship time is a cost with a return. Estimate hours and compare with deal value. Speaker note: Say: show the 21-hour gap. Check: compute percentage. Mistake: treating meals as waste.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Limits and the deal zone. Know your limit, your alternative and theirs. The overlap is the zone. Speaker note: Say: show a zone and a no-zone case. Check: learners find the zone. Mistake: using the stated limit instead of the alternative.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Concessions and trades. Move in shrinking steps and always ask for something. Trade what is cheap to you and dear to them. Speaker note: Say: draw the ladder. Check: no step passes the limit. Mistake: matching their moves exactly.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Deciding, closing and keeping the bond. Map who really decides, write measurable terms and review at 90 days. Speaker note: Say: ask who can say no. Check: does every term have a number or date? Mistake: closing with vague words.
