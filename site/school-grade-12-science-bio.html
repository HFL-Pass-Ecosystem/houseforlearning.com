Grade 12 Science: Biology

5 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Science Biology, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 5 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

Unit 1 Reproduction in organisms and humans Weeks 1 to 8Understand

In this unit you work on 'Reproduction in organisms and humans', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe sexual reproduction in flowering plants and humans. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Reproduction in organisms and humans' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe sexual reproduction in flowering plants and humans. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Draw and label the structure, state the function of each part in one line, then link structure to function: for example, thin walls of the air sacs let oxygen pass quickly into the blood. Method: Model run for 'Reproduction in organisms and humans'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe the flower as the reproductive organ. The stamen has an anther that makes pollen grains, and the carpel (stigma, style, ovary) holds ovules. Inside the ovule, one cell divides to form an embryo sac of 7 cells and 8 nuclei, including one egg cell and 2 polar nuclei. Pollen grains hold the male gametes.
  2. Step 2, Describe pollination and double fertilization. Pollen lands on the stigma and grows a tube down the style to the ovule. Two male gametes are released. One fuses with the egg to form the zygote. The other fuses with the 2 polar nuclei to form the primary endosperm nucleus. Two fusions in one embryo sac is why it is called double fertilization.
  3. Step 3, Describe the result. The zygote becomes the embryo, the endosperm nucleus becomes the food store, the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary becomes the fruit. Check the chromosome numbers in maize, which has 20 chromosomes in body cells: egg 10, zygote 20, and endosperm 30 because it has three sets of 10.
  4. Step 4, Describe human gametes. Sperm are made in the seminiferous tubules of the testes by spermatogenesis and each carries 23 chromosomes. Eggs are made in the ovaries by oogenesis, also with 23. Fusion at fertilization restores 46 chromosomes, the normal number for a human body cell, and the sex is set by whether the sperm carries X or Y.
  5. Step 5, Describe the cycle and early pregnancy. In a typical 28-day menstrual cycle, ovulation occurs around day 14. Fertilization happens in the fallopian tube, and the embryo reaches the uterus and implants about a week later. Pregnancy lasts about 9 months, roughly 40 weeks when counted from the start of the last period. The placenta feeds the foetus.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a pea plant has 14 chromosomes in a body cell. How many are in a pollen grain, the egg, the zygote and the endosperm? Answer: pollen grain 7, egg 7, zygote 14, endosperm 21 (three sets of 7). Name the process by which one fusion forms the zygote and another forms the endosperm: double fertilization.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe sexual reproduction in flowering plants and humans: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Genetics and evolution Weeks 9 to 15Apply

In this unit you work on 'Genetics and evolution', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: apply inheritance laws and molecular basis of inheritance. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Genetics and evolution' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: apply inheritance laws and molecular basis of inheritance. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Draw and label the structure, state the function of each part in one line, then link structure to function: for example, thin walls of the air sacs let oxygen pass quickly into the blood. Method: Model run for 'Genetics and evolution'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Apply the law of segregation to a monohybrid cross. Tall (T) is dominant over short (t). Cross two heterozygous plants, Tt x Tt. Gametes: T or t from each parent. Offspring: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Phenotypes are 3 tall to 1 short, because TT and Tt both look tall. A test cross with tt shows an unknown plant's genotype.
  2. Step 2, Apply independent assortment to a dihybrid cross. Round (R) and yellow (Y) are dominant. Cross RrYy x RrYy. Each parent makes four gamete types, RY, Ry, rY, ry, so there are 16 combinations. Count phenotypes: 9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green. The ratio is 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  3. Step 3, Apply the molecular basis. DNA is a double helix with A pairing with T and G with C. Read a template strand 3' TAC CGA TTT ACT 5'. The mRNA made is 5' AUG GCU AAA UGA 3', because RNA uses U instead of T. The codons code for methionine (start), alanine, lysine and then UGA, a stop signal.
  4. Step 4, Apply it to a mutation. In sickle cell disease one base changes in the gene for the haemoglobin beta chain, so the codon GAG becomes GUG in mRNA and the amino acid glutamic acid is replaced by valine. One changed letter alters one amino acid and can change the shape of red cells. Heterozygous carriers (HbA HbS) usually stay healthy.
  5. Step 5, Apply it to evolution. Natural selection changes allele frequencies. In a population where 4 in 100 are recessive (q squared = 0.04), q = 0.2 and p = 0.8. Carriers are 2pq = 2 x 0.8 x 0.2 = 0.32, or 32 in 100, far more than the 4 who show the trait. The equation p squared + 2pq + q squared = 1 holds when nothing changes the frequencies.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: cross a round pea plant Rr with a wrinkled plant rr. State the genotypes and phenotype ratio. Answer: gametes R or r from the first, r from the second, so offspring are 1 Rr : 1 rr, which is 1 round : 1 wrinkled. This is also a test cross, showing the round parent is heterozygous.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Apply inheritance laws and molecular basis of inheritance: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Biology and human welfare Weeks 16 to 22Understand

In this unit you work on 'Biology and human welfare', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe health, disease and food production. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Biology and human welfare' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe health, disease and food production. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Draw and label the structure, state the function of each part in one line, then link structure to function: for example, thin walls of the air sacs let oxygen pass quickly into the blood. Method: Model run for 'Biology and human welfare'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe disease causes. Typhoid comes from the bacterium Salmonella typhi in contaminated food and water. Malaria comes from the parasite Plasmodium, carried by the female Anopheles mosquito. Dengue is a virus spread by Aedes mosquitoes. Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba. Prevention follows the route: clean water, hand washing, nets and removing stagnant water.
  2. Step 2, Describe malaria. An infected Anopheles bites and injects the parasite, which enters the liver and then the red blood cells. When red cells burst, toxins release and cause cycles of chills and high fever every 2 to 3 days. Control means draining breeding water, using nets and treating patients with prescribed medicine.
  3. Step 3, Describe immunity. Innate immunity is present at birth: skin, stomach acid, white cells. Acquired immunity develops after exposure, using memory B and T cells. A vaccine contains a weakened or dead pathogen or part of it, which trains memory cells so a later real infection is cleared fast without causing the disease.
  4. Step 4, Describe AIDS. HIV infects helper T cells, so the body slowly loses the ability to fight infection. It spreads through unprotected sex, infected blood or needles, and from mother to child, but not through touch, hugging, or sharing a meal. Early testing, safe blood and clean needles help prevent spread, and treatment can control it.
  5. Step 5, Describe food production. Plant breeding improves crops in steps: collect varied plants, evaluate and select the best, cross-hybridise, select and test the offspring, then release a new variety. Examples are high-yield, disease-resistant wheat and biofortified crops rich in iron or vitamins. Animal husbandry improves milk, eggs and meat by good breeding, feed and care.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: match each disease to its cause or carrier: malaria, dengue, typhoid. Answer: malaria is caused by Plasmodium carried by the female Anopheles; dengue is a virus carried by Aedes mosquitoes; typhoid is caused by Salmonella typhi in contaminated food or water. Prevention for the first two is stopping mosquito breeding, for typhoid clean water.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe health, disease and food production: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Biotechnology and its applications Weeks 23 to 29Understand

In this unit you work on 'Biotechnology and its applications', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain recombinant DNA technology. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Biotechnology and its applications' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: explain recombinant DNA technology. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Draw and label the structure, state the function of each part in one line, then link structure to function: for example, thin walls of the air sacs let oxygen pass quickly into the blood. Method: Model run for 'Biotechnology and its applications'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain the idea of recombinant DNA technology: cut a useful gene from one organism, join it to a carrier called a vector, and place it in a host such as a bacterium so the gene works there. The tools are restriction enzymes (molecular scissors), DNA ligase (glue), a vector such as a plasmid, and a host cell.
  2. Step 2, Explain the cutting. A restriction enzyme recognises a palindromic site. EcoRI recognises GAATTC, which reads the same on the opposite strand from 5' to 3'. It cuts between G and A on each strand, leaving short single-stranded sticky ends (AATT). Cutting the vector and the gene with the same enzyme gives matching sticky ends.
  3. Step 3, Explain the joining. Mix the cut plasmid with the cut gene. The sticky ends pair up by base pairing, and DNA ligase seals the gaps in the sugar-phosphate backbone, giving a recombinant plasmid. Add the plasmid to bacteria after making their walls permeable, in a step called transformation.
  4. Step 4, Explain selection. A plasmid used as a vector carries marker genes, for example one for ampicillin resistance. Spread the bacteria on a plate containing ampicillin: only cells that took up the plasmid survive and form colonies. Safety: handle bacteria only as taught, wear gloves, keep plates closed, wash hands and sterilise waste.
  5. Step 5, Explain amplification by PCR. Each cycle has three steps: denaturation at about 94 degrees C, annealing of primers at about 55 degrees C, and extension at about 72 degrees C. Each cycle doubles the DNA, so n cycles give 2 to the power n copies. After 30 cycles, 2^30 = 1,073,741,824, over a billion copies from one molecule.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: how many copies result from one DNA molecule after 25 PCR cycles, and which enzyme seals the gene into the plasmid? Answer: 2^25 = 33,554,432 copies. The enzyme is DNA ligase. Human insulin made this way in bacteria is a real use, joining the A chain of 21 and the B chain of 30 amino acids.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain recombinant DNA technology: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 5 Ecology and environment Weeks 30 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Ecology and environment', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain ecosystems, biodiversity and conservation. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Ecology and environment' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: explain ecosystems, biodiversity and conservation. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Draw and label the structure, state the function of each part in one line, then link structure to function: for example, thin walls of the air sacs let oxygen pass quickly into the blood. Method: Model run for 'Ecology and environment'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain an ecosystem. It is the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts of an area together: producers such as grass, consumers such as grasshoppers, frogs and hawks, and decomposers such as bacteria and fungi, with abiotic factors such as light, water and soil. A pond and a forest are both ecosystems.
  2. Step 2, Explain energy flow. Sunlight is fixed by producers, and only about 10 percent of the energy passes to each next trophic level, the rest is lost as heat in respiration. If grass holds 10,000 J, grasshoppers get 1,000 J, a frog 100 J, and a snake 10 J. That is why food chains seldom exceed four or five levels.
  3. Step 3, Explain population change. Population size changes by births, deaths, immigration and emigration: N next = N + B - D + I - E. Start with 500 animals, with 60 births, 20 deaths, 10 arrivals and 15 departures. Then 500 + 60 - 20 + 10 - 15 = 535. A growing population needs food and space, so growth slows near the carrying capacity.
  4. Step 4, Explain biodiversity. It means the variety of life at gene, species and ecosystem level. It is highest in warm, wet areas near the equator. India is one of the megadiverse countries and includes biodiversity hotspots such as the Western Ghats. Biodiversity gives food, medicine, clean air and water, and stabilises ecosystems.
  5. Step 5, Explain the four main threats and the conservation methods. Threats: habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien species and co-extinction. In-situ conservation protects species in their natural home, such as national parks, sanctuaries and biosphere reserves. Ex-situ conservation protects them elsewhere, such as zoos, botanical gardens and seed banks.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: grass holds 20,000 J. Following the 10 percent rule through grasshopper, frog, snake and hawk, how much reaches the hawk? Answer: grasshopper 2,000 J, frog 200 J, snake 20 J, hawk 2 J. Only a tiny share reaches the top consumer, so the hawk is rare compared with the grass.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain ecosystems, biodiversity and conservation: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Living or not living

On the slide: L1 Basic · Living or not living

Notes: Name the signs of life, such as movement, feeding, growth and reproduction, and sort everyday things into living and non-living. Notes: collect ten objects or pictures, such as a pebble, a leaf and a toy; learners sort them and give one reason for each choice. Wash hands after touching soil or plants.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Cells and the microscope

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Cells and the microscope

Notes: Describe the main parts of plant and animal cells and say what a microscope does. Notes: practise magnification with eyepiece 10 times and objective 40 times, which gives 400 times. Carry the microscope with two hands and never point it at the sun.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Photosynthesis and respiration

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Photosynthesis and respiration

Notes: Explain photosynthesis and respiration and write the word and symbol equations. Notes: learners check 6CO2 + 6H2O gives C6H12O6 + 6O2 balances by counting atoms. For a leaf starch test, soften the leaf in hot water, never in a flame near ethanol, and wear goggles.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Enzyme rate investigation

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Enzyme rate investigation

Notes: Apply the idea of rate to plan and run a fair enzyme test. Notes: practice case is starch gone in 40 s at 20 C and 20 s at 30 C. Rate is 1/time, so 0.025 and 0.05 per second, a doubling. Learners name three controlled variables and one hazard.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Genetics and chi-squared (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Genetics and chi-squared

Notes: Analyse monohybrid crosses and test results against the expected ratio. Notes: Tt crossed with Tt expects 3 to 1, so 120 tall and 40 short from 160 offspring. Observed 112 and 48 gives chi-squared 2.13, below 3.84 at 5 per cent with one degree of freedom, so no significant difference.

This course: Grade 12 Science Biology is at this level. Its units include Reproduction in organisms and humans; Genetics and evolution; Biology and human welfare; Biotechnology and its applications and more.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Population genetics and molecular tools

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Population genetics and molecular tools

Notes: Apply Hardy-Weinberg and molecular reasoning to predict allele and genotype frequencies. Notes: if 4 per cent of people show a recessive trait, q squared is 0.04, so q is 0.2 and p is 0.8; carriers are 2pq, which is 32 per cent. Learners list the five assumptions and test which one fails.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Design and critique a genomic study

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Design and critique a genomic study

Notes: Evaluate and create an experiment with sound sampling, controls, ethics and multiple-testing correction. Notes: testing 20,000 genes at the 5 per cent level gives about 1,000 false positives by chance, so use false discovery rate control. Learners write a pre-registered plan and list biosafety and consent approvals.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.
