Quantum computing application builder

Run a pilot of quantum computing application builder in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small quantum computing application builder offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Find problems where quantum may help 6 hUnderstand

Quantum computers do not speed up everything. Your first job is to find problems where they may help and to say honestly where they will not.

  • Quantum methods are studied for three families of problem: simulating molecules and materials, searching or optimising among huge numbers of choices, and some cryptography tasks.
  • Most office work, databases and web pages gain nothing from quantum hardware. A buyer who hears 'quantum' for these should be told so.
  • Ask of any problem: how many choices, how costly is a wrong answer, and what does the best classical method already achieve? Write the three answers before you propose anything.
  • Count the choices with a simple formula and write the number down. A route that visits 10 stops once each, with the first stop fixed, has 9 x 8 x 7 x ... x 1 = 362,880 possible orders, which a laptop checks in moments.
  • Learn the term 'quantum-inspired': classical software that borrows ideas from quantum methods and runs today on ordinary computers. Test it before you recommend buying quantum time.
  • Canadian buyers include research groups, banks, logistics firms and energy companies; write one concrete problem for each.
  • End every screen with a one-line verdict: use now, watch and look again on a set date, or not suitable. Give the reason in plain words.
Worked example. Delivery routing for 10 stops, with the first stop fixed, has 9 x 8 x ... x 1 = 362,880 orders, which a laptop checks at once. For 40 stops the count is a 47-digit number, but good classical heuristics already find routes close to the best. Verdict: quantum is not yet worth the cost; recommend a classical solver and a watch list with a review date.

Activity. Write a one-page 'is it quantum?' screen for three real problems and give a verdict with the three answers for each.

Standard to meet. Each verdict cites the classical baseline; at least one problem is rejected with reasons.

Module 2 Know the rules, exports and standards in Canada 6 hApply

Quantum technology is a controlled field. The rules decide who you may work with and what you may send abroad, so check them before you share anything.

  • Strategic technology can need a federal export permit; check the control list before you send code, designs or papers to another country.
  • Research funding and public contracts often require security screening of staff and a data-handling plan.
  • Cryptography guidance from the federal cyber agency sets timelines for moving to quantum-safe methods; track it.
  • Intellectual property: agree in writing who owns code and results when you work with a university or client.
  • Cloud quantum hardware may sit in another country. Before you upload client data, read the contract to see where data is stored, and check the federal and provincial privacy rules.
  • Foreign students, staff or visitors may need a check before they see controlled designs; check the provincial and federal rule for your case.
  • Date every row of your rules table. Lists and guidance change, so set a review reminder every six months.
Worked example. Row: 'Send circuit design to a partner abroad | federal export control | check list, ask for permit if listed | before first send | permit or written no-permit-needed note on file'. If the table has 12 rows and reviews run every 6 months, you recheck 12 x 2 = 24 rows a year, about 2 a month.

Activity. Build the rules table for a 3-person quantum start-up in your province, with a source and date per row.

Standard to meet. Every row has an enforcer, action and date; no rule guessed.

Module 3 Build and run a small quantum program 10 hApply

You learn by running real circuits on a simulator and on freely available cloud hardware, and recording what the noise does.

  • A qubit holds a blend of 0 and 1 until measured. Measure many times ('shots') and you get a pattern of counts, not one answer.
  • Start with two circuits: a coin-flip (one gate) and an entangled pair (two gates). Predict the counts, then run them.
  • Compare simulator and real hardware: the hardware shows errors. Record the error rate as wrong counts divided by total shots.
  • Use version control, and save the circuit, shots, device name, date and result as one table row per run.
  • Turn counts into shares by dividing each count by the shots. With 1,000 shots, a count of 470 is 47%.
  • Run the same circuit at 100 shots and at 1,000 shots and compare. Write one sentence on why more shots give a steadier result.
  • Change one thing at a time between runs, such as the device or the shots, so you know what caused any change.
Worked example. Entangled pair, 1,000 shots, ideal result: about 500 '00' and 500 '11'. Real device: 470 '00', 455 '11', 75 other. The three counts add to 470 + 455 + 75 = 1,000, so nothing is missing. Error rate = 75 / 1,000 = 7.5%.

Activity. Run both circuits on a simulator and on one real device, 1,000 shots each, and tabulate the results.

Standard to meet. Predictions written before runs; error rate computed; run table complete.

Module 4 Cost a proof-of-concept project 8 hEvaluate

Quantum work is paid by the project. Price staff time, cloud hardware time and review, and show a buyer what they get.

  • Variable cost per package is the hardware time, extra staff review and reporting that each additional study uses.
  • Fixed cost per month is the tools, insurance, workspace, training and a share of the lead's time that you pay whatever you sell.
  • Offer a fixed-price study (for example 6 weeks) with a clear deliverable: a verdict, a baseline comparison and a plan.
  • Add 15% contingency because hardware queues and noise slow the schedule.
  • Margin per package = price minus variable cost. Break-even packages = fixed cost per month divided by margin, rounded up.
  • Write three cases, with 3, 5 and 8 packages sold in a month, and show the profit or loss for each.
  • Check what free or low-cost simulator and cloud access can do before you charge for paid hardware time.
Worked example. Study package price 2,400; variable cost 900; margin 1,500 (about 63%). Fixed cost 7,000 a month needs 7,000 / 1,500 = 4.67, so 5 packages. With 15% contingency on variable cost, 900 x 1.15 = 1,035, margin 1,365, and 7,000 / 1,365 = 5.13, so 6 packages. Three cases at the 1,500 margin: 3 packages give 4,500, a loss of 2,500; 5 give 7,500, a profit of 500; 8 give 12,000, a profit of 5,000. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build the cost sheet and compute break-even; show a case where only 3 packages sell.

Standard to meet. Units and currency shown; three cases; arithmetic checked.

Module 5 Operate the lab and keep records 8 hApply

Good quantum work is repeatable work. Records make results believable.

  • Record device, software version, calibration date, shots, random seed and result for every run.
  • Re-run any surprising result three times before you report it.
  • Keep a record of expected versus observed counts for each circuit and flag any drift over time.
  • Control access to cloud accounts and rotate keys; never paste keys into notes.
  • Back up the run table and circuit files to a second place each week, and test that you can open the copy.
  • Name files by date and circuit so a colleague can find a run in under a minute.
  • At the end of each week, write one page: runs made, surprises, causes found and one change for next week.
Worked example. Weekly page: 12 runs, 2 surprising. Each surprise was repeated 3 times, so 2 x 3 = 6 extra runs and 18 runs in all. One surprise traced to a device recalibration; action: add calibration date to the run table.

Activity. Keep a 4-week run log and one weekly page.

Standard to meet. Every run has all six fields; surprises are repeated.

Module 6 Explain it and win a first client 6 hCreate

Buyers fund what they understand. Learn to explain quantum value in plain words with numbers.

  • Use the answer-first form: result, reason, risk, next step.
  • Never promise advantage you have not shown; show a measured comparison with the best classical method.
  • Offer a no-regret first step: a two-week screen of the client's problems.
  • Partner with a university group or industry cluster for credibility and access to hardware.
  • Replace jargon with a picture or a number. Say 'tests many options at once' only if you can show how.
  • Prepare one slide that shows the classical result, the quantum result and the difference, with units.
  • List the three questions a doubtful buyer will ask and write a one-sentence honest answer to each.
Worked example. Pitch: 'We screened six scheduling problems; two may benefit from quantum-inspired methods now, which is 2 of 6, or 33%. We propose a six-week study with a stop point after week two.' The client can say yes to a small step, not a large bet.

Activity. Give a five-minute explanation to a non-expert and collect three written questions.

Standard to meet. Listener can repeat the verdict and the next step.

Module 7 Handle security, ethics and trust 6 hEvaluate

Quantum advances create real risks as well as benefits.

  • Data stolen today may be decrypted later; help clients inventory what must stay secret for ten years or more.
  • Plan the move to quantum-safe encryption: list systems, rank by life of data, test, replace in stages.
  • Be honest in marketing: do not claim speed-ups that you have not measured.
  • Include researchers and users from many backgrounds when you choose test problems.
  • Tell clients which of their keys, certificates and vendors would need change, and who in their team owns each.
  • Keep client problem data to the minimum needed, and delete it when the study ends as agreed.
  • Say what you do not know. A short list of open questions builds more trust than a confident guess.
Worked example. Inventory row: 'Customer records | secret for 25 years | high risk | pilot quantum-safe key exchange in test system by Q3'. A system whose data must last 25 years and whose migration takes 3 years should start planning now.

Activity. Write a one-page quantum-safe inventory for a fictional clinic.

Standard to meet. Each system has a data life and a migration step.

Module 8 Run a pilot study and plan growth 8 hCreate

A six-week pilot with a real client proves your process.

  • Agree scope, a deliverable and a stop rule in writing.
  • Report weekly: tried, learned, next.
  • Close with a verdict, evidence and a recommendation, even if the verdict is 'not yet'.
  • Plan 12 months: three milestones, funding sources and a hiring plan.
  • Set the success measure before you start, such as a measured gain over the classical baseline with units.
  • Hold a review with the client in week 2 and week 6 and write down any change to scope.
  • List funding routes to check: public research grants, innovation programmes and client co-funding; check each programme's rules and dates.
Worked example. Stop rule: 'If after week 2 no problem passes the screen, we stop and refund the unused part.' In a 6-week pilot, week 2 is one third of the time, so the client risks little. Outcome: one problem passes; study continues.

Activity. Run a pilot on a sample problem and present the review.

Standard to meet. Review includes a verdict, baseline comparison and a 12-month plan.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore
