Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in ESL level E4 English as a second language, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 9 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

9 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Academic reading Weeks 1 to 4Remember

In this unit you work on 'Academic reading', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: read subject texts and find main ideas and evidence. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Academic reading' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: read subject texts and find main ideas and evidence, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Skim the title and first lines to predict, then scan for a name or number. Text: 'The water cycle has four stages.' Predict four stages, then find and list them. Method: Model run for 'Academic reading'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Preview the text. Text: 'Bees are important for food. Many crops, such as apples and almonds, need pollinators to make fruit and seeds. Scientists estimate that about one third of the food people eat depends on animal pollination. However, bee numbers have fallen in some regions because of habitat loss and pesticides. Planting flowers can help.' Read the title and first sentence first.
  2. Step 2, Find the main idea. The first sentence often gives it: 'Bees are important for food.' Check the other sentences. They explain why (crops need pollinators), a problem (fewer bees) and a solution (plant flowers). So the main idea is that bees matter for our food, and they need protection.
  3. Step 3, Find the evidence. Evidence supports the main idea with facts. Evidence 1: apples and almonds need pollinators. Evidence 2: about one third of our food depends on animal pollination. Underline both in two colours: one for examples, one for numbers or estimates.
  4. Step 4, Notice signal words. 'However' marks a contrast: after good news about bees, the text gives a problem. 'Because of' introduces causes: habitat loss and pesticides. 'Can help' introduces a solution. Signal words show how ideas connect, so circle each one.
  5. Step 5, Take notes in your own words. Main idea: bees help produce food. Evidence: apples, almonds, one third of food. Problem: fewer bees, because of lost habitat and pesticides. Solution: plant flowers. Use short phrases, not full sentences, and do not copy long parts of the text.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: give the main idea and one piece of evidence from the text. Answer: Main idea: bees are important for our food. Evidence: about one third of the food people eat depends on animal pollination (or apples and almonds need pollinators).

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Read subject texts and find main ideas and evidence: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 2 Academic writing Weeks 5 to 8Apply

In this unit you work on 'Academic writing', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: write a structured paragraph and short essay. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Academic writing' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: write a structured paragraph and short essay, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Paragraph frame: topic sentence, two supports, closing sentence. 'Recycling helps our city. First, it reduces waste. Second, it saves energy. So we should recycle.' Method: Model run for 'Academic writing'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Choose a claim for one paragraph. Topic: Students should read for 20 minutes every day. Use the TEEL structure: Topic sentence, Explanation, Evidence, Link. Write each letter in the margin to plan the paragraph before you write any full sentences.
  2. Step 2, Write the topic sentence and explanation. 'Students should read for twenty minutes every day. Regular reading builds vocabulary and improves concentration, which helps students in every subject.' The topic sentence states the claim. The explanation gives the reason.
  3. Step 3, Add evidence. 'Twenty minutes a day is 7,300 minutes in a year (20 x 365), which is more than 120 hours of reading.' Then link: 'Therefore, a short daily habit gives students a large amount of practice.' Use numbers carefully, and check the arithmetic.
  4. Step 4, Plan a short essay of five paragraphs. Introduction with a thesis, two body paragraphs (TEEL each), a counter-argument paragraph, and a conclusion. Thesis: 'Daily reading helps students learn, so schools should protect time for it.' Use linking words such as firstly, in addition and therefore.
  5. Step 5, Write the conclusion and check. 'In conclusion, daily reading improves vocabulary and builds a habit that lasts. Schools should therefore give time for it.' Do not add new evidence. Check: topic sentences, capital letters, full stops, linking words and one idea per paragraph.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write a TEEL paragraph about 'Students should drink water at school.' Model answer: Students should drink water at school. Water helps the body and brain work well. For example, one bottle of 500 mL is easy to carry. Therefore, water keeps students ready to learn.

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Write a structured paragraph and short essay: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 3 Note-taking and summarising Weeks 9 to 12Apply

In this unit you work on 'Note-taking and summarising', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: take notes and write a short summary. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Note-taking and summarising' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: take notes and write a short summary, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Sentence heard: 'Plants make food using sunlight, water and carbon dioxide.' Notes: plants make food = sunlight + water + CO2. Method: Model run for 'Note-taking and summarising'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read the text once for the main idea, then mark key words. Text: "Urban gardens grow food in small city spaces. They reduce transport costs and give neighbours a shared project." Main idea: city gardens benefit food supply and community.
  2. Step 2, Take notes with a heading and abbreviations. Model: "Urban gardens: = food in small city spaces; benefits: 1. cheaper transport, 2. community project." Reason: arrows, numbers and keywords replace full sentences and save time.
  3. Step 3, Separate main points from examples. Main point: gardens reduce costs. Example: "tomatoes grown on a roof need no lorry." Reason: a summary keeps the main points and usually drops the examples.
  4. Step 4, Paraphrase instead of copying. Original: "give neighbours a shared project." Paraphrase: "bring local people together." Reason: new wording shows understanding and avoids copying the source.
  5. Step 5, Write the summary in about thirty words. "Urban gardens produce food in small city areas. They lower transport costs and encourage neighbours to work together. As a result, they improve both local food supply and community life."
  6. Step 6, Fix an error. Weak: "I think urban gardens are great and my uncle has one." Better: remove personal opinion and detail. A summary is neutral, shorter than the original, and in your own words.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Text: "Libraries now offer digital books, which readers borrow online. This saves travel time and lets people read without paying." Write notes and a one-sentence summary. Model answer: "Libraries lend digital books online, saving readers time and money."

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Take notes and write a short summary: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 4 Subject vocabulary: maths and science Weeks 13 to 16Apply

In this unit you work on 'Subject vocabulary: maths and science', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: use key terms in class tasks. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Subject vocabulary: maths and science' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: use key terms in class tasks, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Word set: angle, triangle, sum; solid, liquid, gas. Use each in a sentence: 'A triangle has three angles.' Make a two-column word bank with a picture clue. Method: Model run for 'Subject vocabulary: maths and science'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Learn maths words with an example. For 12 and 4: sum = 12 + 4 = 16; difference = 12 - 4 = 8; product = 12 x 4 = 48; quotient = 12 / 4 = 3. Say a full sentence: 'The product of 12 and 4 is 48.' Write each term with its operation in a table.
  2. Step 2, Use shape words in a task. A rectangle is 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. Area = length x width = 6 x 4 = 24 cm squared. Perimeter = 2 x (6 + 4) = 20 cm. Perimeter is the distance around the shape; area is the space inside.
  3. Step 3, Learn science measuring words. Mass is how much matter an object has (grams). Volume is the space it takes (millilitres). To measure 50 mL of water, put a measuring cylinder on a flat table and read the scale at eye level, at the bottom of the curved surface.
  4. Step 4, Learn action words for experiments. Dissolve: sugar dissolves in water. Evaporate: water evaporates when heated. Observe, record, compare. Safety: wear goggles when heating, wipe spills at once so nobody slips, and ask a teacher before using hot water.
  5. Step 5, Follow an instruction and report it. 'Measure 50 mL of water, add one spoon of salt, stir, and record what you observe.' Report: 'The salt dissolved in the water. I observed that the water stayed clear.' Use past tense for what you did and observed.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a rectangle is 5 cm by 3 cm. Give the area and perimeter in a sentence. Answer: The area is 15 cm squared (5 x 3) and the perimeter is 16 cm (2 x (5 + 3)).

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Use key terms in class tasks: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 5 Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing Weeks 17 to 20Apply

In this unit you work on 'Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: use key terms in class tasks. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: use key terms in class tasks, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Word set: angle, triangle, sum; solid, liquid, gas. Use each in a sentence: 'A triangle has three angles.' Make a two-column word bank with a picture clue. Method: Model run for 'Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Learn social studies words. Population: the number of people living in a place. Government: the group that makes decisions for a country. Economy: how money, work and goods are made and shared. Climate: usual weather over many years. Write each word with a short example sentence about your own region.
  2. Step 2, Use a number task. Population density is population divided by area. A town has 6,000 people and an area of 3 square kilometres. 6,000 / 3 = 2,000 people per square kilometre. Say: 'The population density is 2,000 people per square kilometre.' Use 'per' for rates.
  3. Step 3, Use map words. A map key explains symbols, and a scale shows real distance. If 1 cm on the map is 5 km, then 4 cm is 4 x 5 = 20 km. Say: 'The distance between the two towns is 20 kilometres.' Use compass directions: north, south, east, west.
  4. Step 4, Learn computing words. A file is one saved document. A folder holds files. A password protects an account, and you should never share it. An algorithm is a list of steps to solve a problem. Input is the data you give; output is the result you get.
  5. Step 5, Read a tiny program. age = 12 / if age >= 10: / print('junior'). The program stores 12 in age, tests if age is at least 10, and prints junior, so the output is: junior. Explain: 'If the condition is true, the computer runs the next line.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a village has 900 people and covers 2 square kilometres. Say the density. Answer: 900 / 2 = 450, so the population density is 450 people per square kilometre.

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Use key terms in class tasks: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 6 Presentations and discussion Weeks 21 to 24Apply

In this unit you work on 'Presentations and discussion', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: present and respond to questions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Presentations and discussion' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: present and respond to questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Structure: Hello, my topic is..., first..., second..., to finish..., thank you. Practise aloud with a one-minute timer and look at the listeners. Method: Model run for 'Presentations and discussion'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Plan the structure. A short talk has three parts: introduction, main points, conclusion. Topic: Why I recycle. Plan two main points: it saves materials and it reduces waste. A three-minute talk at about 120 words a minute is around 360 words, so choose only two or three ideas.
  2. Step 2, Open clearly. 'Good morning, everyone. My name is Sara, and today I will talk about recycling. I will give two reasons: it saves materials and it reduces waste.' Greet, introduce yourself, say the topic and tell the audience what is coming.
  3. Step 3, Signpost the main points. 'My first point is that recycling saves materials.' 'Now I will move to my second point.' 'In conclusion, recycling is easy and useful.' Signpost phrases help the audience follow you. Look at the audience, speak slowly and pause after each point.
  4. Step 4, Use a visual simply. Put one picture or three key words on each slide, not a whole paragraph. Point to the slide: 'As you can see here, a bottle can be reused.' Do not read from the slide. Practise your talk aloud twice, and time it.
  5. Step 5, Respond to questions. 'Thank you for your question.' 'Good question. I think...' If you do not understand: 'Could you repeat that, please?' If you do not know: 'I am not sure, but I can find out.' Keep answers short, polite and honest.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write an opening and a closing for a talk about school sport. Model answer: Good morning, everyone. Today I will talk about school sport. In conclusion, sport keeps us healthy and teaches teamwork. Thank you. Are there any questions?

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Present and respond to questions: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 7 Grammar for accuracy Weeks 25 to 28Apply

In this unit you work on 'Grammar for accuracy', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: control tenses, articles and prepositions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Grammar for accuracy' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: control tenses, articles and prepositions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Present simple for habits: 'She goes to school by bus.' Present continuous for now: 'She is reading.' Fix the slip: 'He go' becomes 'He goes'. Method: Model run for 'Grammar for accuracy'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Control the tense by matching it to the time clue. Finished time uses the past simple: I lived in Cairo in 2015. Time that continues to now uses the present perfect: I have lived here since 2019. Ask first: does the time word say 'finished' (yesterday, in 2015) or 'up to now' (since, for)?
  2. Step 2, Add the past perfect for the earlier of two past events. When I arrived, the train had left. The leaving happened first, so it takes had plus the past participle. Compare: When I arrived, the train left (it left after I came). Write two pairs of sentences and say which event came first.
  3. Step 3, Control articles. Use a or an for one of many: She is a doctor. Use the for something known or unique: The doctor I met was kind. Use no article for general plural or abstract ideas: Doctors work hard. Life is short. Also say She plays the piano and I go to school by bus.
  4. Step 4, Control prepositions with fixed patterns. Time: at six o'clock, on Monday, in July. Adjective plus preposition: good at maths, interested in music, afraid of dogs. Verb plus preposition: depend on, wait for, listen to. Learn them as whole chunks and test yourself by covering the preposition.
  5. Step 5, Edit a paragraph with three types of error. Wrong: Yesterday I have gone to market. I am interested on fruit. Right: Yesterday I went to the market. I am interested in fruit. The errors were a tense (yesterday needs the past simple), an article (the market) and a preposition (interested in). Check each type separately.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: correct this sentence. I am living here since three years and my sister is good in maths and plays piano. Answer: I have lived here for three years and my sister is good at maths and plays the piano.

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Control tenses, articles and prepositions: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 8 Exam skills Weeks 29 to 32Apply

In this unit you work on 'Exam skills', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: manage time and read questions carefully. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Exam skills' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: manage time and read questions carefully, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Read each question twice, underline the command word (describe, explain, compare), plan three points, and leave two minutes to check. Method: Model run for 'Exam skills'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Divide the time before you start. For a 60-minute paper with three sections, give reading 20 minutes, writing 30 minutes, checking 10 minutes. Reason: a fixed plan stops you from spending too long on one task.
  2. Step 2, Read the instruction line carefully. Question: "Write 100-120 words about a day you remember." Underline: "100-120 words", "a day you remember". Reason: word limits and topic words are easily missed.
  3. Step 3, Notice command words. "Describe" means give details, "explain" means give reasons, "compare" means show similarity and difference. Model: for "Explain why", start "This happened because..." Reason: the command word decides the answer type.
  4. Step 4, Answer a multiple-choice item by checking each option. Question: "The writer feels worried." Options: A "calm", B "nervous", C "proud". Text says "her hands shook". Answer: B. Reason: pick the option that matches evidence in the text.
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Wrong: writing 200 words when asked for 100-120 and having no time to check. Right: write a short plan of three bullets, then write about 110 words and keep two minutes for checking.
  6. Step 6, Use a checking list at the end. "Did I answer every part? Are my verbs in the right tense? Capital letters and full stops? Spelling of difficult words?" Reason: a routine catches small errors fast.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. A 40-minute reading test has 4 passages. Plan your time. Model answer: about 8 minutes reading and answering each passage, which leaves 8 minutes to check, so skip a hard question and return to it later.

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Manage time and read questions carefully: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Unit 9 Review and exit check Weeks 33 to 36Apply

In this unit you work on 'Review and exit check', taught in a way that is short, spoken, with pictures and real objects. The target is clear: show readiness to join the main class. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Review and exit check' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: show readiness to join the main class, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Read each question twice, underline the command word (describe, explain, compare), plan three points, and leave two minutes to check. Method: Model run for 'Review and exit check'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Check your sentence control with a short paragraph. Model: "Although I was nervous, I spoke clearly. My teacher said my presentation was well organised." Reason: a joined sentence with although shows you can combine ideas accurately.
  2. Step 2, Review verb tenses in one passage. "Yesterday I went to the library. I am going to borrow two books. I have read one already." Reason: past simple, going to and present perfect show three time frames used correctly.
  3. Step 3, Check word choice and connectors. Weak: "It was good. And then it was good again." Better: "The first lesson was useful; moreover, the second was interesting." Connectors: however, therefore, in addition.
  4. Step 4, Write a short opinion paragraph. "I believe students should learn to cook because it builds independence. Furthermore, it encourages healthy eating. Therefore, schools should include cooking classes." Reason: claim, reason, extension and conclusion.
  5. Step 5, Fix typical errors. Wrong: "Each of the students have a book." Right: "Each of the students has a book." Wrong: "I am agree." Right: "I agree." Check subject-verb agreement and do not use be before agree.
  6. Step 6, Speak your readiness aloud. Prompt: "What did you enjoy about your course?" Answer: "I enjoyed the group projects because they helped me practise speaking, and I became more confident." Reason: give an answer plus a reason.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Write three sentences about your progress using one connector and two tenses. Model answer: "I have improved my speaking. Last term I was shy; however, now I join discussions. Next year I am going to write longer essays."

Activity. About 15 to 25 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a voice note or one page of work.

Standard to meet. Show readiness to join the main class: at least 4 of 5 attempts understood by a listener who does not know you.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Unit 1 (Academic reading): Give the main idea and one piece of evidence from the text.

Model answer: Main idea: bees are important for our food. Evidence: about one third of the food people eat depends on animal pollination (or apples and almonds need pollinators). (Covered in unit 1: Academic reading.)

Q2. Unit 2 (Academic writing): Write a TEEL paragraph about 'Students should drink water at school.'.

Model answer: Students should drink water at school. Water helps the body and brain work well. For example, one bottle of 500 mL is easy to carry. Therefore, water keeps students ready to learn. (Covered in unit 2: Academic writing.)

Q3. Unit 3 (Note-taking and summarising): Text: "Libraries now offer digital books, which readers borrow online. This saves travel time and lets people read without paying." Write notes and a one-sentence summary.

Model answer: "Libraries lend digital books online, saving readers time and money." (Covered in unit 3: Note-taking and summarising.)

Q4. Unit 4 (Subject vocabulary: maths and science): A rectangle is 5 cm by 3 cm. Give the area and perimeter in a sentence.

Model answer: The area is 15 cm squared (5 x 3) and the perimeter is 16 cm (2 x (5 + 3)). (Covered in unit 4: Subject vocabulary: maths and science.)

Q5. Unit 5 (Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing): A village has 900 people and covers 2 square kilometres. Say the density.

Model answer: 900 / 2 = 450, so the population density is 450 people per square kilometre. (Covered in unit 5: Subject vocabulary: social studies and computing.)

Q6. Unit 6 (Presentations and discussion): Write an opening and a closing for a talk about school sport.

Model answer: Good morning, everyone. Today I will talk about school sport. In conclusion, sport keeps us healthy and teaches teamwork. Thank you. Are there any questions? (Covered in unit 6: Presentations and discussion.)

Q7. Unit 7 (Grammar for accuracy): Correct this sentence. I am living here since three years and my sister is good in maths and plays piano.

Model answer: I have lived here for three years and my sister is good at maths and plays the piano. (Covered in unit 7: Grammar for accuracy.)

Q8. Unit 8 (Exam skills): A 40-minute reading test has 4 passages. Plan your time.

Model answer: about 8 minutes reading and answering each passage, which leaves 8 minutes to check, so skip a hard question and return to it later. (Covered in unit 8: Exam skills.)

Q9. Unit 9 (Review and exit check): Write three sentences about your progress using one connector and two tenses.

Model answer: "I have improved my speaking. Last term I was shy; however, now I join discussions. Next year I am going to write longer essays." (Covered in unit 9: Review and exit check.)

Q10. Unit 1 (Academic reading): Find the main idea. Show how you do it and say what the result is.

Model answer: The first sentence often gives it: 'Bees are important for food.' Check the other sentences. They explain why (crops need pollinators), a problem (fewer bees) and a solution (plant flowers). So the main idea is that bees matter for our food, and they need protection. (Covered in unit 1: Academic reading.)

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: First words and survival phrases (this course)

On the slide: L1 Basic · First words and survival phrases

Notes: Say hello, give your name and ask for help in simple English. Notes: teach Hello, My name is... and Can you help me? with gestures and pictures; learners practise each phrase with three partners and point to objects as they name them.

This course: ESL level E4 English as a second language is at this level. Its units include Academic reading; Academic writing; Note-taking and summarising; Subject vocabulary: maths and science and more.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Everyday words and short sentences

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Everyday words and short sentences

Notes: Understand and write short sentences about family, food and daily routine, using present simple. Notes: show I eat rice, she eats bread; learners write five true sentences about their day, read them to a partner, and fix the verb ending together.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Questions, past tense and short texts

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Questions, past tense and short texts

Notes: Ask and answer questions and write a short paragraph about last weekend using past simple. Notes: contrast I go and I went; learners interview a partner using What did you do?, then write six sentences from the answers and underline each past verb.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Real-life communication and listening

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Real-life communication and listening

Notes: Follow a two-minute conversation and take part in a phone call, a shop visit or a clinic visit. Notes: play a short recording with a booking at a clinic; learners note three key facts, then role-play the same call with their own details and ask for repetition politely.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Academic and workplace English

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Academic and workplace English

Notes: Write a 250-word report or email and give a short talk with clear structure, linking words and few errors. Notes: teach First, However, As a result; learners rewrite a plain paragraph with three linkers, then present it in two minutes with a peer giving feedback.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Language analysis and teaching methods

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Language analysis and teaching methods

Notes: Explain why learners make common errors and design a lesson that targets them using evidence from learner writing. Notes: collect ten samples; learners classify errors such as article, tense or word order, rank them by frequency, and plan a thirty-minute fix for the top two.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research and curriculum design

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research and curriculum design

Notes: Design a classroom-based study or a full course plan for adult learners, with measures and ethics set out. Notes: choose a question such as whether peer feedback improves writing; define the sample, the pre-test and post-test, and one risk, then present the plan for critique.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.
