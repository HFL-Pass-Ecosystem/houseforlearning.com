E-commerce Operations

Run the back end of a small online shop: stock, orders, packing, returns and customer service. You learn the order-to-delivery cycle, stock control, accurate picking and packing, shipping cutoffs, returns, service replies and error analysis.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 130 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Run the back end of a small online shop: stock, orders, packing, returns and customer service. You learn the order-to-delivery cycle, stock control, accurate picking and packing, shipping cutoffs, returns, service replies and error analysis.

  • Understand Explain the order-to-delivery cycle.
  • Apply Handle orders accurately.
  • Analyse Analyse errors and returns.
  • Create Design a stock and returns process.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run order handling for a small online shop for 30 days and report accuracy, speed, returns and service replies, with a plan to cut the top error cause.

Condition

About 130 hours over 3 to 5 months. A small shop partner, a free spreadsheet, a mentor and a peer circle.

Standard

Stock system; order log; accuracy and speed numbers; returns handled; service replies; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (130 h guided)

Module 1 Order cycle 8 hUnderstand

An online order goes through a cycle from the customer clicking buy to the product arriving and maybe coming back. Each step can fail. Knowing the cycle shows where to put checks.

  • The cycle has six steps: order, pick, pack, ship, deliver and return.
  • Each step has a person or system responsible and a typical time, which you should record.
  • Errors early in the cycle, such as picking the wrong item, cause costly returns later.
  • Time each step for a sample of orders to find the slowest step.
  • A common mistake is to look only at shipping speed and ignore errors in picking.
Worked example. Out of 50 orders in a test week, 3 are returned because the wrong size was picked. That is 3 divided by 50, or 6%, and all three trace back to the pick step.

Activity. Map the cycle for your shop as a flow chart with the person, the tool and the typical time at each step.

Standard to meet. Your map shows all six steps with owners and times, and you mark at least two places where errors are likely.

Module 2 Stock 14 hApply

Good stock control means you know what you have and where it is. You count regularly and reorder in time. Running out loses sales and holding too much ties up money.

  • Give every item a bin or shelf label and a code that matches your sales list.
  • Count stock each week and fix any difference between the count and the record.
  • Calculate the reorder point as daily sales times the days to restock plus a safety amount.
  • Record each reorder with the date, quantity and supplier.
  • A common mistake is to trust the stock record without counting.
Worked example. An item sells 4 units a day and takes 3 days to restock. Lead-time demand is 4 times 3, which is 12 units. Adding a safety amount of 8 gives a reorder point of 20 units.

Activity. Set up a stock sheet for at least 15 items with bin labels, counts and reorder points, then do a count and fix any differences.

Standard to meet. Your count matches the record for at least 90% of items after correction, and each reorder point has a worked calculation.

Module 3 Pick and pack 16 hApply

Picking is finding the right item and packing is protecting it for delivery. Small checks at this stage stop most errors. Speed comes after accuracy.

  • Use a pick list that shows the item code, location and quantity for each order line.
  • Check the item against the order by scanning or by reading the code aloud before it goes in the box.
  • Pack with filling so the item cannot move, and add a packing slip with the order number.
  • Measure accuracy as the number of correct orders divided by the total orders.
  • A common mistake is to speed up without checking, which raises errors and returns.
Worked example. You pick and pack 20 test orders and 19 are correct. Accuracy is 19 divided by 20, which is 95%, and the one wrong order had a similar looking colour.

Activity. Pick and pack at least 20 real or practice orders, record the time and errors for each, and calculate accuracy and average time per order.

Standard to meet. Your accuracy is at least 95%, you recorded the time of each order and you can say what caused each error.

Module 4 Ship and track 12 hApply

Shipping is about keeping a promise on time. A daily cutoff and tracking numbers help customers know when to expect delivery. Delays should be told early.

  • Set a daily cutoff time, such as 2 pm, and tell customers what it means for their order.
  • Hand parcels to the carrier at the same time each day and keep the receipt.
  • Send tracking information to the customer within an hour of dispatch.
  • Contact customers early when a parcel is delayed and offer a clear next step.
  • A common mistake is to promise next-day delivery for orders placed after the cutoff.
Worked example. On a test day, 40 orders come in and 34 arrive before the 2 pm cutoff. So 34 divided by 40, or 85%, ship the same day, and the other 6 are told they will ship the next day.

Activity. Ship at least 10 test orders and complete a log with cutoff time, dispatch time, tracking number and the message sent.

Standard to meet. Your log shows every order dispatched on its promised day and every customer message sent within an hour.

Module 5 Returns 14 hApply

Returns are part of online selling. A clear policy and a quick, fair process keeps customers and saves money. You also learn from why items come back.

  • Write a returns policy in plain words with the time limit, condition required and how refunds are made.
  • Inspect each returned item and record the reason and the condition.
  • Refund or swap within a stated time, such as 5 days after the item arrives.
  • Put returned goods back into stock only if they are in saleable condition.
  • A common mistake is to keep no record of reasons, which loses useful information.
Worked example. Out of 80 orders, 4 are returned, which is 4 divided by 80, or 5%. Each refund is S$24, so refunds total 4 times 24, which is S$96, and 3 of the 4 are for size problems.

Activity. Handle at least five test returns and keep a log with the reason, condition, decision, refund amount and time taken.

Standard to meet. Every return has a recorded reason, all are processed within your stated time and you identify the most common reason.

Module 6 Customer service 16 hCreate

Good service means a fast, kind and helpful reply. Template answers save time but must still feel personal. You also know when to pass a case to someone else.

  • Aim to reply to every message within 24 hours and track the time to first reply.
  • Write template answers for common questions such as delay, wrong item and refund status.
  • Add the customer name and order number to every reply so it feels personal.
  • Pass a case to a senior person when it involves a safety issue, a legal threat or an amount above your limit.
  • A common mistake is to copy a template without checking it fits the question.
Worked example. You receive 25 messages in a week and reply to 23 within 24 hours. That is 23 divided by 25, or 92%, and the 2 late replies were sent over a weekend.

Activity. Write at least six template replies and answer 10 test messages, recording the reply time for each.

Standard to meet. Your templates are clear and polite, at least 90% of replies are within 24 hours and each reply includes the order number.

Module 7 30 days 40 hApply

You now run the shop operations for 30 days. Keep a complete log of orders, errors and costs. Honest numbers are the base for improvement.

  • Record every order with the pick time, pack time, ship time and any error.
  • Record every return and every complaint with a reason.
  • Add up the cost of packing materials, labour time and shipping for each order.
  • Calculate the error rate as orders with errors divided by total orders.
  • A common mistake is to stop recording when the work gets busy.
Worked example. In the first week there are 95 orders and 2 have errors. The error rate is 2 divided by 95, which is about 2.1%, and both errors were a wrong size.

Activity. Run order handling for 30 days and submit the order log, the error and returns log and a weekly summary with the error rate.

Standard to meet. Your log covers all 30 days, the weekly error rates are correct and each error has a cause.

Module 8 Review 10 hEvaluate

The review looks at your data and decides what to fix first. You find the biggest cause of errors and plan a simple fix. Then you measure again.

  • Group errors by cause, such as wrong size, wrong item or damage, and count each group.
  • Find the cause that creates the most errors and focus on it first.
  • Choose a simple, low-cost fix, such as a scan check or a better label.
  • Set a target, such as halving that error cause, and a date to re-measure.
  • A common mistake is to fix several things at once and not know which worked.
Worked example. Of 12 errors in a month, 7 were wrong size, which is 7 divided by 12, or about 58%. A scan check at packing could remove most of them, so you set a goal of fewer than 4 wrong size errors next month, which is about half of 7.

Activity. Present a plan showing your error causes, the chosen fix, the target and the date for the next review.

Standard to meet. Your plan targets the largest cause with a fix, a numeric target and a date.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the steps in the order cycle.

Model answer: Order, pick, pack, ship, deliver and return. (Covered in module 1: Order cycle. To pass that module: Your map shows all six steps with owners and times, and you mark at least two places where errors are likely.)

Q2. What is a reorder point?

Model answer: The stock level that triggers a new order so you do not run out. (Covered in module 2: Stock. To pass that module: Your count matches the record for at least 90% of items after correction, and each reorder point has a worked calculation.)

Q3. What is the accuracy if 19 of 20 orders are correct?

Model answer: 19 divided by 20 is 95%. (Covered in module 3: Pick and pack. To pass that module: Your accuracy is at least 95%, you recorded the time of each order and you can say what caused each error.)

Q4. Why use a daily cutoff?

Model answer: It makes delivery promises reliable. (Covered in module 4: Ship and track. To pass that module: Your log shows every order dispatched on its promised day and every customer message sent within an hour.)

Q5. Why have a clear returns policy?

Model answer: It reduces disputes because everyone knows the rules. (Covered in module 5: Returns. To pass that module: Every return has a recorded reason, all are processed within your stated time and you identify the most common reason.)

Q6. What is a good target for the first reply?

Model answer: Within 24 hours. (Covered in module 6: Customer service. To pass that module: Your templates are clear and polite, at least 90% of replies are within 24 hours and each reply includes the order number.)

Q7. What is the error rate if 2 of 95 orders have errors?

Model answer: 2 divided by 95 is about 2.1%. (Covered in module 7: 30 days. To pass that module: Your log covers all 30 days, the weekly error rates are correct and each error has a cause.)

Q8. What do you do first in a review?

Model answer: Find the top cause of errors in the data. (Covered in module 8: Review. To pass that module: Your plan targets the largest cause with a fix, a numeric target and a date.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Run order handling for a small online shop for 30 days and report accuracy, speed, returns and service replies, with a plan to cut the top error cause. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Stock system; order log; accuracy and speed numbers; returns handled; service replies; at least 70% on the final check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

  4. Slide 4: L4 Practitioner, Run the full task alone (this course). Speaker note: Task: Run order handling for a small online shop for 30 days and report accuracy, speed, returns and service replies, with a plan to cut the top error cause.   5. Slide 5: L5 Specialist, One narrow area to depth. Speaker note: Learner picks one area, for example 30 days (the longest module at 40 h), and goes deeper than this course asks. Learner then teaches a peer circle of three or four. Note for the guide: check the teaching, not only the work.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Advanced, Lead a team and review evidence. Speaker note: Learner leads a small team or designs a service in this field and marks other people's evidence against the rubric. Roles this points to: Order processor; Warehouse assistant; Customer service agent. Note for the guide: sample three records from the team and compare marks.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Super expert, Set and audit the standard. Speaker note: Learner writes the method for E-commerce Operations as a short guide, audits other learners' evidence, and publishes worked examples back to learners. Note for the guide: worked examples keep currency and units on every number and are checked by a second expert.
