Industrial Activity: Light Manufacturing

Plan, run and measure a small safe production batch, from drawing to checked product.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 150 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Plan, run and measure a small safe production batch, from drawing to checked product.

  • Understand Explain flow from material to checked product.
  • Apply Run a small batch safely.
  • Analyse Analyse defects and time.
  • Create Design a better layout or step.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and run one small production batch with a safety plan, quality checks and a cost record.

Condition

About 150 hours over 3 to 6 months. Access to a supervised workshop, basic tools, free spreadsheet, a qualified supervisor, mentor and peer circle.

Standard

Safety plan approved; batch records; quality results; cost sheet; improvement plan; at least 70% on the check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (150 h guided)

Module 1 Flow of work 10 hUnderstand

Every product follows a path from material to finished item. Waiting and carrying often take more time than working. In this module you draw that path and find the slowest step.

  • Draw each step from material in to product out, using boxes for work and arrows for movement.
  • Time each step with a clock on three different parts and use the average.
  • Mark steps that add no value, such as waiting, carrying, searching for tools and rework.
  • Name the slowest step, since the slowest step limits how many parts you can make in a day.
  • A common mistake is to speed up a fast step, which only builds a pile of parts before the slow step.
Worked example. A part takes 120 minutes from start to finish, of which only 45 minutes is real work and 75 minutes is waiting and carrying. Value-adding time is 45 / 120 = 37.5%, and cutting a 40-minute wait for a tool is the best first change.

Activity. Draw a flow map of your batch process with times for each step, and mark waiting and carrying steps.

Standard to meet. Show that all steps are timed, non-value steps are marked, and the slowest step is named with its time.

Module 2 Safety first 16 hApply

No batch starts without a safety plan that a qualified supervisor has signed. Safety rules differ by place, so you check with your local authority. Habits such as protection and tidy work areas prevent most injuries.

  • List every hazard in the batch, such as moving parts, sharp edges, dust, noise, heat, lifting and electricity.
  • Write a control for each hazard, such as a guard, a lock-out step, ear protection or a lifting limit.
  • Wear the protection the plan requires, tie back hair and loose clothing, and never wear jewellery near machines.
  • Learn where the emergency stop, first-aid kit and exits are before you begin.
  • Do not start work until a qualified supervisor has read and signed the plan, and check with your local authority for rules that apply.
Worked example. Your plan lists 6 hazards for a saw station and each has a control, for example a fixed guard, eye protection and a rule to switch off before clearing offcuts. The supervisor signs and dates the plan before the first cut.

Activity. Write a safety plan with hazards, controls and protection for your batch and obtain a supervisor's signature.

Standard to meet. Show that each hazard has a control, emergency points are marked and the plan is signed and dated.

Module 3 Standards and drawings 16 hApply

Everyone must make the same part the same way. A drawing shows size and tolerance, and a gauge or tool checks it. You learn to read a simple drawing and measure with care.

  • Read the title, scale, units and each dimension on the drawing before you cut any material.
  • Tolerance is the allowed difference from the target size, written as plus or minus a number.
  • Choose the right tool for the tolerance, such as a steel rule for 1 mm and a calliper for 0.1 mm.
  • Measure at the same point on every part and write the result in a table.
  • Mark parts outside tolerance and set them aside, never mix them with good parts.
Worked example. The drawing says 100 mm plus or minus 0.5, so a good part measures between 99.5 and 100.5 mm. A part measuring 100.3 mm passes, and a part measuring 100.7 mm fails.

Activity. Measure five parts against a drawing and record each size, the allowed range and pass or fail.

Standard to meet. Show that all five measurements are in a table with the range, each pass or fail is correct, and failed parts are set aside.

Module 4 Plan the batch 16 hCreate

A good plan lists materials, time and people before you start. It also includes an allowance for waste. You will build a bill of materials and a time plan for 50 parts.

  • List every material with quantity per part and for the whole batch, with units such as kilograms or metres.
  • Add a scrap allowance, usually 5 to 10 percent, because cutting and testing always lose some material.
  • Time each step per part and multiply by the batch size to find total hours.
  • Check stock and order missing material, noting the lead time so that you do not stop mid-batch.
  • Write who does each step and what tools they need.
Worked example. Fifty parts need 12 kg of material, and a 10% allowance adds 1.2 kg, so you order 13.2 kg. Cutting 4 minutes, drilling 3 and sanding 2 gives 9 minutes per part, and 50 x 9 = 450 minutes, which is 7.5 hours.

Activity. Write a batch plan with a bill of materials, a scrap allowance, times per step and a list of tools and people.

Standard to meet. Show that quantities include scrap, total time is calculated correctly, and stock has been checked.

Module 5 Run the batch 40 hApply

Running a batch is where the plan meets reality. You log time and defects as you go and stop when the pattern changes. Tidy work keeps both quality and safety high.

  • Log the start and end time for each step and the number of parts finished every hour.
  • Check the first part fully before running the rest, and check every fifth part afterwards.
  • Write each defect with its type and the time it happened.
  • Stop and investigate if two defects of the same type happen close together, instead of continuing and wasting material.
  • Clean the area at the end of every shift and return tools to their place.
Worked example. In a batch of 50 parts, 4 are defective, so the defect rate is 4 / 50 = 8%. Two defects of the same type appear in parts 27 and 28, so you stop and find that the blade is worn. Parts 29 and 30, already cut, are also defective. You replace the blade and the next 20 parts (31 to 50) have no defects.

Activity. Run the batch with a time log, a defect log and a record of each stop and what you changed.

Standard to meet. Show that the log covers every step, defects are counted by type and each stop has a cause and action.

Module 6 Quality and defects 20 hAnalyse

Quality work finds causes instead of blame. You count defects by type, find the biggest group and trace it to the root. Then you fix the cause at its source.

  • Count defects by type in a table and calculate each type as a percentage of all defects.
  • Sort the table from largest to smallest and work on the largest first.
  • Ask why five times: keep asking why the problem happened until you reach a cause you can change.
  • Fix the cause, for example replace a tool or change a method, rather than only fixing the part.
  • Check again after the fix and record whether the defect count fell.
Worked example. Of 10 defects, 6 are burrs, 3 are wrong size and 1 is a scratch, so burrs are 6 / 10 = 60%. Asking why five times leads from burrs to a blunt blade to no sharpening schedule, so you create a schedule.

Activity. Chart the defects by type, find the root cause of the biggest group and write the corrective action.

Standard to meet. Show that the chart shows counts and percentages, the root cause is reached by repeated why questions and an action has an owner.

Module 7 Cost per part 12 hEvaluate

Cost per part decides your price and your profit. It includes material, labour, energy and waste. You count only good parts, because bad parts add cost without value.

  • List material cost with the scrap included, labour at a fair hourly rate, energy and tool wear.
  • Add up the total batch cost and divide it by the number of good parts, not by the number started.
  • Compare your cost per part with a price quote for the same item to see if you are competitive.
  • Find which line is biggest and think about how to reduce it.
  • Put a currency on every line and ask your mentor to check the sheet.
Worked example. Material is 13.2 kg x EUR 4 = EUR 52.80, labour is 7.5 hours x EUR 15 = EUR 112.50, and energy is EUR 6.00, so the total is EUR 171.30. With 46 good parts, cost per part is 171.30 / 46 = EUR 3.72.

Activity. Build a cost sheet for the batch and calculate cost per good part.

Standard to meet. Show that all lines have currency, the total is correct, the division uses good parts and the mentor has checked it.

Module 8 Improve 20 hEvaluate

Improvement means one small change that you measure. You choose the slowest or most wasteful step, trial a change and compare the numbers. Changing one thing at a time shows what really worked.

  • Pick the step that limits output or causes the most defects, using your flow map and defect chart.
  • Write the change you will trial, the number you expect and how you will measure it.
  • Change one thing only, such as a jig, a tool position or a work order, and run a small test.
  • Measure the same number before and after, and calculate the change in percent.
  • If it works, write it into the standard method, and if not, record why and try another idea.
Worked example. Setup time was 20 minutes and a simple jig cut it to 14 minutes, a saving of 6 minutes. That is 6 / 20 = 30% less, and over 10 setups a week it saves 60 minutes.

Activity. Present one improvement with the problem, the change, and numbers before and after.

Standard to meet. Show that the numbers before and after are measured the same way, the percent change is correct and only one change was made.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a waiting step and why does the slowest step matter?

Model answer: A waiting step is time when no work is done on the part. The slowest step sets the pace of the whole line. (Covered in module 1: Flow of work. To pass that module: All steps are timed, non-value steps are marked, and the slowest step is named with its time.)

Q2. Who must sign off the safety plan and when?

Model answer: A qualified supervisor signs it before any work on the batch starts. (Covered in module 2: Safety first. To pass that module: Each hazard has a control, emergency points are marked and the plan is signed and dated.)

Q3. What is tolerance and what range does 100 mm plus or minus 0.5 allow?

Model answer: Tolerance is the allowed difference from the target size. The range is 99.5 mm to 100.5 mm. (Covered in module 3: Standards and drawings. To pass that module: All five measurements are in a table with the range, each pass or fail is correct, and failed parts are set aside.)

Q4. Why add a scrap allowance?

Model answer: Some material is always lost in cutting, setting up and testing, so ordering exactly the needed amount leaves you short. (Covered in module 4: Plan the batch. To pass that module: Quantities include scrap, total time is calculated correctly, and stock has been checked.)

Q5. When should you stop a batch?

Model answer: Stop when defects of the same type begin to repeat, then find and fix the cause before you continue. (Covered in module 5: Run the batch. To pass that module: The log covers every step, defects are counted by type and each stop has a cause and action.)

Q6. What does ask why five times mean?

Model answer: It means asking why again and again until you reach a root cause you can fix, instead of stopping at the first symptom. (Covered in module 6: Quality and defects. To pass that module: The chart shows counts and percentages, the root cause is reached by repeated why questions and an action has an owner.)

Q7. Why divide by good parts and not by parts started?

Model answer: Defective parts cost money but cannot be sold, so their cost must be spread over the good parts. (Covered in module 7: Cost per part. To pass that module: All lines have currency, the total is correct, the division uses good parts and the mentor has checked it.)

Q8. Why change only one thing at a time?

Model answer: So you know which change caused the result, and you can repeat it with confidence. (Covered in module 8: Improve. To pass that module: The numbers before and after are measured the same way, the percent change is correct and only one change was made.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Plan and run one small production batch with a safety plan, quality checks and a cost record. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Safety plan approved; batch records; quality results; cost sheet; improvement plan; at least 70% on the check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Ladder L1 to L7, slide by slide with notes

Use module 1, Flow of work, as the first short unit (about 2 to 6 hours). Say this rule aloud: Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money. No record is needed at this level.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Awareness, What the field is and who it serves. Speaker note: Learner explains the idea in plain words: Explain flow from material to checked product. Shared with schools and community groups. Note for the guide: ask one learner to explain it to a neighbour and check the words are right.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Foundation, Guided tasks with a checklist. Speaker note: Learner does guided tasks from the first three modules (Flow of work; Safety first; Standards and drawings) with a checklist. Evidence is a small set of dated records. Note for the guide: sit beside the learner for the first record, then step back.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Practitioner, Run the full task alone (this course). Speaker note: Task: Plan and run one small production batch with a safety plan, quality checks and a cost record. Standard: Safety plan approved; batch records; quality results; cost sheet; improvement plan; at least 70% on the check. Note for the guide: a mentor reviews the records; two peers mark with the rubric above.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Specialist, One narrow area to depth. Speaker note: Learner picks one area, for example Run the batch (the longest module at 40 h), and goes deeper than this course asks. Learner then teaches a peer circle of three or four. Note for the guide: check the teaching, not only the work.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Advanced, Lead a team and review evidence. Speaker note: Learner leads a small team or designs a service in this field and marks other people's evidence against the rubric. Roles this points to: Production operator; Quality checker; Workshop assistant. Note for the guide: sample three records from the team and compare marks.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Super expert, Set and audit the standard. Speaker note: Learner writes the method for Industrial Activity: Light Manufacturing as a short guide, audits other learners' evidence, and publishes worked examples back to learners. Note for the guide: worked examples keep currency and units on every number and are checked by a second expert.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
