Wiring inside a switchboard

Cables inside a panel sit in warmer, tighter spaces than cables in a room. This lesson covers how to size and fit them.

Learning material, not a design or safety instruction. Electrical work must be done by qualified people following the maker's instructions and local rules.

Size for the hottest place

  • Inside a closed panel, the air can be well above room temperature. Planning rules often assume about 35 °C for the panel and as much as 55 °C where the wiring runs.
  • Choose the cable cross-section for the hotter figure, not the room.
  • If the room is warmer than the rule assumes, reduce the allowed heat loss to stay inside the limit.

Pick the right cable

Use heat-resistant insulation rated for high conductor temperatures. Halogen-free types give less smoke if there is a fire.

Thin conductors are usually laid in ducts and bundles. Heavier ones are laid in air, in small bundles.

Protect against two different problems

  • Overload is a slow rise in current. The protection must match the cable's current rating.
  • Short circuit is a sudden, very large current. The cable must survive until the protective device clears it, so check how long that takes.
  • Where a device limits the fault current, the section between busbar and device can be sized for the smaller load.

Hold short wiring firmly

In a short circuit, conductors push apart with great force. Keep short runs tight, use cable supports, or run them in plastic ducts.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

  • Task. Choose and fit internal wiring for a switchboard so it survives heat, overload and short circuits.
  • Condition. Given the room temperature, the temperature allowance inside the panel, load current and the maker's cable and protection data, with design and any work done only by qualified people following local rules.
  • Standard. The learner sizes wiring for the hotter inside temperature, names the two protection needs, and says how short runs are held against fault forces.

four parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1: Size for the hottest place (15 min, Apply: Calculate)

Air inside a closed panel is warmer than the room, and wiring often sits in the warmest zone. Cable ratings must be reduced for heat.

  • Planning rules often assume about 35 °C for the panel air and as much as 55 °C where wiring runs.
  • Choose cable size for the hotter figure, not the room.
  • If the room is warmer than the rule assumes, lower the allowed heat loss to stay inside the limit.

Worked example. A cable is rated 30 A at 30 °C. At 55 °C a teaching factor of 0.8 applies, so the usable rating is 30 × 0.8 = 24 A. The load is 26 A. Since 26 is more than 24, the cable fails. Step up to a cable rated 40 A at 30 °C: 40 × 0.8 = 32 A, which is above 26, so it passes with a margin of 6 A. The factor 0.8 is for teaching, and real factors come from the maker's tables.

Activity. A load is 18 A. Cable options are rated 20 A, 25 A and 32 A at 30 °C. Use a factor of 0.8 and say which is the smallest that passes.

Standard. Learner derates the rating for heat and picks the smallest passing cable.

Part 2: Pick the right cable (15 min, Understand: Select)

Not every cable suits a hot, tight space. Insulation type and how cables are bundled both matter.

  • Use heat-resistant insulation rated for high conductor temperatures.
  • Halogen-free types give less smoke if there is a fire.
  • Thin conductors are usually laid in ducts and bundles, heavier ones in air in small bundles.

Worked example. A panel has 12 control wires, each carrying 1 A, packed in one duct. A second run has 2 heavy cables carrying 60 A each. In the duct, heat from 12 wires builds up, so each wire loses some rating, which is acceptable because 1 A is small against the wire's rating. The two heavy cables are laid apart in air, each with room around it, so each loses less rating. Putting the 2 heavy cables into the crowded duct as well would raise the heat and risk the insulation. Total of 14 conductors in the duct would be unwise for this mix.

Activity. List four wires in a panel, two thin control wires and two heavy power cables. Decide where each goes (duct, air, bundle) and why.

Standard. Learner matches thin wires to ducts and heavy cables to open air, and names insulation needs.

Part 3: Two different problems (15 min, Analyse: Distinguish)

Overload and short circuit are different faults needing different checks. Wiring must survive both until protection acts.

  • Overload is a slow rise in current, so protection must match the cable's current rating.
  • Short circuit is a sudden, very large current, so the cable must survive until the device clears it.
  • Where a device limits fault current, the section between busbar and device can be sized for the smaller load.

Worked example. A cable has a derated rating of 24 A and a protective device rated 20 A. Because 20 is below 24, the device protects the cable from overload. A device rated 32 A would not, since 32 is above 24. For a short circuit, the maker's table says this cable can carry a fault for 0.1 s at the available fault level. The device clears in 0.01 s, which is 10 times faster than the limit, so the cable passes. If the device took 0.5 s instead, it would fail. Figures are examples only.

Activity. A cable derates to 36 A. Devices of 32 A and 40 A are offered. Say which protects against overload. Then say what extra check short circuit needs.

Standard. Learner picks the device that protects against overload and states the clearing time check.

Part 4: Hold short wiring firmly (15 min, Evaluate: Judge)

In a short circuit, conductors push apart with great force. Short runs are especially at risk because they sit close and carry the full fault.

  • Keep short runs tight and supported at the spacing the maker gives.
  • Use cable supports or run them in plastic ducts.
  • Never leave loose loops or unsupported lengths near busbars.

Worked example. A run of 0.6 m between a busbar and a device has one support at the middle only. The maker asks for a support every 150 mm. At 0.6 m, that means 600 ÷ 150 = 4 sections, so about 3 supports along the run plus the ends. With only one support, each half of 300 mm is twice the allowed gap of 150 mm. The run is under-supported and fails the check. Add supports until no gap exceeds the maker's figure. Spacing here is an example.

Activity. A 450 mm run needs a support every 150 mm. Work out how many gaps and supports, and compare with a layout that has one support in the middle.

Standard. Learner calculates the supports needed and judges whether a layout is acceptable.

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: A hot box. Cutaway of a closed panel with a thermometer reading much higher than the room. Speaker note: Start by asking where it is hottest in a panel. Introduce that wiring sits in the warm zone.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Explain: Derating. A sum 30 × 0.8 = 24 A beside a cable and a thermometer. Speaker note: Explain why rating drops with heat. Say the 0.8 factor is for teaching and the maker tables are the source.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Apply: Pick a size. Load 26 A, three cables rated 25, 30 and 40 A at 30 °C. Speaker note: Learners derate each and pick the smallest that passes. Answer is the 40 A cable at 32 A usable.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Analyse: Two faults. Two columns: overload slow rise, short circuit sudden surge, with clearing time arrows. Speaker note: Draw out the difference. Use the 20 A device and 24 A cable, then the 0.01 s against 0.1 s check.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Design: Lay it out. Panel sketch with a duct, an open-air zone, a busbar and a short run to a device. Speaker note: Learners assign each wire to a place and mark supports. Remind them all design and fitting belongs to qualified people following local rules.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Evaluate: Find the faults. A layout with an undersized cable, a crowded duct and one support on a 0.6 m run. Speaker note: Learners list the faults and rank them. Compare their lists in pairs.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Create: Wiring plan. Blank plan: loads, temperature, cable size, protection, supports, review. Speaker note: Each learner makes a plan for an invented small panel. Collect and discuss one. Close with the safety message again.

where it leads

Jobs this supports

  • Panel wireman. Cuts, routes and fits internal wiring to the approved design.
  • Design checker. Reviews wiring sizes and protection against the design rules before build.
  • Test technician. Inspects and tests finished panels and records the results.
  • Quality inspector. Checks cable supports, routing and labelling against the build standard.
