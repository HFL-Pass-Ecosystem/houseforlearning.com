flagship course

Smart Apparel

Understand and plan wearable-sensor clothing: materials, sensors, washing, data privacy and a small product pilot.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner28:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain how sensors are built into fabric.
  • Apply Plan a prototype for one use.
  • Analyse Test comfort, washing and accuracy.
  • Evaluate Judge privacy, safety and cost.
  • Create Write a pilot plan.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and test a small smart-garment prototype (or a bought kit), with use case, materials list, washing test, data privacy plan and cost.

Condition

Basic electronics kit or a purchased kit, fabric, a computer, two peers and a mentor. Safe low voltage only.

Standard

Use case defined; washing test repeated and logged; accuracy compared to a reference; privacy plan written; low-voltage safety rules followed; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Fashion students, makers, sports teams, textile staff. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What smart apparel is, and who needs it 3 hUnderstand

Smart apparel is clothing that senses, records or responds, usually through small sensors and a removable electronic module. The best products solve a clear problem for a clear wearer; the worst add gadgets nobody asked for.

  • Common signals: heart rate, breathing, body temperature, movement, posture, pressure and sweat. Each needs a different sensor and a different place on the body.
  • Use cases: sport and training, elder and patient care, worker safety, baby monitoring, rehabilitation and fashion effects such as light-up garments.
  • Ask first: who wears it, for how long, doing what, in what weather, and who will read the data?
  • Smart does not replace medical devices; a garment that claims to diagnose may need regulatory approval in many places.
Worked example. A care home wants to know when a resident gets out of bed at night. A pressure pad in a night garment or a mat is a better answer than a heart-rate shirt. The need (alert staff within 30 seconds) drives the design, not the technology.

Activity. Write a user need statement for one wearer: who, where, what must be sensed, how often, who is alerted and how fast.

Standard to meet. The statement names the wearer, signal, accuracy needed, time limit and who sees the data.

Module 2 Fabrics, comfort and skin 3 hUnderstand

A garment that is uncomfortable will not be worn, and an unworn garment gives no data. Fabric choice affects both sensor contact and comfort.

  • Breathable, stretchy fabrics such as cotton-elastane blends or knitted polyester keep sensors snug without chafing.
  • Dry electrodes need skin contact; loose fabric loses signal, and very tight fabric is uncomfortable. Aim for firm but not tight.
  • Conductive yarns, often silver-coated, carry signals; metallic coatings can wear off in washing, and some people are sensitive to metals.
  • Seams over sensors cause hot spots; place seams away from contact areas.
Worked example. Two chest-band prototypes are tested. The band with flat seams and a wider elastic gives a steadier signal and fewer red marks after a one-hour walk than the narrow band with a raised seam.

Activity. Test two fabrics for comfort and signal on two volunteers who consent, and record scores from 1 to 5.

Standard to meet. Results table has both fabrics, two users, comfort and signal scores and one design decision.

Module 3 Sensors, power and electronics 4 hApply

Electronics in clothing must be small, low voltage, protected against sweat and easy to remove.

  • Start from a ready sensor board for heart rate, movement or temperature; sewing the conductive parts can come later.
  • Use a small rechargeable cell with a protection circuit and charge only with the right charger. Never use a damaged cell next to skin.
  • Battery life = capacity (mAh) / average current (mA). A 400 mAh cell with a 20 mA module lasts about 20 hours.
  • Keep wires short, use strain relief and seal connectors with flexible sealant.
Worked example. A 400 mAh module draws 20 mA when streaming (20 hours) and about 5 mA when storing data. Storing and sending a short burst every 10 minutes averages about 6.7 mA, which extends battery life to about 60 hours (400 / 6.7).

Activity. Draw a block diagram (sensor, processor, radio, battery, charger) and calculate battery life for two data modes.

Standard to meet. Diagram is complete and the two battery-life figures are correct.

Module 4 Building a prototype 4 hApply

Prototype quickly with a kit before investing in special fabrics.

  • Bench test the sensor first, then test it on a person, then attach it to a garment.
  • Attach the module in a pocket or with snaps so it can be removed for washing.
  • Test continuity of every conductive path before closing seams.
  • Record every change, photograph the layout and keep the version number on the garment.
Worked example. The first sleeve prototype loses signal when the elbow bends because the wire crosses the joint. Moving the wire along the side seam with a loose loop fixes the break.

Activity. Build or plan a prototype with removable module and test it on two users.

Standard to meet. Signal remains stable during walking, arm movement and sitting for five minutes each.

Module 5 Washing, wear and durability 4 hAnalyse

Washing is the harshest test a smart garment faces.

  • Test cycles: gentle cold wash, air dry. Count cycles to failure.
  • Detach modules, close zips and use a wash bag.
  • Inspect connections, coatings and fabric after every five washes.
  • Decide a minimum life such as 25 washes and design to that.
Worked example. Three prototypes are washed. The first fails at 8 washes (corroded thread), the second at 15 (cracked sealant), the third passes 25 after a flexible silicone seal is added.

Activity. Wash-test a sample and record the number of cycles to failure and the failure cause.

Standard to meet. Log shows cycles, faults and one improvement tested.

Module 6 Accuracy and honest claims 3 hAnalyse

A sensor is only useful if you know how wrong it can be.

  • Compare against a trusted reference such as a medical-grade pulse meter or a manual count.
  • Test at rest, walking and running; accuracy usually falls with movement.
  • Report average error and worst-case error, not only the best result.
  • Make claims no stronger than the evidence.
Worked example. Heart rate from the shirt differs from a reference by 6 per cent on average at rest and 12 per cent while running, with worst case 20 per cent. The product description says "wellness tracking, not a medical device".

Activity. Compare your prototype with a reference and calculate average and worst-case percent error.

Standard to meet. Both figures are calculated and the claim wording matches the evidence.

Module 7 Privacy, safety and rules 4 hEvaluate

The body is the most personal data source, so privacy and safety come before features.

  • Collect only what is needed, explain it in plain words and let the wearer delete it.
  • Store data on the phone where possible; encrypt anything that leaves the device.
  • Check electrical safety, skin safety and local rules for wearable electronics and textile labelling.
  • Do not share data with third parties without clear consent.
Worked example. A consent screen lists: what is recorded (heart rate every minute), where it is kept (the phone only), who can see it (the wearer) and how to delete it.

Activity. Draft a one-screen consent text and a safety checklist for your garment.

Standard to meet. Consent text is readable by a non-expert and the checklist has at least eight items.

Module 8 Pilot, cost and plan 3 hCreate

A small pilot shows whether anyone wants and can use the garment.

  • Recruit about ten users with consent and a clear task.
  • Measure wear time, comfort, washing results and satisfaction.
  • Cost per garment = fabric + module share + labour + packaging + warranty reserve.
  • Decide: improve, change use, or stop.
Worked example. The pilot garment costs 780 INR to make and is priced at 1,600 INR (estimates; check your market). Users wear it on 8 out of 10 days; two report the collar irritates, so the module moves to the side seam.

Activity. Write a pilot plan with users, measures, cost and decision rule.

Standard to meet. Plan names ten users, a cost per unit and a go/stop rule.

ten locations and global

Your place

Product-safety, textile labelling and health-data rules differ by place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.

Global

The global version uses plain English, metric units, and your own currency. Machine translation is available in every language; the English text is the official text. Before you act for real, check your local rules with an adviser. Currency: USD (or your own currency, written next to every number).
