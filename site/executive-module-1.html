executive programme

Module 1: Global exposure audit and distance diagnostics

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 1

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 16 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Profile memo 10%
  • Wargame participation 10%
  • Midterm case 20%
  • Governance quiz 20%
  • Market-entry memo 40%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Audit a firm's exposure in each foreign market and rank the markets by how far they sit from home.

Condition

Given a firm's sales, cost and supplier figures for at least four countries and a set of public country data.

Standard

Every share, score and loss figure is calculated correctly, and the ranking is backed by written reasons.

Who it is for: Senior managers and directors who must decide where a firm should grow, hold or pull back abroad.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 Mapping revenue and cost exposure by country 9 hRemember and apply: list and calculate

Exposure means how much of the firm depends on each country. We start by turning raw figures into shares.

  • Exposure = share of sales, costs, assets and staff
  • Add the shares up to check they reach 100%
  • A concentration score shows how spread out the firm is

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency: a firm sells 200m a year: home 120m, country B 40m, country C 24m, country D 16m. Shares are 60%, 20%, 12% and 8%. Concentration score = 0.60 squared + 0.20 squared + 0.12 squared + 0.08 squared = 0.36 + 0.04 + 0.0144 + 0.0064 = 0.4208. A score of 1.0 means one country only. A score of 0.25 means four equal countries. At 0.42 the firm leans heavily on home.

Activity: Using a table of sales, costs and staff for five countries, calculate the share of each and the concentration score for sales and for costs.

Standard: All shares add to 100% within 0.5 points and both scores are correct to two decimals.

Part 2 Measuring distance between two markets 10 hUnderstand and apply: explain and score

Distance is more than kilometres. It has cultural, rule-based, physical and economic parts.

  • Four distance types: cultural, administrative, geographic, economic
  • Score each type 1 (close) to 5 (far)
  • Weight scores by the firm's product

Worked example: A food maker compares home with a target market. Scores: cultural 4, administrative 2, geographic 3, economic 5. Fresh food depends on shipping, so weights are cultural 20%, administrative 20%, geographic 40%, economic 20%. Weighted score = 0.2x4 + 0.2x2 + 0.4x3 + 0.2x5 = 0.8 + 0.4 + 1.2 + 1.0 = 3.4 out of 5. A plain sum of 14 would hide that shipping matters most.

Activity: Score two target markets on all four distance types for a product of your choice, choose weights, and state which market is nearer.

Standard: Eight scores each have a one-line reason, weights add to 100%, and the weighted totals are correct.

Part 3 Currency exposure and hedge ratios 9 hApply: calculate

Money earned in one currency and spent in another can move profit without any change in the business. We measure the gap and decide how much to cover.

  • Net exposure = receipts minus payments in that currency
  • A move of x% changes profit by x% of net exposure
  • A hedge ratio is the share of exposure covered

Worked example: A plant has euro costs of 30m and euro sales of 10m. Net short position = 30m - 10m = 20m euro. If the euro rises 10%, cost rises 2m. The board sets a 60% hedge: 0.6 x 20m = 12m covered. Open position = 8m, so the same 10% move now costs 0.8m instead of 2m.

Activity: Given receipts and payments in three currencies, work out net exposure in each and the open loss from a 10% move at a 50% hedge.

Standard: Net positions and open losses are correct for all three currencies.

Part 4 Political and legal risk scoring 9 hAnalyse: compare and rank

Some risks come from rules and governments, not from the market. We turn them into scores that can be compared.

  • List risks: tax change, expropriation, sanctions, contract enforcement
  • Give each a chance and an impact
  • Risk score = chance x impact

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. For country C: tax rise, chance 40%, impact 3m a year, so 1.2m. Contract dispute, chance 25%, impact 8m, so 2.0m. Sanctions, chance 5%, impact 40m, so 2.0m. Total expected loss = 1.2 + 2.0 + 2.0 = 5.2m. The rare sanction risk is as large as the common dispute risk, so it needs its own plan.

Activity: Build a risk table of five risks for one country with chances and impacts and rank them by expected loss.

Standard: Five risks, correct products, a correct total and a ranked list with one sentence on the top item.

Part 5 Supply-chain concentration 9 hAnalyse: test and quantify

A firm can look spread out on sales but depend on one supplier country. We test how much a failure would cost.

  • List suppliers by share of inputs
  • Estimate weeks of outage
  • Lost output = share x outage weeks / 52 x annual output

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Annual output is 130m. Supplier shares: country X 55%, Y 25%, Z 20%. If X fails for 6 weeks and nothing can replace it: 130m x 0.55 x 6/52 = 8.25m of lost output. If a backup supplier can cover half of X, loss = 4.13m. Paying 1.0m a year to keep the backup is worth it if such a failure is expected more often than once in four years.

Activity: For a supplier table you are given, calculate the loss from the loss of each country for 4 weeks and name the highest.

Standard: All three loss figures correct and the largest correctly identified.

Part 6 Comparing market-entry options 9 hEvaluate: judge and choose

Selling by export, by licence or through a local company has different cost, control and risk. We compare them on the same numbers.

  • Compare setup cost, yearly profit and risk
  • Use a simple payback in years
  • Match the option to the distance scores

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Export: setup 1m, profit 0.5m a year, payback 2 years. Licence: setup 0.2m, profit 0.2m a year, payback 1 year, but little control. Local company: setup 6m, profit 1.8m a year, payback 6/1.8 = 3.3 years. With a high cultural score of 4 the licence is safer; with a 3-year time limit the local company fails the test.

Activity: Compare three entry options for the market you scored in module 2 and choose one with reasons.

Standard: Three paybacks are correct and the choice agrees with the stated time limit.

Part 7 Writing the exposure profile memo 10 hCreate: write

The profile memo pulls the audit into one page a board can act on.

  • Lead with the answer
  • Show three numbers that matter
  • End with a recommended limit for each country

Worked example: Memo opening (worked-example figures, in your currency): 'We recommend capping country C at 15% of sales.' Evidence: C now 22% of sales (44m of 200m), expected political loss 5.2m, weighted distance score 3.4 (taken from module 2 for this example). Working: 15% of 200m = 30m, so 14m of sales growth should go elsewhere. A board can say yes or no to this in one minute.

Activity: Write a one-page memo using your own results from modules 1 to 6, with one recommended limit.

Standard: One page, answer first, at least three correct numbers from earlier work, one limit with its working.

Part 8 Governance checks and the exposure wargame 10 hEvaluate: defend a position

Limits only work if someone checks them. We set the checks and then test them in a role-play where a shock hits.

  • Set a limit, an owner and a review date
  • Define a trigger that forces action
  • Run a shock and revise the plan

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Rule: no country over 25% of sales or 40% of any input. A shock cuts country C sales by 30%: 44m x 0.30 = 13.2m lost, which is 6.6% of total sales. The firm stays within its profit floor if costs fall 4m. The team checks whether the plan names who cuts which 4m.

Activity: In a group role-play, one team applies a shock and the others adjust limits and answer questions for 15 minutes.

Standard: The revised plan names an owner and date for each action and the loss figure is correct.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. Four countries give a firm 60%, 20%, 12% and 8% of sales. What is the concentration score?
    0.36 + 0.04 + 0.0144 + 0.0064 = 0.4208. (Covered in part 1: Mapping revenue and cost exposure by country. To pass that part: All shares add to 100% within 0.5 points and both scores are correct to two decimals.)
  2. Why can a plain sum of distance scores mislead?
    It treats all distance types as equally important, but a product may depend mostly on one, such as shipping for fresh goods. (Covered in part 2: Measuring distance between two markets. To pass that part: Eight scores each have a one-line reason, weights add to 100%, and the weighted totals are correct.)
  3. Cultural 4, administrative 2, geographic 3, economic 5 with weights 20, 20, 40, 20. What is the weighted score?
    0.8 + 0.4 + 1.2 + 1.0 = 3.4. (Covered in part 2: Measuring distance between two markets. To pass that part: Eight scores each have a one-line reason, weights add to 100%, and the weighted totals are correct.)
  4. Costs of 30m euro and sales of 10m euro. What is the net exposure and what does a 10% rise in the euro do?
    Net short 20m euro; cost rises by 2m. (Covered in part 3: Currency exposure and hedge ratios. To pass that part: Net positions and open losses are correct for all three currencies.)
  5. With a 60% hedge on a 20m exposure, how much is still open?
    8m, because 20m x 0.4 = 8m. (Covered in part 3: Currency exposure and hedge ratios. To pass that part: Net positions and open losses are correct for all three currencies.)
  6. A risk has a 25% chance and an 8m impact. What is its expected loss?
    0.25 x 8m = 2.0m. (Covered in part 4: Political and legal risk scoring. To pass that part: Five risks, correct products, a correct total and a ranked list with one sentence on the top item.)
  7. Why does a 5% chance of a 40m loss need its own plan even though it is rare?
    Its expected loss is 2.0m, the same as a common risk, and the actual hit would be very large. (Covered in part 4: Political and legal risk scoring. To pass that part: Five risks, correct products, a correct total and a ranked list with one sentence on the top item.)
  8. Output is 130m and a 55% supplier fails for 6 weeks. What output is lost?
    130m x 0.55 x 6/52 = about 8.25m. (Covered in part 5: Supply-chain concentration. To pass that part: All three loss figures correct and the largest correctly identified.)
  9. An option costs 6m and earns 1.8m a year. What is its payback?
    6 / 1.8 = about 3.3 years. (Covered in part 6: Comparing market-entry options. To pass that part: Three paybacks are correct and the choice agrees with the stated time limit.)
  10. Country C is 44m of 200m sales and the cap is 15%. How much sales must move elsewhere?
    The cap is 30m, so 14m must move. (Covered in part 7: Writing the exposure profile memo. To pass that part: One page, answer first, at least three correct numbers from earlier work, one limit with its working.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: What exposure means. Exposure is the share of the firm that depends on one country. It covers sales, costs, assets and staff. Speaker note: Say: think of the firm as a table with four legs. Check: ask who can name one leg. Mistake: counting only sales.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Concentration in numbers. Shares squared and added give a score from 0 to 1. Higher means more reliance on few countries. Speaker note: Say: do the sum slowly on the board. Check: learners compute a four-country case. Mistake: forgetting to square.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Four kinds of distance. Cultural, administrative, geographic and economic distance each get a 1 to 5 score. Speaker note: Say: give one example of each type. Check: score a familiar market. Mistake: scoring on opinion without a reason.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Weighting by product. The same market is near for one product and far for another. Weights show which distance matters. Speaker note: Say: compare software with fresh fish. Check: weights add to 100. Mistake: equal weights by default.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Money and risk in the numbers. Net currency exposure, expected loss and supplier loss turn worry into figures. Speaker note: Say: every risk needs a chance and an impact. Check: check one result by hand. Mistake: ignoring rare big risks.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Choosing an entry route. Payback, control and distance together decide between export, licence or local company. Speaker note: Say: no option wins on every measure. Check: ask what time limit applies. Mistake: choosing on profit alone.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Setting limits and testing them. A limit needs an owner, a trigger and a review. A shock exercise shows whether it works. Speaker note: Say: run the shock live. Check: is each action owned? Mistake: limits with no owner.

Marking rubric

jobs this leads to

Where this takes you

  • Head of international strategy. Decides which countries the firm should grow in and sets the limits.
  • Chief risk officer. Measures country, supplier and currency risks and reports them to the board.
  • Regional director. Runs a group of markets and keeps them inside the agreed limits.
  • Corporate development manager. Compares entry routes and prepares cases for new markets.
