Electronic Computer Manufacturing

Run a pilot of electronic Computer Manufacturing in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small electronic Computer Manufacturing offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know the parts of a computer and how it is made 6 hUnderstand

An electronic computer is made from boards, chips, memory, storage, power and a case. Learn the parts and the making steps.

  • Name the main parts: processor, memory, storage, mainboard, power supply, cooling and case.
  • Explain what each part does in one sentence.
  • Explain the steps from parts to product: buy parts, build boards, assemble, test, pack.
  • Explain the difference between designing a computer and assembling one.
  • Name the main safety risk to parts: static electricity.
  • Sort the parts of a computer into bought, made in-house and software.
  • Draw the path of one unit through a workshop from the parts store to shipping.
Worked example. A small maker assembles desktop computers from bought boards and parts. They do not make chips. If bought parts cost 600 CAD per unit, a batch of 10 units needs 10 x 600 = 6,000 CAD of parts. Their value is choosing parts, building neatly and testing well.

Activity. Draw a block diagram of a computer with ten blocks and write the job of each.

Standard to meet. Task: draw a computer block diagram. Condition: ten blocks. Standard: every block has an input, an output and a job, all correct.

Module 2 Work safely with electronics 6 hApply

Electronics work needs safe habits for people and for parts.

  • Use an anti-static wrist strap and mat when you handle boards and chips.
  • Keep a clean, lit bench and store parts in labelled bins.
  • Use eye protection when you cut, solder or use compressed air.
  • Handle batteries with care: do not crush, short or heat them.
  • Check the current rules with the workplace safety board on training, ventilation and protective gear.
  • Store and carry boards in anti-static bags.
  • Practise a bench-end routine: power off, unplug, tidy tools and note any hazard.
Worked example. A new worker touches a memory card without a strap, so the batch of 40 units is flagged for extra tests. The tests take 2 hours, which is 120 minutes / 40 = 3 minutes per unit. The company adds a strap check at the bench door.

Activity. Write a one-page bench safety checklist and use it for a week.

Standard to meet. Task: use a safety checklist. Condition: one week at a practice bench. Standard: each check is signed with a date and no unsafe step is skipped.

Module 3 Know the rules for making and selling electronics 10 hApply

Making electronics in Canada involves product, radio, safety and environment rules.

  • Explain that electrical and electronic products often need approval by a recognised testing body before sale; check the current rule with the federal and provincial authorities.
  • Explain that devices that send radio signals must meet the federal radio rules; check the current rule with the federal regulator.
  • Explain that electronic waste has take-back rules that differ by province; check the local rule.
  • Explain that labels may need to be in English and French; check what applies.
  • Explain that selling abroad may need export permits for some technology; check before you ship.
  • Explain that products with a battery may have extra transport and safety rules; check the carrier and federal rules.
  • Keep a compliance file for each model with test reports, dates and label proofs.
Worked example. A maker plans to sell a mini computer with built-in Wi-Fi. The checklist shows radio approval and electrical safety approval are needed first. If the two tests run one after the other and take 3 weeks and 4 weeks, sales wait 3 + 4 = 7 weeks.

Activity. Build a rules table with rule, who enforces it, your product's status and the dated source.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table. Condition: official pages only. Standard: each row has an enforcer, an action and a dated source; nothing is guessed.

Module 4 Assemble a board or a system step by step 8 hEvaluate

Assembly follows a written method so every unit is the same.

  • Write a work instruction with numbered steps, photos and a torque or fit note.
  • Lay out parts in order and check them against a parts list before you start.
  • Install parts gently and check seating, cable routes and screws.
  • Mark each unit with a serial number and record who built it and when.
  • Do the first article check: the first unit is tested fully before the batch continues.
  • Build a jig or tray so every worker places parts the same way.
  • Time each step and add the times to find the time per unit.
Worked example. A work instruction for a ten-step build is tested with a new worker. She stalls at step six, so the photo is changed and the step is split in two, which makes 11 steps. If each step takes 3 minutes, a unit takes 11 x 3 = 33 minutes.

Activity. Write a work instruction and assemble two units from it.

Standard to meet. Task: assemble two units. Condition: a written instruction. Standard: both units match, a second person can follow the steps and each unit has a record.

Module 5 Test, find faults and improve yield 8 hApply

Testing proves a unit works. Faults give the facts to improve the process.

  • Write a test plan: power on, boot, memory check, storage check, ports, heat, run time.
  • Run a burn-in: run the unit for a set time and watch for faults.
  • Log each fault with unit, test, cause and fix.
  • Compute yield = good units / started units.
  • Fix the top cause first and test again.
  • Explain first-pass yield and final yield and why rework can hide problems.
  • Keep a defect chart that ranks causes by count.
Worked example. Of 50 started units, 43 pass at first test. Yield = 43 / 50 = 86%. The 7 failures are 4 loose memory seats, 2 bad cables and 1 other. A new seating check is added and the next batch reaches 94%, which is 47 of 50.

Activity. Run a test plan on five units and compute yield and the top fault cause.

Standard to meet. Task: test and measure yield. Condition: five practice units. Standard: faults are logged with causes and the yield sum is correct and checked.

Module 6 Manage parts, suppliers and quality 6 hCreate

Missing or bad parts stop a line. Plan supply and check what comes in.

  • Check incoming parts against the order and inspect a sample.
  • Keep a supplier list with lead times and a second source for key parts.
  • Set a reorder point = daily use x lead time + safety stock.
  • Keep spares for the parts with long lead times.
  • Record batch numbers so a bad lot can be traced.
  • Take a sample such as 5 of every 100 parts on arrival, test them and record any bad ones with the batch number.
  • Explain that a part can go out of production; plan a last-time buy or a replacement.
Worked example. Daily use of a part is 6 and lead time is 14 days, with safety stock of 20. Reorder point = 6 x 14 + 20 = 104 units. When stock falls to 104 an order is placed, and the safety stock of 20 covers a late delivery.

Activity. Build a parts list with supplier, lead time and reorder point for ten parts.

Standard to meet. Task: plan parts supply. Condition: ten parts. Standard: reorder points are calculated correctly and a second source is named for the key parts.

Module 7 Price the product and prove it pays 6 hEvaluate

A computer maker earns by margin on each unit after paying for parts, time and testing.

  • Variable cost per unit = parts + labour + test + packing + shipping.
  • Margin = price - variable cost.
  • Fixed cost = rent, tools, insurance, software and approvals, paid whatever you sell.
  • Break-even volume = fixed cost / margin per unit.
  • Test the price with real buyers before you commit; talk to a licensed accountant about tax.
  • Include warranty returns as a cost in the cost sheet.
  • Compare your price with three similar products and write why a buyer would pick yours.
Worked example. Price 1,200 CAD, variable cost 780, so margin = 1,200 - 780 = 420. Fixed cost is 10,000 per month, so break-even = 10,000 / 420 = 23.8, which rounds up to 24 units a month. At 30 units the monthly profit is 30 x 420 - 10,000 = 2,600 CAD (teaching numbers only).

Activity. Build a cost sheet, compute break-even and write what you would change if you were 30% short.

Standard to meet. Task: price a computer. Condition: a spreadsheet. Standard: formulas are visible, three what-if cases are shown and a mentor checks the arithmetic.

Module 8 Run a small production batch for real 8 hCreate

Run a pilot batch and measure it. Then decide to stop, change or grow.

  • Set the batch size, schedule and a stop rule before you start.
  • Record yield, rework and time per unit each day.
  • Do a final check and pack with a care and warranty note; check the current consumer rules for warranties in your province.
  • Handle returns by reason and fix the top reason.
  • Write a 12-month plan with milestones, a budget and a risk list.
  • Write the stop rule as a number, for example yield below 80%.
  • Hold a short daily meeting to check yield, rework and parts.
Worked example. A pilot of 30 units reaches 90% yield, so 27 units pass, and takes 3 hours per unit. The stop rule was yield below 80%, which would be fewer than 24 good units. The decision is to run a second batch of 50.

Activity. Run a pilot batch of at least 10 units and write the review.

Standard to meet. Task: run a pilot. Condition: a written plan and stop rule. Standard: measures are recorded daily, the review reasons from evidence and a panel can repeat the plan.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
