Grade 11 Science: English

5 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5Post-secondary5 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Understand, Apply, Analyse
  • Analyse Analyse theme and voice in two texts.
  • Apply Edit text for tense, agreement and clarity.
  • Apply Write persuasive and informative pieces.
  • Understand Answer inference and evaluation questions.
  • Apply Deliver a structured speech and respond to questions.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 11 Science English, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 5 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

5 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Prose and poetry from world writers Weeks 1 to 9Analyse

In this unit, 'Prose and poetry from world writers', you learn to analyse theme and voice in two texts. You work with exam-style questions and written reasoning. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Prose and poetry from world writers' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: analyse theme and voice in two texts, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Simile: 'The moon is like a silver coin.' Metaphor: 'The classroom was a zoo.' Imagery: 'the dew-wet grass' appeals to sight and touch. Say what feeling each creates. Method: Model run for 'Prose and poetry from world writers'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read Text A, a prose extract. 'Every clear night Imran set up the old telescope on the roof. He did not hope to discover anything; he simply liked knowing that the light he saw had left its star long before he was born.' Note the calm, curious third-person voice.
  2. Step 2, Read Text B, a poem. 'The seed knows nothing of the oak. / It cracks, it reaches, it drinks. / Whatever tree it will become / is built in small, blind inches.' Both texts are about patient growth and the wonder of slow processes in nature.
  3. Step 3, Identify the theme of each. Text A: wonder comes from observation and from understanding the vast scale of time. Text B: great things grow from small, unseen steps. Shared theme: patience and wonder in the natural world. Write each in a full sentence and avoid summarising the plot.
  4. Step 4, Analyse voice. Text A's narrator is gentle and reflective, using a long final sentence to echo the long journey of starlight. Text B's speaker is plain and almost scientific, with verbs 'cracks, reaches, drinks', but ends on the tender image 'blind inches'. Compare tone: awe against quiet respect.
  5. Step 5, Write a comparison. 'Both texts present patience as the heart of discovery. In Text A, Imran enjoys the light that left its star "long before he was born", while in Text B the seed grows in "small, blind inches". The first looks outward in space; the second looks inward at growth.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: why is 'blind' an effective word in Text B? Model answer: It suggests the seed grows without knowing its goal, so growth is shown as patient trust rather than plan. It also gives the plant a human-like vulnerability that makes the reader care.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Analyse theme and voice in two texts: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Grammar and editing Weeks 10 to 18Apply

In this unit, 'Grammar and editing', you learn to edit text for tense, agreement and clarity. You work with exam-style questions and written reasoning. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Grammar and editing' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: edit text for tense, agreement and clarity, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Passive: 'The cat chased the mouse' becomes 'The mouse was chased by the cat.' Reported: 'I will come,' she said becomes She said that she would come. Modals: 'should' for advice, 'must' for rules. Agreement: 'The list of items is long.' Edit: 'He go to school' becomes 'He goes to school.' Method: Model run for 'Grammar and editing'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Edit for tense. In a lab report the method is usually past tense. Wrong: 'We heat the water and record the temperature every minute yesterday.' Correct: 'We heated the water and recorded the temperature every minute.' A general fact stays in the present: 'Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.'
  2. Step 2, Edit for agreement. 'The results of the experiment is clear.' The subject is 'results', so write 'are clear'. 'Each of the samples were tested' becomes 'was tested'. 'Neither the teacher nor the students was wearing goggles' becomes 'were wearing', and safety goggles should be worn in any practical.
  3. Step 3, Edit for clarity. 'After heating the beaker, the temperature rose.' The temperature did not heat the beaker. Rewrite: 'After we heated the beaker, the temperature rose.' Put the actor next to the action. Also change 'it' to the noun when the reference is unclear: 'the acid reacted with the metal, and the metal dissolved'.
  4. Step 4, Remove wordiness. 'It is a fact that, due to the fact that metals are good conductors, they are able to be used for wires' becomes 'Because metals conduct well, they are used for wires.' Short, exact sentences suit scientific writing. Keep units and numbers precise.
  5. Step 5, Edit a passage. Original: 'The data shows that the plants in light grows taller, and they was measured weekly.' Edited: 'The data show that the plants in light grew taller, and they were measured weekly.' Data is treated as plural in formal science writing, and the past tense suits a completed experiment.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: correct 'The results of the test shows that the mixture were heated too long.' Answer: 'The results of the test show that the mixture was heated too long.' 'Results' is plural so 'show', while 'mixture' is singular so 'was'.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Edit text for tense, agreement and clarity: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Writing: speeches, articles and reports Weeks 19 to 27Apply

In this unit, 'Writing: speeches, articles and reports', you learn to write persuasive and informative pieces. You work with exam-style questions and written reasoning. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Writing: speeches, articles and reports' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: write persuasive and informative pieces, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Formal letter: 12 November 2026; The Principal; Subject: request for a library card; 'Respected Sir, I am a student of Class 8...'; Yours faithfully, Aman. Informal: Dear Ria, ... Your friend, Aman. Report: title, date, place, what happened, result, writer's name. Method: Model run for 'Writing: speeches, articles and reports'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Set purpose and audience. Task: persuade classmates to reduce waste in the school laboratory and inform the principal by report. Persuasive writing uses appeals and a call to action; informative writing uses neutral facts in a fixed order. Decide on tone: serious and clear for both audiences.
  2. Step 2, Calculate evidence. The recycling bin held 480 kg of paper in the first half of the year and 600 kg in the second: 120 divided by 480 is 25 percent more. If a class of 30 wastes 2 sheets each daily, that is 60 sheets, so in 20 school days 1,200 sheets are wasted.
  3. Step 3, Write the speech. 'Dear friends, imagine twelve hundred sheets of paper vanishing in one month, from one class alone. Our recycling rose by 25 percent this year, so change is possible.' A striking figure, then a hopeful fact, makes listeners want to act.
  4. Step 4, Write a short article. Headline: 'Small Changes, Big Savings in Our Lab'. By-line. Opening: 'Students recycled 600 kg of paper in the second half of the year, 25 percent more than before.' Close with a practical tip, such as printing on both sides.
  5. Step 5, Write a practical lab-safety report paragraph. 'During the experiment, all students wore goggles and tied back long hair. Chemicals were handled with gloves, and spills were reported at once.' Reports state what was done, in past tense and third person, with no personal opinions.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a class of 25 wastes 3 sheets each day. How many sheets in 10 days? Answer: 25 times 3 is 75 sheets per day, and 75 times 10 is 750 sheets in 10 days. Write it as 'The class wastes 750 sheets in 10 days.'

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Write persuasive and informative pieces: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Reading: unseen passages and poems Weeks 28 to 32Understand

In this unit, 'Reading: unseen passages and poems', you learn to answer inference and evaluation questions. You work with exam-style questions and written reasoning. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Reading: unseen passages and poems' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: answer inference and evaluation questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: cat, hat and mat rhyme. Make a couplet: 'The sun is high, a bird can fly.' High and fly rhyme. Method: Model run for 'Reading: unseen passages and poems'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read my own passage. "The experiment's results were consistent, yet the researcher hesitated to publish; a single instrument error, she knew, could undo months of work." Gist: she values accuracy over speed.
  2. Step 2, Find evidence for her attitude. "Hesitated" and "could undo months of work" suggest caution and awareness of risk. Reason: the verb and the consequence together reveal attitude.
  3. Step 3, Answer an inference question. "Why does she hesitate?" Model: "She hesitates because she fears an instrument error might invalidate the results; 'could undo months of work' shows the risk is serious."
  4. Step 4, Answer an evaluation question. "How effective is the sentence structure?" Model: "The contrast introduced by 'yet' creates tension between success and doubt, and the long final clause makes the risk feel heavy."
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Weak: "She is nervous because she is a scientist." (no evidence) Right: "She is cautious, since she thinks about one instrument error undoing her work." Always anchor an inference in words from the text.
  6. Step 6, Read a poem, my own: "The telescope counts what the night will give: / a cold white scatter, patient, slow." Inference: observation requires patience. Evidence: "patient, slow". Tone: calm, respectful of nature.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Passage: "The data fit the theory so neatly that the team grew suspicious." What does this suggest? Model answer: "Perfect fit seems unlikely; 'suspicious' shows they suspect error or bias, so they will check their method."

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Answer inference and evaluation questions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 5 Speaking and listening Weeks 33 to 36Apply

In this unit, 'Speaking and listening', you learn to deliver a structured speech and respond to questions. You work with exam-style questions and written reasoning. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Speaking and listening' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: deliver a structured speech and respond to questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Retell in order with first, next, then, last. Ask: 'What happened next?' Reply politely: 'Thank you, I agree because...' Method: Model run for 'Speaking and listening'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Plan a structured speech. Topic: "Why scientific literacy matters." Thesis: "Citizens need scientific literacy to judge evidence and make informed decisions." Structure: hook, thesis, three points, counter-view, conclusion.
  2. Step 2, Write the hook and thesis. "During the last flu season, a false claim about a cure spread faster than the virus. Scientific literacy is the best protection against such claims." Reason: a real-world hook shows relevance.
  3. Step 3, Develop a point with evidence. "First, literacy helps people judge sources. A claim from a controlled trial deserves more trust than a single story." Reason: contrast shows how to test reliability.
  4. Step 4, Handle the counter-view. "Some argue that experts should decide. Experts matter, but citizens vote and choose; they need to understand evidence to hold decisions accountable." Reason: a rebuttal strengthens the speech.
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Weak: "Science is cool and everyone should like it." Better: "Science teaches a method of testing claims, and that method improves everyday decisions." Replace opinion words with precise benefits.
  6. Step 6, Respond to a question. Q: "Does everyone need to know the details?" A: "No, but everyone should understand how evidence is gathered and tested; that is the core skill." Reason: concede, then clarify.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Write a thesis and one rebuttal for a speech on "Renewable energy investment". Model answer: "Thesis: Investing in renewables secures long-term energy and cleaner air." "Rebuttal: Initial costs are high, but lower running costs repay them over time."

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Deliver a structured speech and respond to questions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Letters, sounds and first reading

On the slide: L1 Basic · Letters, sounds and first reading

Notes: Match letters to sounds and read short words and simple sentences aloud. Notes: use the sounds s, a, t, p, i, n; learners blend them into sat, pin and tap, then read a three-word sentence and clap the syllables.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Sentences and simple writing

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Sentences and simple writing

Notes: Write a clear sentence with a capital letter and full stop, and read a short story with understanding. Notes: give a picture prompt; learners say the sentence first, write it, and check capital, spacing and full stop with a partner before reading it aloud.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

Notes: Write a paragraph of five connected sentences and identify nouns, verbs and adjectives in a text. Notes: colour nouns blue and verbs red in a short passage; learners then write about their street, using at least two adjectives and one linking word.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Essays, speaking and responding to texts

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Essays, speaking and responding to texts

Notes: Write a 300-word structured essay and give a short talk, quoting evidence from a text to support a view. Notes: model Point, Evidence, Explain; learners write one paragraph about a character, quote one line, and explain what it shows, then speak it aloud.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Analysis, argument and style (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Analysis, argument and style

Notes: Analyse a writer's choices of language and structure and write a sustained argument of 600 words. Notes: take two openings of different short stories; learners compare tone, sentence length and imagery in a table, then argue which opening works better and why.

This course: Grade 11 Science English is at this level. Its units include Prose and poetry from world writers; Grammar and editing; Writing: speeches, articles and reports; Reading: unseen passages and poems and more.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Literary and linguistic study

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Literary and linguistic study

Notes: Evaluate texts through a critical lens, tracing how language changes across time and context in a researched essay. Notes: compare an older and a modern passage; learners list five differences in vocabulary and syntax and argue in 1,500 words what each reveals about its period.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research, editing and authorship

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research, editing and authorship

Notes: Produce an original piece of research or a polished long-form text, with a literature review and defended method. Notes: pick a narrow question about language in one genre; build a corpus of fifty samples, analyse patterns, write 5,000 words, and revise after external review.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
