flagship course · GL-03

Artificial Intelligence in Practice

Use AI tools safely and usefully at work: pick the right task, write clear instructions, protect data, check results, measure the value and keep people in charge.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5 Specialist60 h of lessons plus practiceLong Module, 1-3 months

Use AI tools safely and usefully at work: pick the right task, write clear instructions, protect data, check results, measure the value and keep people in charge.

  • Understand Explain what current AI tools do well, where they fail and why they sometimes state false things confidently.
  • Apply Write structured instructions that produce a usable draft for a real task.
  • Analyse Test outputs against a checklist and a small test set, and measure errors.
  • Evaluate Judge risk, bias, privacy and copyright for a task and decide whether AI should be used.
  • Create Design a small human-in-the-loop workflow and report time saved and quality.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design, test and report one AI-assisted workflow for a real small task, with a human check and measured results.

Condition

A free or institution-approved AI tool, only public or made-up sample data, a spreadsheet, a real task owner, two peers and a mentor. About 60 hours.

Standard

No personal or confidential data entered; at least 20 test cases checked by a person; error rate and time saved reported with units; a human sign-off step is in the workflow; two peers rate it Meets or better.

Who it is for: Office workers, small-business owners, teachers, analysts, students. Requirements: comfortable with a computer and a spreadsheet. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Never enter personal, health, financial, client-confidential or exam data into a public tool. Do not use AI output for medical, legal, safety-critical or financial decisions without a qualified person. Always disclose AI help where your workplace or place requires it.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What AI is, and what it is not 6 hUnderstand

Today's generative tools predict likely text, images or code from patterns in large amounts of data. They do not know facts the way a person who checked a source does.

  • Predictive AI estimates a number or label (for example, whether a message is spam). Generative AI makes new content from instructions.
  • Strengths: drafting, summarising, rewording, brainstorming, sorting, translating a first draft, finding patterns in tables.
  • Weaknesses: made-up facts and sources (hallucination), uneven maths, old information, unseen bias, no accountability.
  • The same instruction can give different answers; results need checking every time.
  • AI is a tool inside a process. The person using it is responsible for the result.
Worked example. Ask a tool to list five government rules with numbers. Two numbers are wrong and one source does not exist. The lesson: use AI to draft questions to ask, then confirm facts from the original source.

Activity. Test one task three times, compare answers and write what changed and what was wrong.

Standard to meet. Build a comparison table with at least three differences, and list the errors you found.

Module 2 Choosing the right task 6 hAnalyse

Good first tasks are frequent, low risk and easy to check. Poor first tasks are rare, high risk or hard to check.

  • Score each candidate task from 1 to 5 on frequency, time cost, risk if wrong and ease of checking.
  • Start with tasks scoring high on frequency and ease of checking and low on risk.
  • Examples of good starts: first drafts of routine emails, meeting summaries from your own notes, tidying tables, FAQ drafts.
  • Examples to avoid at first: decisions about people, medical or legal advice, anything with secret data.
  • Write the human who will check and what they will look for.
Worked example. Task A: weekly customer reply drafts (frequency 5, time 4, risk 2, checkability 5) scores well. Task B: choosing which staff to promote (risk 5) is excluded.

Activity. Score ten tasks from your own work and pick one to build.

Standard to meet. Score a table of ten tasks, and name a checker for the chosen task.

Module 3 Writing clear instructions (prompts) 8 hApply

A good instruction gives the tool what a new colleague would need.

  • Use five parts: role (who you want it to act as), context (the situation), task (the exact job), format (length, layout, tone) and examples (one good sample).
  • Put facts in the instruction instead of expecting the tool to know them.
  • Ask for steps or a checklist when the task is complex, and ask it to say when it does not know.
  • Change one part at a time and keep a log of which version works best.
  • Keep a reusable template for each routine task.
Worked example. Weak: "Write a reply." Strong: "You are a polite support agent for a small online shop. A customer says order 4821 arrived damaged. Write a 90-word reply that apologises, offers a replacement or refund, and asks for a photo. Tone: warm and plain. Example tone: [short sample]."

Activity. Write three versions of the instruction for your chosen task and compare the outputs.

Standard to meet. Keep a version log that shows what changed and why the best version won, and save the final template.

Module 4 Data, privacy and copyright 6 hEvaluate

What you type may be stored, used or seen by others. What the tool produces may resemble existing work.

  • Classify data: public, internal, confidential, personal. Only public or made-up data goes into public tools.
  • Replace real names and numbers with placeholders (Customer A, 1,000).
  • Read your workplace rule and the tool's data policy; ask whether inputs are used to improve the tool.
  • Copyright and licensing differ by place. Do not present copied or closely mimicked work as your own; check licences for any images, text or code you publish.
  • Disclose AI help where required, and keep a record of what the tool contributed.
Worked example. A customer email with a name and phone number is turned into: "Customer A (phone removed) reports a delayed delivery of order [number]." Only that safe version goes to the tool.

Activity. Write a one-page data rule for your task: what may and may not be entered, with five examples each.

Standard to meet. Write a rule that names the data classes, gives five allowed and five forbidden examples, and says who to ask.

Module 5 Checking outputs: accuracy, bias, hallucination 8 hAnalyse

Trust is earned by testing. Build a small test set and a checklist.

  • Test set: 20 or more realistic inputs with the answers you would accept. Include tricky and unusual cases.
  • Checklist: facts correct, numbers recomputed, sources real, tone right, nothing sensitive, nothing unfair to a group.
  • Verify any claim that matters in an original source. If a source is cited, open it.
  • Bias: ask who could be treated unfairly by the output and test with varied names, places and cases.
  • Record the error rate: errors divided by test cases.
Worked example. 20 test cases: 3 contain a wrong fact, 2 have an unclear tone. Error rate = 5 / 20 = 25%. After improving the instruction and adding a rule "say unknown if unsure", errors fall to 2 / 20 = 10%.

Activity. Run your 20 test cases, score them, improve the instruction and run them again.

Standard to meet. Compare two score tables, compute the error rate correctly, and explain the improvement.

Module 6 Building a human-in-the-loop workflow 10 hCreate

Design who does what: the tool drafts, the person checks and decides.

  • Map the steps: input, tool draft, human check, send or publish, record.
  • Set stop rules: when to skip the tool and do it by hand.
  • Log each use: date, task, tool, who checked, result.
  • Keep the final decision and signature with a person.
  • Write a short guide so a colleague can follow it.
Worked example. Support reply workflow: agent pastes a masked customer message; tool drafts reply; agent checks facts and tone; agent sends; agent logs the case. Time per reply falls from 8 minutes to 5 minutes.

Activity. Draw your workflow and run it on 20 real (masked) or sample cases.

Standard to meet. Diagram has a human check and a stop rule; the log shows all 20 cases.

Module 7 Measuring value 8 hEvaluate

Value is time saved, quality kept and risk avoided. Measure before and after.

  • Baseline: time per task by hand, error rate, cost per hour.
  • After: time with the tool including checking, error rate.
  • Time saved per task x tasks per month x hourly cost = monthly value. Subtract tool cost.
  • Include the learning time and any checking time honestly.
  • Report quality too: customer satisfaction, rework, complaints.
Worked example. Before: 8 minutes per reply, 120 replies a month = 16 hours. After: 5 minutes = 10 hours. Saved 6 hours x USD 12 per hour (an estimate; check your market) = USD 72 a month. Tool cost USD 20. Net value USD 52 a month. Errors stayed at 2%.

Activity. Compute baseline, after, and net value for your workflow.

Standard to meet. Calculate the arithmetic correctly with units and currency, report a quality measure, and list the assumptions.

Module 8 Responsible use, limits and jobs 8 hEvaluate

Use AI to give people better work, not to hide mistakes or replace judgement.

  • Be open: tell customers and colleagues when AI helped, if required.
  • Keep humans in charge of decisions about people, safety, health, law and money.
  • Review regularly: tools change, rules change, and errors may appear later.
  • Skills that grow in value: asking good questions, checking, domain knowledge, ethics.
  • Jobs: AI-assisted operations, content and support assistant, data clean-up, workflow designer, trainer for colleagues.
Worked example. Policy line: "AI drafts; a named person reviews every message that leaves the company; no personal data is typed into public tools; we review this rule every six months."

Activity. Write a one-page team policy and present your whole project in 10 minutes.

Standard to meet. Write a policy with five rules, an owner and a review date, and present the results, limits and one thing you chose not to use AI for.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: task scoring, instruction log, data rule, test sets and scores, workflow diagram, time log, net-value sheet, team policy. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Marking rubric (Not yet, Meets, Exceeds)
CriterionNot yetMeetsExceeds
Task choiceNo scoringTen tasks scored; checker namedScoring justifies a clear first choice
InstructionsOne tryThree versions comparedTemplate reused and improved

TestingNo test set20 cases, error rateRe-test shows measured improvement
Value and ethicsNo numbersNet value computed; limits statedHonest result with policy and review date

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a hallucination in an AI tool?

Model answer: A confident answer that is false or invented, such as a made-up fact or source. (Covered in module 1: What AI is, and what it is not. To pass that module: Build a comparison table with at least three differences, and list the errors you found.)

Q2. Name the five parts of a clear instruction.

Model answer: Role, context, task, format, examples. (Covered in module 3: Writing clear instructions (prompts). To pass that module: Keep a version log that shows what changed and why the best version won, and save the final template.)

Q3. 20 test cases give 5 errors. What is the error rate?

Model answer: 5 / 20 = 25%. (Covered in module 5: Checking outputs: accuracy, bias, hallucination. To pass that module: Compare two score tables, compute the error rate correctly, and explain the improvement.)

Q4. Which data may go into a public tool?

Model answer: Only public or made-up data. (Covered in module 4: Data, privacy and copyright. To pass that module: Write a rule that names the data classes, gives five allowed and five forbidden examples, and says who to ask.)

Q5. Give two good first tasks for AI help.

Model answer: Routine email drafts and summaries of your own notes (low risk, easy to check). (Covered in module 2: Choosing the right task. To pass that module: Score a table of ten tasks, and name a checker for the chosen task.)

Q6. Why score tasks by risk and checkability?

Model answer: To start with tasks where mistakes are cheap and easy to spot. (Covered in module 2: Choosing the right task. To pass that module: Score a table of ten tasks, and name a checker for the chosen task.)

Q7. Time per task falls from 10 to 6 minutes for 150 tasks a month. How many hours are saved?

Model answer: 4 minutes x 150 = 600 minutes = 10 hours. (Covered in module 7: Measuring value. To pass that module: Calculate the arithmetic correctly with units and currency, report a quality measure, and list the assumptions.)

Q8. Who is responsible for AI-assisted work?

Model answer: The person who uses and publishes it. (Covered in module 1: What AI is, and what it is not. To pass that module: Build a comparison table with at least three differences, and list the errors you found.)

Q9. Why log each use?

Model answer: For accountability, learning and later checking. (Covered in module 6: Building a human-in-the-loop workflow. To pass that module: Diagram has a human check and a stop rule; the log shows all 20 cases.)

Q10. Name one decision that should stay with a person.

Model answer: Decisions about hiring or promoting staff, health, legal matters, safety or large sums of money. (Covered in module 8: Responsible use, limits and jobs. To pass that module: Write a policy with five rules, an owner and a review date, and present the results, limits and one thing you chose not to use AI for.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Check workplace rules, data-protection law and any rules on disclosing AI use for your place. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.
