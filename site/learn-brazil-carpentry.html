Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan, start and run carpentry in São Paulo: serve a home owner or a builder, deliver a made or fitted wooden piece, keep records, and show a working month of numbers.

Condition

About 8 weeks part-time. Own phone or computer, real customers or a real practice site, a peer and a mentor for the final review. Local rules and licences checked against official sources.

Standard

Every module standard is met; all claims are true and backed by records; peers and a mentor mark the evidence 'Meets' or better.

Who it is for: students, graduates, career changers, owners, workers and returners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, tracker, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know your customer and market 4 hAnalyse

Every sound business in carpentry begins with one clear customer and one clear problem. In São Paulo and the wider state, demand is real but crowded, so a small, well-described customer group wins before a broad one.

  • In carpentry: Customers want fit, finish and timing; take measurements and photos.
  • Write the customer in one sentence: a home owner or a builder. Add the place, the moment of need and what they use today.
  • List five rivals or substitutes within reach. For each, record price, strength, weakness and how people reach it.
  • Interview at least eight real customers with open questions. Record exact words, not your summary.
  • Count the market in units: how many pieces could one area buy in a month? Use two public sources and your own count, then take the lower figure.
  • Choose one first segment and say why the others wait.
Worked example. Weak: 'Everyone needs a made or fitted wooden piece.' Strong: 'Our first customer is a home owner or a builder within 8 km of the centre, who today pays R$ 1.980 per piece and complains about delay. We target 7 pieces a month by month three.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: ten customers are interviewed for carpentry. Seven say price, two say speed, one says trust. Question: which first message do you write, and which interview record backs it? Model answer: lead with the piece price and speed promise, keep trust proof as the second line, and cite the seven price answers by date.

Activity. Write the one-sentence customer, a five-row rival table and eight interview records. Share them with a peer for a challenge round.

Standard to meet. Customer is specific; rival table has prices; every claim traces to an interview or a source.

Module 2 Rules, registration and data duties in Brazil 4 hUnderstand

Brazil asks every trader to be visible and accountable. Know which register, tax regime and sector rules apply before the first sale, and check each against the current official text.

  • In carpentry: Register trade or MEI; wood sourcing should be legal with documentation.
  • Choose a legal form: micro-entrepreneur (MEI) only if your activity appears on the official MEI list, or a company registered with a CNPJ. Regulated activities need their sector authorisation first. Check the current limits and lists on the official portal.
  • Learn the tax regime that fits your size and write down which taxes you will collect and pay, and when.
  • Find the sector rules for carpentry: licences, inspections, professional registers. List each with the office that issues it and the renewal date.
  • Personal data of customers falls under Brazil's data protection law (LGPD). Collect only what you need, say why, keep it safe, and name who answers questions.
  • Consumer law gives buyers clear rights on information, returns and complaints. Put your terms in plain Portuguese.
Worked example. Checklist for a new carpentry trader: 1) legal form (CNPJ, or MEI only if your activity is on the official list); 2) city operating licence; 3) sector licence or register; 4) tax set-up; 5) privacy notice; 6) written terms. Each has an owner and a date.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a neighbour says registration can wait until the first R$ 9.000 of sales. Question: what do you do? Model answer: do not delay; write the compliance table first, confirm the register, tax and sector licence with the official office, and start only where the table shows no open blocker.

Activity. Build your own six-line compliance table for your first location. Mark what is done, what is pending and who confirms it.

Standard to meet. Every line cites an official source; nothing is assumed; pending items have dates. Local law always wins over this course.

Module 3 Design the offer 4 hCreate

An offer states what the customer gets, how well, by when and at what price. For a made or fitted wooden piece, the offer must make the quality visible and the next step easy.

  • In carpentry: Quote with drawing, materials and finish; show samples.
  • Describe the core offer in three lines: what is delivered (a made or fitted wooden piece), the standard it meets, and the time it takes.
  • Add one proof for each promise: a sample, a test result, a guarantee or a customer record.
  • Price from cost upward and from customer value downward, then pick a price inside both.
  • Write a simple one-page terms sheet: what is included, what is extra, how to cancel.
  • Create two options at most, so choice stays easy.
Worked example. Offer: 'A made or fitted wooden piece, delivered within agreed time, checked against a written standard, price R$ 1.800 per piece. Proof: a dated record the customer can check and sign. Changes or cancellation by written agreement before work begins.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a customer asks for a discount from R$ 1.800 to R$ 1.620 per piece. Question: decide with numbers. Model answer: contribution falls from R$ 900 to R$ 720 per piece; offer an extra of value (for example faster service) before cutting price, and accept only if the lower contribution still clears break-even.

Longer notes. List three pieces you can build well, such as a made-to-measure table, a wardrobe door set or a shelf unit, and write what each includes, such as finish and delivery. Cost a piece from boards, hardware, finish, helper hours and transport. Visit the client's room, measure twice and agree the drawing before cutting. Quote a price per piece, with a deposit and a delivery date. Confirm that the workshop is registered and that the timber you buy carries a legal origin paper. Keep your quote template to one page with a drawing, the wood or board type, the finish, the deposit and the delivery date. Clients who see exactly what they are buying argue less about price and changes.

Extra worked example. One piece sells for R$ 1.800. Variable cost is R$ 520 for boards and timber, R$ 180 for hardware and finish, R$ 150 for helper hours and R$ 50 for delivery, so R$ 520 + R$ 180 + R$ 150 + R$ 50 = R$ 900. Margin is R$ 900, or 50 percent. Fixed cost is R$ 6.000 a month, so break-even is 6.000 / 900 = 6,67, rounded up to 7 pieces. These component costs are invented, not real prices. Twelve pieces leave R$ 4.800 after rent and power.

Activity. Write the offer card, the proof list and the terms sheet. Test them with three customers and note every point where they hesitated.

Standard to meet. Promise, proof and price are consistent; terms fit one page; three customer reactions are recorded.

Module 4 Run the work step by step 5 hApply

Good operations make results repeatable. For carpentry, write each step so a new person could follow it on their first day, and measure each step.

  • In carpentry: Steps: measure, cut list, cut, join, sand, finish, install.
  • Map the process from first contact to paid and closed. Typical stages: enquiry, quote, preparation, delivery of the piece, check, payment, follow-up.
  • Name the key resource at each stage: sharp, guarded tools and dry timber. Note what happens if it is missing.
  • Write a one-page standard procedure per stage: who, what, tool, time, record.
  • Choose three measures: speed, quality (errors per 100) and customer rating. Review them every week.
  • Fix the cause of a defect, not the symptom. Ask 'why' five times and record the answer.
Worked example. Stage record: 'Prepare. Owner: lead. Time: 30 min. Tool: checklist. Record: signed sheet. Defect found on 1 of 3 pieces last week; cause: missing check on sharp, guarded tools and dry timber; fix: add check at start, review next Friday.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: of 12 pieces last month, 1 had a defect traced to sharp, guarded tools and dry timber. Question: what is the defect rate and what is your first action? Model answer: about 8 defects per 100; stop the cause (add a check at the start), correct affected customers, log the cause, and re-measure next week.

Activity. Draw the process map and write procedures for the two riskiest stages. Run each once and time it.

Standard to meet. Procedures are followable by a stranger; measures have weekly numbers; one defect is traced to a root cause.

Module 5 Safety, quality and risk 4 hEvaluate

The most serious risk in this field is a cut injury or a poor fit. A calm, written risk routine protects people first and the business second.

  • In carpentry: Saws, dust and chemicals injure; guard machines, wear gear, extract dust.
  • List ten things that could go wrong. Score each for how likely (1 to 5) and how serious (1 to 5). Multiply and sort.
  • For the top five, choose a control: remove the hazard, replace it, guard it, train people, or use protective gear, in that order of preference.
  • Write who does what in an emergency, and rehearse it once a quarter.
  • Keep an incident and near-miss log. Near-misses are free lessons.
  • Check that insurance and legal duties match the risk you actually carry.
Worked example. Risk row: 'Hazard: a cut injury or a poor fit. Likelihood 3, severity 5, score 15. Control: written check before each piece, trained staff, log sheet. Owner: site lead. Review monthly.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a near-miss occurs: a cut injury or a poor fit nearly happened but no one was hurt. Question: what do you do in the next 24 hours? Model answer: make safe, record the event, ask the five whys, add or tighten one control, brief the team, and set a review date; near-misses count as lessons, not blame.

Activity. Build a ten-row risk register and a one-page emergency plan. Run a ten-minute drill with a peer and note two improvements.

Standard to meet. Top risks have controls ranked by preference; drill done; log started. Where a regulation applies, follow the official rule.

Module 6 People, skills and service 4 hApply

People deliver the promise. Define roles, train to a standard and give feedback in the open.

  • In carpentry: Train helpers on tool use before power tools.
  • Write a role card for each person: purpose, five tasks, the standard for each, and who to ask for help.
  • Train by showing, doing with support, then doing alone with a check. Record sign-off per task.
  • Set service rules for answering customers: reply times, tone, how to apologise and fix.
  • Hold a ten-minute team check each day: yesterday's problem, today's plan, one risk.
  • Build a growth path: what a new person can do in week 1, month 3 and month 12.
Worked example. Training record: 'Task: handle a complaint. Shown 2 May; done with support 4 May; done alone 8 May, checked: apology, fix, follow-up call. Signed by lead.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a new hire completes training but a customer complaint shows a skipped step. Question: whose gap is it? Model answer: first the system: check the role card, the training sign-off and the checklist; then coach the person and fix the document so the step cannot be skipped.

Longer notes. Give the helper a defined task, such as sanding, drilling hinge holes or applying the first coat, and show the finished standard on a sample board. Build a drilling jig for repeat holes and teach its use. Check each cut against the cut list before assembly. Insist on glasses, ear protection and a dust mask. Agree hours, pay and delivery days in writing, and ask an adviser which rules cover a helper who runs a saw. Store tools on a marked board so a missing chisel is visible at a glance, and sharpen blades on a set day each week. A helper who cares for tools wastes less wood and works more safely.

Extra worked example. Before the jig, 2 of 12 pieces were remade, each wasting R$ 520 of boards and R$ 150 of helper time, which is R$ 670 per remake and R$ 1.340 in all. After training only 1 was remade, or R$ 670. Saving is R$ 1.340 - R$ 670 = R$ 670. Training times and wage rates are invented for the example, not market data. Training took 3 hours at R$ 25, which is R$ 75, so the net gain is R$ 595.

Activity. Write two role cards and a training plan for one new hire. Role-play a hard customer conversation twice and review.

Standard to meet. Role cards have measurable standards; training shows sign-off steps; service rules are written in plain words.

Module 7 Money: cost, price and break-even 5 hAnalyse

A business lives or dies on a few numbers. Know your contribution per unit, your fixed costs and the volume you need.

  • In carpentry: Include waste, glue and finish in cost; invoice by stage.
  • Contribution per piece = price minus variable cost. Fixed costs are paid whether you sell or not.
  • Break-even units = fixed cost divided by contribution. Plan to pass it by a safe margin.
  • Make a 12-month cash plan. Cash timing matters as much as profit: customers may pay late, suppliers may want money first.
  • Keep money for business and money for the owner apart.
  • Test the plan: what if sales fall by a quarter, or cost rises by a tenth?
Worked example. Worked example, all figures in reais: price R$ 1.800, variable cost R$ 900, contribution R$ 900 per piece. Fixed cost R$ 6.000 a month. Break-even = R$ 6.000 / R$ 900 = 6,67, so 7 pieces a month. At 12 pieces a month, profit = R$ 900 x 12 - R$ 6.000 = R$ 4.800. These are teaching figures that belong to this example only; later examples and the case study use their own figures. Use your own.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: sales fall to 9 pieces a month. Question: what is the monthly result? Model answer: R$ 900 x 9 - R$ 6.000 = R$ 2.100; compare with break-even of 7 pieces and decide whether to cut fixed cost or raise volume first. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build your own table: price, variable cost, contribution, fixed cost, break-even and a downside case at 75% of sales. Show it to a peer or mentor.

Standard to meet. Arithmetic is correct; every figure has a source or is marked as an assumption; a downside case is shown.

Module 8 Grow, measure and renew 4 hEvaluate

Growth should follow proof. Measure, learn, then add; and keep returning to the customer.

  • In carpentry: Grow by repeat clients and portfolio photos.
  • Pick one growth lever at a time: new customers, a higher repeat rate, a better price, or a new offer.
  • Set one measurable target for 90 days, such as 'raise repeat customers from 20% to 30%'.
  • Ask each customer how they found you, and record it. Spend more where the record shows results.
  • Review monthly: numbers, defects, complaints, cash. Write three decisions each time.
  • Plan the renewal of licences, equipment and skills a year ahead.
Worked example. 90-day plan: 'Lever: repeat customers. Action: follow-up call after every piece. Target: repeat rate from 20% to 30%. Check: weekly count. Review date: day 45 and day 90.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: you can add 2 pieces a month by one of two levers: a follow-up call to past customers or a price rise of R$ 180 to R$ 1.980. Question: how do you choose? Model answer: test the cheaper reversible lever first (the call), measure for 30 days, and only then try the price change, so you can tell which one worked.

Longer notes. Keep a sheet of every quote with the piece, price, cost, days used and result. Call clients one week after sending a quote. Ask satisfied clients for a photo and a referral to designers or neighbours. Compare time per piece to find slow steps. Keep a small stock of common boards to avoid waiting for suppliers. Review prices each quarter against board and hardware costs. Photograph every finished piece in good light before delivery and keep the pictures with the job sheet. Good photographs bring new enquiries and also show you how your finish has improved over time.

Extra worked example. You sent 30 quotes and won 12, so the win rate is 12 / 30 = 40 percent. Revenue is 12 x R$ 1.800 = R$ 21.600 and variable cost is 12 x R$ 900 = R$ 10.800. After R$ 6.000 fixed cost, profit is R$ 4.800. The extra volumes and conversion rates are invented for the example, not market data. A follow-up call lifts the win rate to 50 percent, which is 15 pieces, so profit becomes 15 x R$ 900 - R$ 6.000 = R$ 7.500, or R$ 2.700 more.

Activity. Write a 90-day plan with one target, three actions and a review date. Present it in five minutes and take questions.

Standard to meet. Target is measurable and dated; actions are small enough to start this week; review date is in the calendar.

case study

A case to work through

Situation. Valdir runs a small carpentry workshop in Sao Paulo and builds made-to-measure pieces such as tables and wardrobe door sets. He charges R$ 1.800 per piece, and boards, hardware, finish, helper hours and delivery cost about R$ 900. His fixed costs are R$ 6.000 a month for the rented bay, power, tools and insurance. Last month he delivered 12 pieces. One client asked for a change after the boards were cut, so he remade a piece, wasting R$ 520 of boards and R$ 150 of helper time. Valdir does not know how many pieces he needs to cover costs, or how much the remake cost him. He keeps prices in his head and works from sketches on scrap wood, so clients sometimes remember the agreement differently.

Task. Say what you would do first, next and last, and what record you would keep.

Model answer. First, margin per piece is R$ 1.800 - R$ 900 = R$ 900, or 50 percent. Break-even is 6.000 / 900 = 6,67, so he needs 7 pieces. At 12 pieces, profit is 12 x R$ 900 - R$ 6.000 = R$ 4.800. Next, the remake wasted R$ 520 + R$ 150 = R$ 670, which is 74,4 percent of one piece's margin, and cut profit to R$ 4.130. Ask for a deposit of 40 percent, R$ 720, when the drawing is signed, and price any change in writing before cutting. Also, keep a one-page quote with the drawing, wood type, finish, deposit and delivery date, and photograph the piece before it leaves. Track hours per piece for a month to learn whether the price still covers the work. The remake costs and deposit are worked-example figures, not market data. Valdir should photograph each piece beside its signed drawing, so the client sees the R$ 1.800 piece they approved.

levels L1 to L7

Where this sits on the ladder

Seven levels, one slide each. Read the slide, then read the speaker notes under it. Credits stack and you can step in and out; this course is L4.

Slide 1 of 7: L1 Basic

  • Name common woods and hand tools
  • Identify basic workshop safety rules
  • Describe simple joints
Notes for slide 1

Handle a try square, marking gauge, chisel and tenon saw under supervision, and name pine, cedar, ipe and plywood by feel and grain. Cut and dry-fit one half-lap joint. Wear glasses, ear protection and a dust mask every session. Hand in a photo log of three labelled joints. Roughly six hours of practice.

Slide 2 of 7: L2 Awareness

  • Explain how wood moves, dries and is graded
  • Recognize power tools and their risks
  • Compare solid wood and board materials
Notes for slide 2

Visit a joinery shop and watch the panel saw, thicknesser and router run, noting guards, extraction and push sticks. Learn why a board cups across the grain, what moisture content suits indoor furniture, and how solid wood differs from MDF or plywood. Evidence is a one-page materials table with cost per square metre and typical uses.

Slide 3 of 7: L3 Foundation

  • Measure, mark and cut wood accurately
  • Make basic joints and assemble a small piece
  • Finish a surface by sanding and sealing
Notes for slide 3

Build a small bench or box from a cut list: mark with a knife, cut to the line, plane, glue, clamp and check square with diagonals. Sand through the grits and seal with oil or varnish. A tutor measures tolerance, aiming for gaps under half a millimetre, and checks that blades finished the day sharp and put away.

Slide 4 of 7: L4 Practitioner

  • Plan a carpentry business or workshop in Sao Paulo
  • Start it with tools, space, registrations and suppliers
  • Run quotes, production, delivery and cash records
Notes for slide 4

Set up on paper a one-bench workshop in a rented bay: tool list, power supply, dust control, timber supplier and a template quote adding material, hours and delivery. Produce and deliver two paid or mock jobs. Evidence is quote, cut list, photos and a cash sheet showing what each piece cost against what was charged.

Slide 5 of 7: L5 Specialist

  • Specialize in furniture, joinery or built-in fittings
  • Read drawings and cut lists
  • Control quality and cost per job
Notes for slide 5

Pick a lane such as kitchens, wardrobes or doors, then read the designer's drawing, turn it into a cut list and nest the parts on a sheet to reduce offcuts. Build a full-size mock-up joint before cutting expensive boards. Evidence is a drawing set, an optimised cut list and a cost-per-job record.

Slide 6 of 7: L6 Advanced

  • Lead a team and schedule several jobs
  • Use machines and jigs safely and efficiently
  • Win repeat clients and design partners
Notes for slide 6

Schedule three overlapping jobs across a cutting day, an assembly day and a finishing day, with a helper on each. Build jigs for repeat drilling and routing, test them on scrap, and document guard and isolation checks. Evidence is a weekly plan, a count of reworked parts, and two repeat clients or designers who send further work.

Slide 7 of 7: L7 Super expert

  • Create a signature product line
  • Mentor apprentices and craftspeople
  • Advise on workshop design and growth
Notes for slide 7

Develop a small catalogue of pieces that others can build to your drawings, and teach an apprentice for six months with a skills checklist signed off at each step. Review a workshop layout for flow, dust and fire risk. Evidence is the catalogue, the apprentice's work and a written note on how output could scale.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local rules change licences, taxes, data duties and customer rights. This course is written for São Paulo. Check the current official text for your own place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registration: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registration: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registration: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registration: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registration: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registration: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registration: registration with ACRA. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registration: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registration: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registration: local business registration in the country where you work.
