Vertical garden developer company

Run a real vertical garden developer company pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small vertical garden developer company offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: food, farming and hospitality. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Choose crops, space and a first customer 6 hUnderstand

Vertical gardens grow food or plants in stacked layers indoors or on walls. They suit places with little land and high prices for fresh produce.

  • Compare crops by days to harvest, price per kilogram and light needed: leafy greens and herbs turn fastest; fruit crops need more space and light.
  • Find a first customer within an hour's drive: restaurants, grocers, food programmes, office tenants who want green walls.
  • Ask each for weekly volume, price they pay now and the freshness gap you can close.
  • Write the offer: 'fresh [crop], harvested the day before, delivered twice a week at [price] per kilogram'.
  • Build a crop table with one row per crop: days to harvest, kilograms per tray, price per kilogram, and revenue per tray (kilograms x price).
  • Measure your space: write length, width, ceiling height, floor load and where the power and water points are, before you choose a rack layout.
  • Pick one crop to start. Write why it beats the second choice on speed, price and the risk of loss.
Worked example. Basil sells at 28 per kilogram to restaurants who now pay 36 for imported, so each kitchen saves 8 per kilogram. Demand is 12 kg per week across three kitchens: 12 x 28 = 336 a week in sales, and the buyers save 12 x 8 = 96 a week. This is a small start, which is right for a first customer test.

Activity. Interview five buyers and fill a crop table with price, volume and delivery frequency.

Standard to meet. Five buyers recorded; one crop chosen with reasons.

Module 2 Know the rules for growing and selling in Canada 6 hApply

Growing under lights is farming, manufacturing and a building project at once.

  • Food grown for sale must follow food-safety and labelling rules, and which federal, provincial or local rules apply depends on where you sell and what you grow, so check the provincial rule; the local public health unit may inspect washing and packing areas.
  • Building and fire rules apply to racks, wiring and water; get electrical work done by licensed trades and inspected.
  • Water use, nutrient discharge and waste are regulated by the municipality and province.
  • Insurance for crop loss, equipment and liability should be arranged before the first harvest.
  • Zoning decides whether you may grow and sell food from a warehouse or shop unit. Ask the municipality in writing and keep the reply.
  • A lease should allow the changes you need, such as extra power and drains. Read it for who pays for repairs and what happens at renewal.
  • Check the provincial rule for any licence or registration needed to sell food directly to restaurants or shops.
Worked example. Row: 'Pack leafy greens for sale | federal or provincial food inspection office and local health unit | register and follow wash and pack rules | before first sale | inspection report'. A table of 10 rows with 3 rows needing a permit means 3 of 10 (30%) have a waiting time to plan around, so list those first.

Activity. Make the rules table for a rented warehouse unit in your province.

Standard to meet. Each row cites an official source and date.

Module 3 Design the system and grow a first crop 10 hApply

Good vertical growing is a controlled loop: light, water, nutrients, air and time.

  • Choose a system: hydroponic (roots in water), aeroponic (roots misted) or a soil-based wall, and write why it suits your crop and budget.
  • Set written targets for light hours per day, nutrient strength (conductivity), water pH, temperature and humidity.
  • Log these readings daily and change only one setting at a time so you can see what caused any change.
  • Track yield per tray and days to harvest for every cycle so that you can compare runs.
  • Calibrate the pH and conductivity meters before the first crop with the test liquids the maker supplies, and write the date.
  • Test the water you use. Hard or chlorinated water changes nutrient needs, so record what the local supply report or your own test shows.
  • Keep a seed lot number for each tray, and compare yield between lots.
Worked example. Lettuce: 28 days to harvest, 0.45 kg per tray, 12 trays per rack, 10 racks. One rack gives 0.45 x 12 = 5.4 kg and ten racks give 54 kg per cycle. If one rack fails, the cycle gives 48.6 kg, a loss of 10%.

Activity. Grow one tray cycle; log daily readings and final yield.

Standard to meet. Daily log complete; yield per tray calculated.

Module 4 Cost it: power, labour and trays 8 hEvaluate

Electricity for lights is usually the largest cost after rent.

  • Power cost per cycle = lamp kilowatts x hours per day x days x price per kWh.
  • Add labour for seeding, moving, harvesting and cleaning, using the hours you actually record.
  • Compute cost per tray and per kilogram and compare them with the selling price.
  • Test a 10% rise in power price and a 15% yield drop, and show the new margin and break-even for each.
  • Use your own electricity bill for the price per kWh, with the delivery and rate-class charges included. Check the provincial rate rules for farms or businesses.
  • Add fixed costs each month: rent, insurance, water, software and a share of your own pay.
  • Break-even trays per month = fixed cost divided by margin per tray, rounded up.
Worked example. Power per tray: 0.2 kW x 16 h x 28 days = 89.6 kWh; at 0.16 per kWh that is about 14. Add seeds and nutrients 6, packaging 4, labour 14 for 38 variable; price 95 per tray-equivalent gives margin 57 (60%). Break-even is 21,700 / 57 = about 381 trays a month. Power test: a 10% rise in power price adds about 1.4, so variable cost is 39.4, margin 55.6 and break-even is 21,700 / 55.6 = about 390 trays. Yield test: a 15% yield drop cuts revenue to 80.75 and margin to 42.75, so break-even rises to about 508 trays. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build the cost sheet for your system and compute break-even.

Standard to meet. Both stress tests shown.

Module 5 Run clean, steady operations 8 hApply

Hygiene, schedules and records keep crops safe and predictable.

  • Clean trays and tanks between cycles, sanitise tools and require hand washing at the entry.
  • Stagger seeding weekly so that harvests arrive steadily.
  • Scout for pests and mould twice a week and remove problem trays at once.
  • Keep a harvest and delivery log with batch numbers so that every bag can be traced.
  • Write a daily checklist by the door: lights on, pumps running, water level, alarms tested, doors closed.
  • Keep tools and cleaning supplies separate for growing and packing areas.
  • Train every helper in the hand-washing and illness rules before they touch a crop, and record the date.
Worked example. Batch code 'B-0417-T3' links a delivered bag to the tray, seed lot and cleaning record. If a 28-day crop is seeded weekly across 4 groups of trays, one group is harvested each week, so a customer receives 4 deliveries in a 28-day period.

Activity. Keep a 4-week operations log with batch numbers.

Standard to meet. Every delivery traces back to a tray.

Module 6 Sell, deliver and keep the customer 6 hCreate

Restaurants and grocers value a steady supply and a person who answers.

  • Offer a standing weekly order with a trial week.
  • Deliver on a fixed day and time; confirm by message the day before.
  • Tell the story: seed to harvest dates, no spray, local. Say 'no spray' only if it is true and recorded.
  • Ask for feedback after four weeks and adjust crop mix.
  • Price by weight, and show what the buyer saves compared with what they pay now, in dollars per week.
  • Agree what happens when a delivery is late or short: a credit or a replacement on the next day.
  • Keep a customer sheet: contact, order, delivery day, price, complaints and next review date.
Worked example. Funnel: 15 visits, 6 samples, 3 trial orders, 2 standing orders. Rates: samples 6/15 = 40%, trials 3/6 = 50%, standing 2/3 = 67%. Overall 2/15 = 13% of visits became standing orders, so 10 more visits would bring about 1 or 2 more.

Activity. Visit ten buyers with samples and record outcomes.

Standard to meet. Funnel recorded; one change made.

Module 7 Manage food safety and power risks 6 hEvaluate

A power cut or contamination can end a crop in hours.

  • List the main risks: power failure, pump failure, contamination, pests and the loss of a buyer.
  • Set controls for each risk, such as backup power for pumps, alarms, spare parts and regular water tests.
  • Write a recall plan that shows which batches went to which buyers and how you will tell them.
  • Write an energy plan: efficient lamps, off-peak operating hours, and renewable power by contract or on the roof where allowed.
  • Score each risk as chance (1 to 5) times harm (1 to 5) and handle the highest scores first.
  • Plan for a hot summer or cold winter day: how the room keeps its temperature if cooling or heating fails.
  • Run a test once a month: switch off the main power and check the alarm and the backup. Write the result.
Worked example. Risk: 'Pump fails overnight | 2 x 5 = 10 | float alarm and spare pump | owner | monthly test'. A contamination risk scored 3 x 5 = 15 ranks higher, so its controls come first.

Activity. Write an eight-row risk register.

Standard to meet. Each row has owner and test date.

Module 8 Pilot one room and plan growth 8 hCreate

Prove one room before you build ten.

  • Run the pilot for 60 days and measure yield, cost per kilogram and orders kept.
  • Set a stop rule: if yield is below 60% of plan after two cycles, pause and fix the cause.
  • Scale in steps: open a second room only after the margin holds.
  • Plan funding from grants for local food and clean technology and from customer pre-orders, but do not count on either until it is confirmed.
  • Write the plan numbers before the pilot: planned yield per cycle, planned cost per kilogram and planned margin.
  • Hold a review at day 30 and day 60. Record what changed and why.
  • Check the funding programmes' rules and dates yourself; do not assume a grant will arrive.
Worked example. Result at day 60: 48 kg per cycle against 54 plan (48/54 = 89%), above the 60% stop line, so proceed. If the yield had been 30 kg (56%), the stop rule would pause the pilot to fix the cause.

Activity. Run the pilot and present review.

Standard to meet. Review has measures and decision.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why compare crops by days to harvest?

Model answer: Faster crops return cash sooner and fit stacked systems best. (Covered in module 1: Choose crops, space and a first customer. To pass that module: Five buyers recorded; one crop chosen with reasons.)

Q2. What is usually the largest cost after rent?

Model answer: Electricity for lights. (Covered in module 4: Cost it: power, labour and trays. To pass that module: Both stress tests shown.)

Q3. How do you compute power cost per cycle?

Model answer: Lamp kilowatts x hours per day x days x price per kWh. (Covered in module 4: Cost it: power, labour and trays. To pass that module: Both stress tests shown.)

Q4. Why use batch codes?

Model answer: They trace a delivered bag back to its tray and enable recalls. (Covered in module 5: Run clean, steady operations. To pass that module: Every delivery traces back to a tray.)

Q5. Name three growing systems.

Model answer: Hydroponic, aeroponic and soil-based wall. (Covered in module 3: Design the system and grow a first crop. To pass that module: Daily log complete; yield per tray calculated.)

Q6. What should a risk register include for pumps?

Model answer: A control such as an alarm and a spare, an owner and a test date. (Covered in module 7: Manage food safety and power risks. To pass that module: Each row has owner and test date.)

Q7. What is a stop rule in the pilot?

Model answer: A pre-agreed trigger, such as yield under 60% of plan, to pause and fix. (Covered in module 8: Pilot one room and plan growth. To pass that module: Review has measures and decision.)

Q8. Why stagger seeding?

Model answer: Harvests stay steady week to week. (Covered in module 5: Run clean, steady operations. To pass that module: Every delivery traces back to a tray.)

Q9. Who inspects wash and pack areas?

Model answer: Depending on the province, the local public health unit or a provincial inspector may; check the provincial rule. (Covered in module 2: Know the rules for growing and selling in Canada. To pass that module: Each row cites an official source and date.)

Q10. Why test a 15% yield drop?

Model answer: It shows whether the business still breaks even under stress. (Covered in module 4: Cost it: power, labour and trays. To pass that module: Both stress tests shown.)

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
