Online Therapy Support (Awareness Level)

Understand how online emotional-support services work, what they can and cannot do, and how to stay safe and kind. Awareness only; this is not clinical training.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L2 Awareness16:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain the difference between peer support, coaching and licensed therapy.
  • Apply Use a safe-contact checklist before joining or recommending a service.
  • Analyse Spot warning signs that need urgent help.
  • Evaluate Judge a service for privacy, qualifications and fairness.
  • Create Draft a short, kind guide for your community.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Produce a one-page safe-use guide for online emotional-support services, with a checklist and an urgent-help card for your place.

Condition

A computer or phone, public information, two peers and a mentor. You do not give therapy or advice on treatment in this course.

Standard

Guide uses plain words; roles are not mixed up; urgent-help numbers are checked for your place and dated; no diagnosis or treatment advice appears; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Teachers, community leaders, HR staff, volunteers and curious learners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Three kinds of help, and who does what 2 hUnderstand

People often take a friend, a coach and a therapist to be the same thing. They are not, and mixing them up leads to bad choices. This module gives you a simple map so you can name each kind of help and say what it is for.

  • Peer support is shared experience between people with no formal training, such as a friends group or a community circle. It gives company and hope, not treatment.
  • Coaching focuses on goals, habits and skills, such as study routines or a career change. A coach does not diagnose or treat illness.
  • Therapy is assessment and treatment by a registered professional who is accountable to a recognised body. Only this kind treats mental-health conditions.
  • When in doubt about which you need, ask a doctor, a school counsellor or a helpline, and say clearly what is happening.
Worked example. A student feels low for three weeks and has trouble sleeping. Her class group chat is peer support, useful but not treatment. A study coach can help her plan revision. Because the low mood has lasted over two weeks and affects sleep, she books a registered counsellor through her college health centre.

Activity. Sort 10 short cases (given by your mentor) into peer support, coaching, therapy or urgent help, and write one sentence of reason for each.

Standard to meet. At least 9 of 10 sorted correctly and every reason names what the helper is trained to do.

Module 2 How online sessions work, step by step 2 hUnderstand

An online session is a normal appointment that happens through a screen or a phone. Knowing the steps reduces fear and prevents lost sessions.

  • Steps: find a service, check who runs it, book a time, agree consent and payment, join on time, talk, agree next steps.
  • Formats: text chat, voice call, video call, or messages left for a reply later. Each suits different people and moods.
  • Set-up: a private room, headphones, charged device, stable internet, a glass of water and a note of what you want to say.
  • Fall-back: agree before the session what happens if the line drops, for example a phone call within five minutes.
Worked example. A client books a video session for 6 pm. At 5:45 they test the link, tell their family they need privacy for an hour, and write three points on paper. The line drops at 6:20, and the counsellor phones the client within two minutes as agreed.

Activity. Write a pre-session checklist of at least six items and a fall-back plan for a failed connection.

Standard to meet. Checklist covers room, device, link test, privacy, notes and fall-back; fall-back names who calls whom and when.

Module 3 Checking qualifications and honesty 2 hAnalyse

Anyone can type "therapist" on a web page. Checking takes ten minutes and protects you or the person you advise.

  • Look for a named professional, a registration number and the register where it can be checked in your place.
  • A trustworthy service states its limits, such as that it is not for emergencies, and gives emergency contacts.
  • Red flags: guaranteed cure, pressure to pay up front for many sessions, no named staff, secret methods.
  • Record what you checked and the date, because services change.
Worked example. A site shows registration number 48213. The learner searches the public register for the profession in their country and finds the person, active, with a matching name and city. The site also lists a crisis number. Result: passes. A second site promises "cure anxiety in 3 days" and shows no names. Result: fails.

Activity. Check one real or practice service with the checklist and write a half-page finding with sources and the date.

Standard to meet. Each check has a source and date; the finding states pass or fail with reasons.

Module 4 Privacy, consent and data 2 hEvaluate

What you say about your feelings is some of the most private information you have. You should know where it goes.

  • Ask what is recorded, who can see it, how long it is kept, and whether it can be deleted.
  • Consent must be clear and can be withdrawn. Children and teenagers may need a guardian too, depending on the place.
  • Use a private device, a strong password and a locked screen. Do not use a shared or work computer.
  • Never share another person's story or screenshots without their agreement.
Worked example. A service keeps session notes for seven years in another country. The learner decides this does not suit them and asks for a service that keeps records locally for a shorter period, then keeps a note of the privacy answers.

Activity. Complete a five-question privacy review of one service and record the answers.

Standard to meet. All five questions answered from the service's own policy, with the page named.

Module 5 Warning signs and urgent help 2 hAnalyse

Some situations cannot wait for an appointment. Knowing them and acting early can save a life.

  • Warning signs: talk of wanting to die or self-harm, plans or means, hopelessness, sudden calm after distress, harm to others, abuse, severe confusion.
  • If someone is in immediate danger, call the local emergency number and stay with them; do not leave them alone.
  • Ask directly and kindly: "Are you thinking of ending your life?" Asking does not plant the idea.
  • After the crisis, help the person link to a registered professional and a trusted contact.
Worked example. A volunteer hears a friend say "I cannot do this any more" and mentions a plan. She stays on the call, asks the question directly, calls the emergency number from a second phone and waits with her friend until help arrives.

Activity. Make an urgent-help card with your local emergency number, a crisis line, and three trusted contacts, and check every number.

Standard to meet. Numbers verified for your place, dated, and tested by calling a non-emergency number to confirm it exists.

Module 6 Being a kind helper, within limits 2 hApply

Good helping is mostly listening. You are not there to fix, diagnose or promise.

  • Listen first: give full attention, do not interrupt, reflect back what you heard.
  • Name feelings gently ("That sounds exhausting") and ask what would help.
  • Do not diagnose, give medicine advice, or promise secrecy you cannot keep when safety is at risk.
  • Know when to hand over: say "I care about you and I want you to have proper help".
Worked example. A coach hears a client describe panic attacks. Instead of advising, he says "That sounds frightening. Have you spoken to a doctor?" and offers to help find an appointment.

Activity. Role-play two listening conversations of five minutes with a peer and collect their feedback.

Standard to meet. Peer reports they felt heard, no advice was forced, and the hand-over sentence was used.

Module 7 Fairness, cost and access 2 hEvaluate

Help that cannot be reached helps nobody. Language, money, disability and stigma all block people.

  • Check language options, text-only options, accessible formats and low-cost or free routes such as public clinics or helplines.
  • Respect culture, faith and gender preferences when matching a helper.
  • Never pressure anyone to join a service.
  • Offer to sit with the person while they make the first call.
Worked example. A community centre lists a free helpline in three languages, a low-cost clinic and a text option for people who cannot talk aloud at home.

Activity. List five accessible options for your area and check each one is real.

Standard to meet. Each option has a contact, hours, language and cost, and was verified by you.

Module 8 Write and test your community guide 2 hCreate

Bring the course together into one page that a neighbour could use tonight.

  • Structure: the three kinds of help, the safe-use checklist, the urgent-help card and where to get help in your language.
  • Use short sentences and large print.
  • Test with two readers who have not taken the course.
  • Plan to update the guide every six months.
Worked example. A school puts the guide on the notice board and asks a teacher to check the numbers each term.

Activity. Finish the guide and test it with two readers.

Standard to meet. Both readers can say the three kinds of help and where to call in an emergency.

ten locations and global

Your place

Mental-health rules and emergency numbers differ widely. Always confirm local numbers.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Global
