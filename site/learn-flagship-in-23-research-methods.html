flagship course

Research Methods and Applied Inquiry

Frame questions, collect and analyse evidence, and report findings clearly and ethically, so that decisions in a school, shop, clinic or small firm rest on data and not on guesses.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 39 h lessons plus practice

Frame questions, collect and analyse evidence, and report findings clearly and ethically, so that decisions in a school, shop, clinic or small firm rest on data and not on guesses.

  • Define a research question that can be answered with data.
  • Apply sampling and data collection methods.
  • Analyse data with simple counts, averages and charts.
  • Evaluate sources, bias and the limits of a study.
  • Create a ten-page report with a clear recommendation.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Complete a small applied study with at least 60 responses and write a ten-page report.

Condition

About 10 weeks part-time. A real local topic, a computer with a spreadsheet, a phone for calls, a mentor.

Standard

The question is answerable; the method and sample are described; consent is recorded; data is cleaned and analysed; limits are stated; every claim in the report links to a number.

Who it is for: Students, analysts, teachers, programme staff, shop and clinic managers. Requirements: Basic arithmetic and the ability to read a table. Materials: lesson text, templates, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Frame a question 4 hDefine
  • Start from a decision someone must make, then ask what evidence would help.
  • Write the question so it names who, what and where, for example: how many households in ward 12 buy water cans each week.
  • Split a broad topic into two or three small questions.
  • Reject questions that need opinion words such as best or good unless you define them.
Worked example. The broad topic better health becomes: What share of 40 shop workers in one market skip lunch on 3 or more days a week?

Activity. Rewrite three broad topics as answerable questions.

Standard to meet. Each question names the group, the measure and the place.

Module 2 Review existing evidence 4 hAnalyse
  • Search for official statistics, past surveys and local reports before collecting new data.
  • Note who wrote each source, when, and how they collected data.
  • Prefer recent sources with a stated method and sample size.
  • Keep a source table with title, year, finding and weakness.
Worked example. Of six sources found, two are older than 2015 and one has no sample size; three go into the source table.

Activity. Build a source table of six sources for your question.

Standard to meet. Each row has year, method, finding and one weakness.

Module 3 Choose a design and sample 5 hApply
  • Match the method to the question: counts need a survey, reasons need interviews, change needs a before and after.
  • Define the population and a sampling frame, such as a list of 400 customers.
  • Pick a random sample by drawing numbers, not the first people you meet.
  • Decide sample size: about 80 of 400 gives a rough but useful picture.
Worked example. From a list of 400 customers, draw every fifth name after a random start between 1 and 5 to get 80 names.

Activity. Write a one-page design with a sample plan.

Standard to meet. Population, sample size and selection method are stated.

Module 4 Build the instrument and consent 5 hCreate
  • Write each survey question with one idea and simple words.
  • Offer answer choices that do not overlap, such as 0, 1 to 2, 3 or more.
  • Pilot the form with five people and fix confusing items.
  • Prepare a consent note that explains purpose, voluntary taking part and how data is kept private.
Worked example. In a pilot of 5 people, 3 misread Question 4 about last week; it is reworded to name the dates.

Activity. Produce a 10-question survey and a consent note.

Standard to meet. Pilot changes are logged and consent wording is plain.

Module 5 Collect data carefully 5 hApply
  • Record each answer on the day in a table with an ID, not a name.
  • Check forms for blanks while the respondent is still present.
  • Keep a log of refusals and unreachable people.
  • Store files in a locked folder and delete phone numbers after the study.
Worked example. Of 80 names, 62 respond and 18 refuse or cannot be reached, a response rate of 62 divided by 80, about 78 percent.

Activity. Collect at least 60 responses and keep a response log.

Standard to meet. Response rate is calculated and the log lists non-responses.

Module 6 Clean and analyse data 6 hAnalyse
  • Remove duplicates, fix typing errors and mark missing values, keeping the raw file untouched.
  • Count each answer and turn counts into percentages.
  • Compute the average and the middle value; use the middle value when a few answers are very large.
  • Compare two groups with a simple table and a bar chart.
Worked example. Daily spend of 9 customers: 40, 45, 50, 50, 55, 60, 60, 65, 400. The mean is 91.7 but the median is 55, so report the median.

Activity. Analyse your data and make two charts.

Standard to meet. Raw file is kept, figures match a recount and charts have labels.

Module 7 Judge bias and limits 4 hEvaluate
  • List sources of bias: who was missed, who refused, how questions led.
  • State what the study cannot show, for example that two things moving together does not prove one causes the other.
  • Give a margin: with 62 answers a 50 percent result could reasonably be 12 points either way.
  • Check the findings against past evidence.
Worked example. 60 percent of 62 say yes; the true share could plausibly be 48 to 72 percent, so avoid saying a majority for sure.

Activity. Write a half-page limits section.

Standard to meet. At least three limits are named with their likely effect.

Module 8 Capstone: applied study report 6 hCreate
  • Write the ten-page report: question, method, results, limits, recommendation.
  • Lead with a one-paragraph summary of the answer.
  • Use plain words, labelled charts and a source list.
  • Present for 10 minutes and answer questions.
Worked example. A report finds 41 of 62 workers, 66 percent, skip lunch 3 or more days a week and recommends a 20-minute shaded break.

Activity. Submit the report and present it.

Standard to meet. Every claim links to a number, limits are stated and the recommendation follows the data.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: the work product from each module, kept in one folder with dates. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What makes a research question answerable?

Model answer: It names who, what and where, and can be answered with data you can collect. (Covered in module 1: Frame a question. To pass that module: Each question names the group, the measure and the place.)

Q2. Why read past evidence first?

Model answer: It saves effort, shows what is already known and sharpens your question. (Covered in module 2: Review existing evidence. To pass that module: Each row has year, method, finding and one weakness.)

Q3. Why prefer a random sample to asking whoever is nearby?

Model answer: It reduces selection bias so the sample looks more like the population. (Covered in module 3: Choose a design and sample. To pass that module: Population, sample size and selection method are stated.)

Q4. Why run a pilot?

Model answer: To find confusing questions before they spoil the real data. (Covered in module 4: Build the instrument and consent. To pass that module: Pilot changes are logged and consent wording is plain.)

Q5. What is a response rate?

Model answer: The share of chosen people who actually answered. (Covered in module 5: Collect data carefully. To pass that module: Response rate is calculated and the log lists non-responses.)

Q6. When is the median better than the mean?

Model answer: When a few extreme values pull the mean away from the typical case. (Covered in module 6: Clean and analyse data. To pass that module: Raw file is kept, figures match a recount and charts have labels.)

Q7. Why state limits in a report?

Model answer: So readers know how far to trust the findings. (Covered in module 7: Judge bias and limits. To pass that module: At least three limits are named with their likely effect.)

Q8. What proves a report is sound?

Model answer: Claims match the data, the method can be repeated and limits are honest. (Covered in module 8: Capstone: applied study report. To pass that module: Every claim links to a number, limits are stated and the recommendation follows the data.)

Q9. What is informed consent?

Model answer: Taking part freely after understanding the purpose and use of the data. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. Why keep the raw data file untouched?

Model answer: So any cleaning can be checked and repeated. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

seven levels

Your ladder, L1 to L7

This course sits at L4 Practitioner. Each level adds to the one below.

  1. L1 Basic. Read simple tables and charts.
  2. L2 Awareness. Explain a research question and a sample.
  3. L3 Foundation. Run a small survey under guidance.
  4. L4 Practitioner. Design and analyse a study alone.
  5. L5 Specialist. Judge bias and review the studies of others.
  6. L6 Advanced. Lead research for a team or programme.
  7. L7 Super expert. Set research standards and train others.

Slide by slide

Seven slides, one per level. Open each for the slide text and the speaker notes.

Slide 1 L1 Basic: Reading tables and charts

Read 5 simple tables and charts, stating the highest value, the lowest value and one clear finding for each without error.

Speaker notes. Teacher gives a bar chart of monthly sales and asks for the top month and the gap to the lowest. Covered in module 6, Clean and analyse data.

Slide 2 L2 Awareness: Question and sample

Explain a research question and a sample in plain words, and judge 3 samples as fair or biased with one reason each.

Speaker notes. Teacher offers a sample of only shop owners near the road and checks the learner spots the bias. Covered in modules 1 and 3.

Slide 3 L3 Foundation: Guided small survey

Run a survey of 20 people under guidance using a 8-question form, with consent noted and every answer entered in a sheet. Check local rules with an expert on data.

Speaker notes. Teacher checks the consent line on the form and the 20 complete rows. Covered in module 4, Build the instrument and consent.

Slide 4 L4 Practitioner: Own study and analysis

Design and run a study alone with a clear question, a sample of 30 or more and a table of results with averages and one chart.

Speaker notes. Teacher reruns one average from the raw file and checks it matches. Covered in module 6, Clean and analyse data.

Slide 5 L5 Specialist: Bias review of studies

Review 3 studies of others with a 8-point bias checklist and write which findings can be trusted, and why, in under 300 words.

Speaker notes. Teacher checks each study has a score and at least one limit named.

Slide 6 L6 Advanced: Research programme lead

Lead a team of 3 through a research programme with a plan, a task board and a data file kept clean, delivering a report by the agreed date.

Speaker notes. Teacher checks the raw data is unchanged and the cleaning steps are logged.

Slide 7 L7 Super expert: Research standards training

Write a standards guide covering consent, data storage and reporting, and train 5 researchers until each passes a 10-point audit of their own study.

Speaker notes. Teacher audits one study against the guide. Check local rules with an expert on data.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local rules change licences, data handling, pricing words and names of bodies. Check them for your place before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
