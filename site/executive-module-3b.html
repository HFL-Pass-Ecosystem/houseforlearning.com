Module 3B: Escalation, attribution bias and transnational mediation

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 3B

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 16 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Memo 30%
  • Live mediation 35%
  • Joint governance brief 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task

Spot attribution bias and escalation early in a cross-border dispute and run a mediation that ends in a written joint plan.

Condition

Given a dispute case with two parties, a timeline of events and costs, and a mediator brief.

Standard

The memo names the bias and escalation stage correctly, the mediation follows a set order, and the joint plan has owners, dates and a cost split that adds up.

Who it is for: Leaders who handle disputes between teams, partners or suppliers in different countries.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 How disputes grow: the stages of escalation 9 hUnderstand: describe and place

Disputes grow in steps, from a difference of opinion to a fight over survival. The earlier you place the stage, the cheaper the fix.

  • Stage 1: talk and disagree
  • Stage 2: positions harden and sides form
  • Stage 3: threats and damage

Worked example: A supplier and a buyer dispute a late delivery. Week 1: emails about the date (stage 1). Week 3: each copies senior managers and blames the other (stage 2). Week 6: the buyer withholds a 90,000 payment and the supplier stops shipping (stage 3). Cost of fixing at week 1: about 5 hours of talk. At week 6: 90,000 frozen plus lost output of 2 weeks.

Activity: Read a case timeline and mark the stage at each date with the sign that shows it.

Standard: Every date has the correct stage and one named sign.

Part 2 Attribution bias: why we blame character not context 9 hUnderstand and apply: explain and test

When others fail, we blame their character. When we fail, we blame the situation. Both sides do this, so each thinks the other is at fault.

  • We explain our lateness by traffic
  • We explain theirs by laziness
  • Test the cause with a list of context facts

Worked example: Team X is 3 days late. Team Y says 'they are careless'. Context list: Team X got specification changes on day 2 and day 6, lost 2 staff to illness, and works on a public holiday cycle of 4 days. Estimated delay from context = 2 days for the two specification changes + 1 day for the staff illness + 4 days for the holiday cycle = 7 days of lost time, more than the 3 days they were late. The data shows context, not carelessness, explains the delay.

Activity: For a case, list the blame statements and replace each with a list of context facts and an estimate of their effect.

Standard: At least three blame statements converted, each with a context fact and a time or cost estimate.

Part 3 Cultural meanings of conflict, face and honour 9 hAnalyse: compare

In some places open argument shows honesty; in others it causes loss of face. The same act can mean different things.

  • Public disagreement may harm standing
  • Private talk may save the relationship
  • Apology may mean respect, not guilt

Worked example: A manager openly corrects a partner's error in a meeting of 12 people. In a face-sensitive setting, the partner loses standing in front of 12 witnesses. Cost estimate: partner stops sharing early warnings, so 1 problem in 4 now surfaces late, adding about 2 weeks each time. Alternative: a private note the same day, correction agreed in 1 conversation.

Activity: Rewrite a public correction as a private one and list what each side gains or loses.

Standard: A private version with the same facts and at least two gains and losses per side.

Part 4 Reading early warning signs 9 hAnalyse: detect

Before a dispute blows up, signs appear: slower replies, more people copied, formal language, new demands.

  • Track reply time
  • Count people copied
  • Watch for a switch to formal or legal wording

Worked example: Reply times in days: week 1, 1; week 2, 2; week 3, 4; week 4, 7. Reply time rose from 1 day to 7 days. People copied: 2, 2, 5, 9. A rise of 7 copied persons in 3 weeks. Rule: if reply time doubles twice and copies triple, call a meeting. Here replies doubled twice by week 3 and copies reached triple (6 or more) only in week 4, when 9 were copied, so by this rule the call was due in week 4.

Activity: Plot reply time and copy count over six weeks from given data and mark the trigger week.

Standard: Correct trigger week and the rule is stated with numbers.

Part 5 Mediator role and neutral ground 9 hApply: plan

A mediator helps both sides talk and does not decide. We choose who, where and how, so neither side feels outnumbered.

  • Choose a person both trust
  • Pick neutral place and language
  • Set ground rules in writing

Worked example: Party A is in a city 8 time zones from party B. Choose a video call at a slot that costs each side equal pain: A at 08:00, B at 16:00. Language: neither party's first language, plain English with a written summary after each hour. 3 sessions of 90 minutes = 4.5 hours, against an estimated 2 weeks of lost trade worth 40,000 if unresolved.

Activity: Prepare a mediation plan with place, time, language, rules and a written agenda.

Standard: The plan names each choice, shows equal burden on the two sides, and states the cost of no agreement.

Part 6 Running a mediation in a set order 10 hApply: facilitate

A fixed order keeps a heated meeting on track: opening, each side's story, shared issues, options, agreement.

  • Each side speaks without interruption
  • Restate the other's view before answering
  • List issues, then options, then choose

Worked example: Time plan, 90 minutes: opening 10, party A story 15, party B story 15, restate each other 10, issue list 10, options 20, agreement 10 = 90 minutes. Issues listed: late payment 90,000, delivery plan, price review. Options: pay 60,000 now and 30,000 on delivery; weekly status call. Agreed in 90 minutes.

Activity: Run a 90-minute role-play mediation with a time plan and record the issues and options.

Standard: The agenda adds to 90 minutes, all steps happen in order, and issues and options are written down.

Part 7 Splitting cost and drafting the joint plan 10 hCreate: write

The plan turns talk into action: who does what by when, and who pays what.

  • Each action has an owner and a date
  • Cost split is shown in numbers
  • A review date is set

Worked example: Total dispute cost 150,000: 90,000 frozen payment, 40,000 extra freight, 20,000 lost output. The two sides agree cause shares in the session: supplier 45% = 67,500 and buyer 55% = 82,500. Check: 67,500 + 82,500 = 150,000. Actions: supplier ships by 14 June, buyer pays 60,000 by 20 June, review on 1 July.

Activity: Write a one-page joint plan from a case with a cost split, actions, owners and dates.

Standard: Cost shares add to the total and every action has an owner and date.

Part 8 Joint governance so the dispute does not return 10 hEvaluate: judge and design

The last step is a simple rule set for the future: how to raise issues early and who meets when.

  • Set an early-warning trigger
  • Name a joint contact on each side
  • Review quarterly

Worked example: Rule: if a delivery is more than 2 days late, the contacts call within 1 day. Escalate to directors only if the call fails. Quarterly review of 4 numbers: late deliveries, payment days, open issues, cost of rework. Last year 9 late deliveries of 120 = 7.5%. Target for next year: 4%, which is 5 of 120.

Activity: Write a one-page governance brief with triggers, contacts and four numbers for review, and present it in 5 minutes.

Standard: Triggers have numbers, review numbers have a baseline and target, and the brief is delivered within 5 minutes.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. Week 1 emails about a date, week 3 managers copied and blame, week 6 payment withheld. Name the three stages.
    Week 1 is stage 1 (disagree), week 3 stage 2 (sides form), week 6 stage 3 (threats and damage). (Covered in part 1: How disputes grow: the stages of escalation. To pass that part: Every date has the correct stage and one named sign.)
  2. Why is it cheaper to deal with a dispute at stage 1?
    Only a few hours of talk are needed, with nothing frozen or lost yet. (Covered in part 1: How disputes grow: the stages of escalation. To pass that part: Every date has the correct stage and one named sign.)
  3. Context facts add up to 7 days of delay and the team was 3 days late. What does this suggest?
    Context, not carelessness, explains the delay. (Covered in part 2: Attribution bias: why we blame character not context. To pass that part: At least three blame statements converted, each with a context fact and a time or cost estimate.)
  4. What is attribution bias in one sentence?
    Blaming other people's character for failures but our own situation for ours. (Covered in part 2: Attribution bias: why we blame character not context. To pass that part: At least three blame statements converted, each with a context fact and a time or cost estimate.)
  5. Why can a public correction before 12 people be costly in a face-sensitive setting?
    The partner loses standing and may hide later problems, so issues surface late. (Covered in part 3: Cultural meanings of conflict, face and honour. To pass that part: A private version with the same facts and at least two gains and losses per side.)
  6. Reply times were 1, 2, 4, 7 days. In which week had replies doubled twice?
    Week 3, since 1 to 2 to 4. (Covered in part 4: Reading early warning signs. To pass that part: Correct trigger week and the rule is stated with numbers.)
  7. Why choose a call slot of 08:00 for A and 16:00 for B?
    The pain of the hour is shared equally, which supports fairness. (Covered in part 5: Mediator role and neutral ground. To pass that part: The plan names each choice, shows equal burden on the two sides, and states the cost of no agreement.)
  8. A 90-minute plan has 10, 15, 15, 10, 10, 20 and a last block. How long is the last block?
    90 - 80 = 10 minutes. (Covered in part 6: Running a mediation in a set order. To pass that part: The agenda adds to 90 minutes, all steps happen in order, and issues and options are written down.)
  9. A 150,000 cost is split 45% and 55%. What are the amounts?
    67,500 and 82,500. (Covered in part 7: Splitting cost and drafting the joint plan. To pass that part: Cost shares add to the total and every action has an owner and date.)
  10. Nine of 120 deliveries were late. What is the rate and what is the 4% target in deliveries?
    7.5%; 4% of 120 = 4.8, about 5. (Covered in part 8: Joint governance so the dispute does not return. To pass that part: Triggers have numbers, review numbers have a baseline and target, and the brief is delivered within 5 minutes.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Disputes grow in steps. A dispute moves from talk to hardened sides to damage. Early action is cheap. Speaker note: Say: draw the three steps. Check: place a short story. Mistake: waiting for a formal complaint.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Blame and context. We explain our failures by context and theirs by character. Test by listing the context facts. Speaker note: Say: give a late-train story. Check: learners list facts. Mistake: treating the list as an excuse.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Face and honour. Public correction can cost trust. A private route often keeps the facts and the relationship. Speaker note: Say: compare two versions. Check: same facts in both. Mistake: thinking private means soft.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Early signs. Reply time, copies and formal language show a dispute before it explodes. Set a rule with numbers. Speaker note: Say: plot the numbers. Check: find trigger week. Mistake: acting only after payment is stopped.
  5. Slide 5: L5 The mediator. A mediator does not decide. Fairness in place, time and language matters. Speaker note: Say: ask who would be trusted. Check: plan fairness. Mistake: choosing a friend of one side.
  6. Slide 6: L6 The mediation order. Opening, each story, restate, issues, options, agreement. Keep to time. Speaker note: Say: use a visible clock. Check: restate step is done. Mistake: skipping the restate.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Plan and governance. A joint plan has actions, owners, dates, cost split and review rules. Speaker note: Say: show a plan that adds up. Check: cost shares sum to total. Mistake: no review date.
