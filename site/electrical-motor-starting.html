Starting a motor: normal and heavy duty

A motor draws far more current for a few seconds when it starts. This lesson explains what makes a start heavy and why that changes the circuit.

Learning material, not a design or safety instruction. Electrical work must be done by qualified people following the maker's instructions and local rules.

The surge at start

When a motor is switched straight onto the supply, called direct-on-line starting, it can draw about six times its running current while it speeds up.

Normal protection is chosen so that this short surge does not trip it. The motor is up to speed in a few seconds and the current falls to its running value.

What makes a start heavy

  • Three things describe a start: how long it takes, how high the current is, and how often it is repeated.
  • As a teaching guide (example values), a start is heavy when it lasts roughly 5 to 30 seconds, or when the current is more than six times running current, or both.
  • Heavy loads usually have a lot of spinning mass: large fans, centrifuges and similar machines. Ask the owner of the machine, because the supplier of the motor knows its starting data.

Why it matters for the circuit

A long start heats cables and contacts much more than the running current does.

The overload protection must be chosen so it copes with a normal start without nuisance tripping while still protecting the motor, and the rest of the main circuit must be chosen for the longer, hotter start: contacts, wiring and protection. A qualified person makes these choices from the motor maker's data.

Repeated starts

Every start adds heat. The number of starts per hour is limited, and the limit assumes a standard ambient temperature.

Between starts the cables and switching devices must cool. If the machine needs frequent starts, treat it as a separate design case.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

  • Task. Tell a normal motor start from a heavy one and explain what a heavy start changes in the circuit design.
  • Condition. Given running current, start time and start current figures for a motor, with design and any work done only by qualified people following local rules and the motor maker's data.
  • Standard. The learner classifies a start as normal or heavy from its time and current, and names the circuit parts that must be chosen for the longer, hotter start.

four parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1: The surge at start (15 min, Understand: Describe)

A stopped motor draws a very large current, because it has not yet built up speed. That is why the current jumps at the start and falls once it is up to speed.

  • With direct-on-line starting, current can reach about six times the running value.
  • The surge lasts only as long as it takes the motor to speed up, usually a few seconds.
  • Normal protection is chosen so this brief surge does not trip it.

Worked example. A motor draws 18 A when running. At a direct-on-line start it can draw about 6 times that, so 6 × 18 = 108 A. If it reaches full speed in 4 seconds, the circuit sees 108 A for only 4 seconds and then 18 A. A supply that copes with 18 A all day must also cope with 108 A for 4 seconds. Check the real figure with the motor maker, since 6 is a typical guide only.

Activity. Take three running currents from motor labels you can read about (for example 7 A, 18 A, 42 A). Multiply each by 6 and write the likely start current.

Standard. Learner multiplies running current by the guide factor and states the surge duration idea correctly.

Part 2: What makes a start heavy (15 min, Apply: Classify)

Three things describe a start: how long, how high and how often. A start is heavy when time or current go past the normal range.

  • As a teaching guide (example values), a start is heavy if it lasts roughly 5 to 30 seconds, or the current is above six times running current, or both.
  • Heavy loads often have a lot of spinning mass, such as large fans and centrifuges.
  • Ask the machine owner and the motor maker for starting data instead of guessing.

Worked example. Start A: 4 s at 6 times running current. It is under 5 s and not over 6 times, so it counts as a normal start. Start B: 22 s at 5 times running current. The time is inside the 5 to 30 s range, so it is heavy. Start C: 8 s at 7 times running current. The current is over 6 times, so it is heavy. So out of three starts, two are heavy. Note that Start B is heavy even though its current is below 6 times.

Activity. Make a table of four invented starts with time and current multiple. Mark each normal or heavy and write the reason.

Standard. Learner classifies each start by the time and current rules and gives the reason.

Part 3: Why it matters for the circuit (15 min, Analyse: Compare)

Heat in a conductor grows with the square of the current and with time. A long start is far hotter than a short one at the same current.

  • Heating is roughly proportional to current squared times time.
  • The overload protection must be chosen so it copes with the normal start without nuisance tripping while still protecting the motor, and the contacts, wiring and protection must be chosen to match. A qualified person does this from the maker's data.
  • A longer start therefore asks more of every part in the main circuit.

Worked example. Use the 108 A start current. For 4 s the heating figure is 108 × 108 × 4 = 46,656. For 20 s it is 108 × 108 × 20 = 233,280. Divide: 233,280 ÷ 46,656 = 5. So the 20 s start puts five times the heat into the same cable and contacts. The figures are relative numbers for comparison, not real temperatures. This is why a heavy start needs its own design choices.

Activity. Compare two starts of your choice with different times at the same current. Calculate the ratio of their heating figures and write one sentence on what it means.

Standard. Learner computes the ratio correctly and links it to stronger parts for a heavy start.

Part 4: Repeated starts (15 min, Evaluate: Judge)

Each start adds heat, and parts need time to cool. A machine that starts often is a special case.

  • The allowed number of starts per hour is limited and assumes a standard room temperature.
  • Between starts, cables and switching devices must cool down.
  • Frequent starts or a hot room call for a separate design check with the maker.

Worked example. A fan starts 3 times an hour, each start 20 s at 108 A, heating figure 233,280 each. Total for the hour is 3 × 233,280 = 699,840. A second fan on a different duty starts 12 times an hour with 4 s starts, heating 46,656 each. Total is 12 × 46,656 = 559,872. So the first fan, with fewer starts, puts more heat into the circuit per hour than the second. Count the total, do not just count starts.

Activity. Invent a machine that starts 6 times an hour. Compute its hourly heating figure for a 10 s start at 80 A and say whether it needs a special check.

Standard. Learner totals heat per hour for repeated starts and decides if a special design check is needed.

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Why the lights dip. Simple picture of a large fan starting and a lamp flickering briefly. Speaker note: Open with the everyday sign of a big start. Ask what might cause the brief dip. Keep it concrete.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Explain: Six times running. Graph showing a tall spike falling to a flat line over a few seconds. Speaker note: Explain the surge. Work 6 × 18 = 108 A on the board. Say that six is a guide and the maker data is the source.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Apply: Normal or heavy. Table of three starts: 4 s at 6 times, 22 s at 5 times, 8 s at 7 times. Speaker note: Have learners classify each. Point out that the 22 s start is heavy even with lower current.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Analyse: Heat grows with time. Bar chart of 46,656 and 233,280 heating figures with a ratio of five. Speaker note: Show the working. Stress these are relative figures only. Ask what parts get hotter.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Design: What must change. A circuit sketch with overload setting, contacts and wiring labelled and question marks. Speaker note: Learners list which parts must be chosen for the long start. Remind them that the design and any work belongs to qualified people following local rules.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Evaluate: Repeated starts. Two fans: 3 long starts an hour and 12 short starts an hour. Speaker note: Compare totals 699,840 and 559,872. Ask which is the harder duty and why. Discuss the limit on starts per hour.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Create: Duty sheet. Blank sheet: machine, running A, start time, start multiple, starts per hour, verdict. Speaker note: Each learner fills a sheet for an invented machine and swaps with a partner to check. End by naming who to ask: machine owner and motor maker.

where it leads

Jobs this supports

  • Controls engineer. Chooses starting methods and protection for motors on plant and machines.
  • Maintenance electrician. Keeps motor circuits healthy and spots signs of overheating from heavy duty.
  • Mechanical engineer. Specifies fans, pumps and machines and supplies the data that sets the start type.
  • Commissioning technician. Proves that a motor circuit starts and runs as designed, following the site rules.
