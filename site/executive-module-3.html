executive programme

Module 3: Cross-cultural communication and decentering

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 3

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 15 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Audit memo 30%
  • Live channel simulation 35%
  • Bilateral video brief 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Send and receive clear messages across languages, time zones and communication styles, and fix a message that was misunderstood.

Condition

Given real or case messages, a meeting plan with people in three time zones, and a partner who speaks a second language.

Standard

Messages are rewritten to a stated plain-English target, meeting slots are correct, and a misunderstanding is traced to its cause and repaired.

Who it is for: Managers and team leaders who work daily with colleagues and partners in other countries.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 Decentering: seeing your own assumptions 9 hUnderstand: identify and explain

Decentering means stepping out of your own way of seeing to look at a situation as the other person might. It starts with naming what you take for granted.

  • List what you assume is normal
  • Ask what the other person might assume
  • Test one assumption each week

Worked example: A manager lists 10 assumptions about meetings: start on time, agenda sent, everyone speaks, decisions made in the room, and so on. She asks a colleague from abroad to mark which hold for her team. 6 of 10 do not. She now writes the start time, agenda, decision method and the other differing rules at the top of every invitation, so 6 hidden rules become written rules.

Activity: List ten assumptions you make about work meetings, ask someone from another background to mark them, and rewrite the invitation.

Standard: Ten assumptions listed, at least three flagged as different, and an invitation that states those rules.

Part 2 Listening across accents and speaking levels 9 hApply: adapt speech

A listener working in a second language needs more time. We can change speed, pauses and word choice without sounding childish.

  • Slow down and pause between ideas
  • Avoid idioms and slang
  • Check understanding by asking for a summary

Worked example: A native speaker talks at 160 words per minute. A listener in a second language copes well at about 120. A 6-minute talk at 160 wpm holds 960 words; at 120 wpm it takes 960 / 120 = 8 minutes. Two extra minutes buys understanding. Replace 'let us touch base and circle back' (7 words, idiom) with 'let us meet again on Friday' (6 words, clear).

Activity: Record a 2-minute talk, count the words per minute and rewrite it with no idioms at 120 wpm.

Standard: Speed calculated correctly, no idioms left, and a partner can restate the main points.

Part 3 Writing plain English that translates 10 hApply: rewrite

Writing for readers in a second language, or for a translation tool, needs short sentences and one idea at a time.

  • Aim for 15 words or fewer in a sentence
  • One idea per sentence
  • Use the same word for the same thing

Worked example: Original: 'Should the shipment not arrive by the agreed date, notwithstanding any earlier delay, we may need to consider alternative arrangements.' That is 20 words. Rewrite: 'If the shipment is late, we will find another route. The new date is 12 May.' Two sentences, 10 and 6 words. Average sentence length falls from 20 to 8 words.

Activity: Take a 150-word email and rewrite it so the average sentence length is 15 words or fewer.

Standard: Average sentence length of 15 or fewer words, counted by the learner, with no change in meaning.

Part 4 Meeting design across time zones 9 hApply: schedule

A fair meeting time shares the pain. We find overlap and rotate who meets at a bad hour.

  • Convert working hours to one clock
  • Find the overlap
  • Rotate the early and late slots

Worked example: Team A at UTC+0, team B at UTC+8, team C at UTC-5. Everyone works 09:00 to 17:00 local. UTC is the world reference clock. A = 09:00-17:00 UTC. B = 01:00-09:00 UTC. C = 14:00-22:00 UTC. A and B only touch at 09:00 UTC, so they overlap 0 hours. A and C overlap 14:00-17:00 UTC, 3 hours. All three never overlap. Plan: no hour suits all three, so hold two meetings. A and C meet at 14:00-17:00 UTC. A and B meet for one hour: 08:00-09:00 UTC one week (A starts an hour early) and 09:00-10:00 UTC the next (B stays an hour late).

Activity: Given three offices, find all overlaps and set a rotating schedule for a weekly meeting.

Standard: All overlaps correct in one clock and a rotation that shares late or early slots equally.

Part 5 Giving and receiving feedback across styles 9 hAnalyse: compare and adapt

Some people want feedback straight; others expect it softened or given in private. We learn to read the style and choose a form.

  • Say what, why and next step
  • Choose private or group by style
  • Check how it landed

Worked example: Direct version: 'The report has three errors in the totals.' Indirect version: 'The report is nearly ready. May we look at the totals together? Three figures on page 4 may need a second check.' Same facts: 3 errors, page 4. Test: after both, the receiver states what to fix. If they can name 3 errors from the indirect one, it worked; if only 1, move the 3 errors into a short list.

Activity: Rewrite one piece of critical feedback in two styles and test which one a partner can restate fully.

Standard: Both versions contain every fact and the partner can restate all of them from at least one version.

Part 6 Working with interpreters and translation tools 9 hApply: plan and check

Interpreters and tools help, but they change timing and meaning. We plan for both and check key points.

  • Brief the interpreter beforehand
  • Allow extra time
  • Check key numbers by writing them down

Worked example: A 60-minute meeting with consecutive interpreting takes about 1.6 times as long: 60 x 1.6 = 96 minutes. A one-hour slot would cut the agenda to about 37.5 minutes of real talk (60 / 1.6). Decision: cut the agenda from 6 items of 10 minutes to 3 items (30 minutes of talk). Write key numbers on a shared sheet: price 4,250 (in your currency) and date 18 June, so nothing depends on hearing alone.

Activity: Plan a 60-minute interpreted meeting with a cut agenda, a one-page brief and a list of numbers to confirm in writing.

Standard: Time is recalculated correctly, the agenda is cut to fit, and all numbers appear in writing.

Part 7 Choosing the channel: video, message or call 10 hAnalyse: choose

The wrong channel causes rework. Match the task to the channel by how much nuance, speed and record it needs.

  • Message for facts and records
  • Call for conflict and nuance
  • Video when faces help trust

Worked example: Task: change a delivery date. Facts only, needs a record: written message, 2 minutes. Task: a late-delivery dispute. Needs tone and nuance: video call, 20 minutes. Rework cost check: a vague message about the dispute caused 3 follow-up emails at 10 minutes each = 30 minutes and a delay of 2 days, so the call saves 10 minutes and 2 days.

Activity: Choose and justify the channel for six tasks and estimate time and rework for each.

Standard: Six choices with reasons, times estimated, and at least one rework cost calculated.

Part 8 Producing the bilateral video brief 10 hCreate: produce

A short video brief for a partner abroad pulls together everything above: plain words, pace, structure and a clear request.

  • Three minutes, one request
  • Subtitles in plain English
  • End with a check question

Worked example: Plan a 3-minute brief at 120 words per minute: 3 x 120 = 360 words. Structure: 30 words on purpose, 200 words on three points, 80 words on the request, 50 words on the date and check question. Total 360. The script is tested on a colleague, who restates 3 of 3 points and the date.

Activity: Write, record and test a 3-minute brief with subtitles for a partner in another country.

Standard: Length within 10 seconds of 3 minutes, one clear request, and a listener restates all main points.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. A talk of 960 words is given at 120 words per minute. How long does it take?
    960 / 120 = 8 minutes. (Covered in part 2: Listening across accents and speaking levels. To pass that part: Speed calculated correctly, no idioms left, and a partner can restate the main points.)
  2. Why replace 'let us touch base and circle back' with 'let us meet again on Friday'?
    The idioms may not translate; the plain version has a clear action and day. (Covered in part 2: Listening across accents and speaking levels. To pass that part: Speed calculated correctly, no idioms left, and a partner can restate the main points.)
  3. A sentence of 20 words is split into 10 and 6 words. What is the new average?
    8 words. (Covered in part 3: Writing plain English that translates. To pass that part: Average sentence length of 15 or fewer words, counted by the learner, with no change in meaning.)
  4. Name the main step of decentering.
    List your own assumptions, then ask how the other person might see it differently. (Covered in part 1: Decentering: seeing your own assumptions. To pass that part: Ten assumptions listed, at least three flagged as different, and an invitation that states those rules.)
  5. A manager lists 10 meeting assumptions and 6 do not hold for a colleague. How many hidden rules should she write down?
    Six. (Covered in part 1: Decentering: seeing your own assumptions. To pass that part: Ten assumptions listed, at least three flagged as different, and an invitation that states those rules.)
  6. Office A works 09:00-17:00 at UTC+0 and office C works 09:00-17:00 at UTC-5. How long is the overlap?
    C is 14:00-22:00 UTC, so the overlap is 14:00-17:00, 3 hours. (Covered in part 4: Meeting design across time zones. To pass that part: All overlaps correct in one clock and a rotation that shares late or early slots equally.)
  7. Why rotate the early or late meeting slot?
    So the cost of a bad hour is shared and no team always carries it. (Covered in part 4: Meeting design across time zones. To pass that part: All overlaps correct in one clock and a rotation that shares late or early slots equally.)
  8. How do you test whether indirect feedback worked?
    Ask the receiver to restate what needs fixing; if they miss items, make the list clearer. (Covered in part 5: Giving and receiving feedback across styles. To pass that part: Both versions contain every fact and the partner can restate all of them from at least one version.)
  9. A 60-minute meeting uses an interpreter at 1.6 times as long. How much real talk fits in a 60-minute slot?
    60 / 1.6 = 37.5 minutes. (Covered in part 6: Working with interpreters and translation tools. To pass that part: Time is recalculated correctly, the agenda is cut to fit, and all numbers appear in writing.)
  10. How many words fit in a 3-minute video at 120 words per minute?
    360 words. (Covered in part 8: Producing the bilateral video brief. To pass that part: Length within 10 seconds of 3 minutes, one clear request, and a listener restates all main points.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: What decentering is. Decentering means seeing the situation from the other person's side. It begins with noticing your own assumptions. Speaker note: Say: tell a story where both sides were right in their own frame. Check: ask for one assumption. Mistake: thinking it means agreeing.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Speed, pause and word choice. A second-language listener needs about 120 words a minute, clear words and short pauses. Speaker note: Say: demo a fast and a slow version. Check: time a 100-word passage. Mistake: speaking louder instead of slower.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Plain writing. Short sentences, one idea each, the same word for the same thing. Aim for 15 words or fewer. Speaker note: Say: rewrite a long sentence live. Check: count average length. Mistake: removing needed facts.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Time zones and fair meetings. Put all hours on one clock, find the overlap, rotate the pain. Speaker note: Say: draw three bars on a board. Check: find overlap of two zones. Mistake: forgetting daylight changes.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Feedback and channels. Choose style and channel by the nuance needed and test that it landed. Speaker note: Say: compare two versions of one message. Check: restate test. Mistake: hiding the fact in soft words.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Interpreters and tools. Brief the interpreter, allow 1.6 times the time, write key numbers down. Speaker note: Say: show a number misheard. Check: recalc the agenda. Mistake: treating a tool as a final truth.
  7. Slide 7: L7 The bilateral brief. Three minutes, one request, plain words, subtitles and a check question. Speaker note: Say: play a good and a poor example. Check: listener restates. Mistake: trying to cover too much.
