Online Education

Run a real online Education pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small online Education offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: health, care, wellness and education. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know online learning 6 hUnderstand

Online education means teaching and learning through the internet. Learn the main forms and the words.

  • Name the forms: live class, recorded lessons, self-paced course, coaching, community group.
  • Describe the parts of a course: goal, lessons, practice, feedback, check.
  • Explain the difference between live and self-paced learning.
  • List words: outcome, module, assessment, rubric, learner.
  • Compare the cost, time and support of live, recorded and self-paced learning for one learner.
  • Identify what a learner needs at home: a device, a quiet space and internet.
  • Analyze one real course page and mark its goal, lessons and check.
Worked example. A short course on basic budgeting has 4 recorded lessons of 8 minutes, 4 practice tasks of 15 minutes and a live question hour each week. The learner's work is 4 x 8 + 4 x 15 = 92 minutes, about 1.5 hours, plus the weekly live hour.

Activity. Take one free short online lesson. Write what helped you learn and what did not.

Standard to meet. Task: name parts of an online course. Condition: from one real lesson. Standard: five parts named with one example each.

Module 2 Know your learner 6 hApply

Good teaching starts with knowing who will learn, what they know and what they need.

  • Describe your learner: age, work, language, devices, time, internet access.
  • Ask three learners what they want to do after the course.
  • Check for barriers: cost, time zone, disability, low bandwidth, language.
  • Explain why plain words and clear steps help every learner.
  • Write a short survey of five questions to learn goals and limits before you build.
  • Identify what the learner can already do, using a short pre-check.
  • Plan for different reading levels and languages with plain words and a glossary.
Worked example. Learners are shift workers with phones and weak internet. The course uses short lessons, text and low-size audio, with a printable page. If a video lesson is 50 MB and an audio lesson is 5 MB, audio uses one tenth of the data.

Activity. Interview three possible learners and write a one-page learner profile.

Standard to meet. Task: write a learner profile. Condition: three interviews. Standard: needs, barriers and goals written with real quotes.

Module 3 Follow the rules in Canada 10 hApply

Education, consumer, privacy and copyright rules apply. Check with the local authority.

  • Explain that education is a provincial matter; some private training must be registered or approved; check your province.
  • Describe consumer protection for prices, refunds and honest claims.
  • Apply privacy rules for learner data and, for minors, extra care and parent consent.
  • Check copyright: use your own work or material you have permission or a licence to use.
  • Identify whether your course counts as private training under your province's rule; check the provincial rule.
  • Describe where to store learner data safely and how long to keep it; check the privacy rule.
  • Check accessibility duties for online services in your province.
Worked example. Rules table row: Private training approval | provincial education body | check if your course type needs registration | before selling | written reply kept. A second row: Learner data | privacy regulator | collect only what you need and tell learners why | at sign-up | notice kept. Both rows get a checked date.

Activity. Make a rules table covering approval, consumer rules, privacy and copyright for your province.

Standard to meet. Task: map the rules. Condition: official pages only. Standard: each row has an enforcer, an action and a checked date.

Module 4 Design a course with clear outcomes 8 hEvaluate

Start with what learners will be able to do, then build lessons and checks to match.

  • Write 4 to 6 outcomes with action verbs such as explain, apply, build, check.
  • Build a course map: outcome, lesson, practice, check.
  • Apply the rule: one main idea per lesson and one task per idea.
  • Compare your plan with a real task a learner will do after the course.
  • Sequence lessons from easy to hard.
  • Match one practice task to each outcome.
  • Estimate total learner time and compare it with the time learners have.
Worked example. Outcome: apply a monthly budget. Lesson: income and costs. Practice: fill a sheet from a sample case. Check: a short quiz and a review of the sheet. With 5 outcomes and 15 minutes of lesson plus practice for each, the course is 5 x 15 = 75 minutes.

Activity. Build a course map for a topic you know and ask two people to find gaps.

Standard to meet. Task: design a course map. Condition: one topic. Standard: every outcome has a lesson, a practice and a check.

Module 5 Make clear lessons and tools 8 hApply

Short, clear lessons beat long ones. Use simple tools you can manage.

  • Build a lesson: aim, short explanation, example, task, feedback.
  • Apply plain words, short sentences and one picture per idea.
  • Check accessibility: captions or text, readable colours, keyboard use, low data.
  • Choose simple tools for video, documents and quizzes; test on a phone.
  • Write a model answer for each task.
  • Record clear audio in a quiet room and check the sound on a phone speaker.
  • Make a printable one-page summary for each lesson.
Worked example. A five-minute lesson: one aim, a two-minute explanation, one worked example, a task and a model answer shown after the learner tries. If the explanation takes 2 minutes and the example 1, the task and model answer share the last 2 minutes.

Activity. Build one lesson with a task and test it with three learners on their own devices.

Standard to meet. Task: build a lesson. Condition: for a phone. Standard: three learners complete the task and say what the aim was.

Module 6 Teach, give feedback and check learning 6 hCreate

People learn by doing and by feedback. Checking must match the outcome.

  • Apply feedback: say what worked, what to change, and the next step.
  • Build a simple rubric with three levels for each task.
  • Evaluate checks: do they test the outcome or only memory?
  • Explain academic honesty and how to handle copied work.
  • Set a response time for questions, such as one working day.
  • Collect feedback from learners with two questions: what helped, and what to change.
  • Use results to revise the weakest lesson.
Worked example. Rubric row: Budget sheet | Not yet: totals wrong | Meets: totals right | Strong: totals right and a plan to cut one cost. A sheet with income 2,000 and costs 1,850 has a surplus of 150. If the totals are right it meets the standard, and a plan to cut one cost makes it strong.

Activity. Mark three practice submissions with your rubric and write feedback for each.

Standard to meet. Task: give feedback. Condition: three submissions. Standard: each feedback names a strength, a change and a next step.

Module 7 Price, sell and support 6 hEvaluate

Learners pay for results and trust. Price fairly and be clear about what is included.

  • Compute cost per learner: time, tools, support, payment charges, tax.
  • Compare pricing: one fee, monthly fee, free with paid support.
  • Write honest claims about results; never promise jobs or income.
  • Track the funnel: visitors, sign-ups, starters, finishers.
  • Write a clear refund and cancellation rule in plain words; check the consumer rule in your province.
  • Calculate the completion rate: finishers divided by starters.
  • List what is and is not included, such as live help hours.
Worked example. Price 65 per hour of live help, direct cost 0. If fixed costs are 500 a month, break-even is 500 / 65 = 7.7, so 8 hours. If 20 learners start and 12 finish, the completion rate is 12 / 20 = 60%, below an aim of 75%, so the first two lessons need work.

Activity. Build a cost sheet and a one-page offer, and invite 20 people to a free first lesson.

Standard to meet. Task: price a course. Condition: all costs counted. Standard: break-even correct and the offer makes no unsupported promises.

Module 8 Run the course for real 8 hCreate

Now run your course with real learners, measure results and improve it.

  • Run a 60-day pilot with a stop rule agreed in advance.
  • Review weekly: sign-ups, completion, feedback, task results.
  • Create a 12-month plan: updates, new courses and support.
  • Lead others: train a helper to answer questions and mark tasks with your rubric.
  • Calculate the completion rate for the pilot and compare it with the stop rule.
  • Collect two comments from learners, with consent, on what changed for them.
  • Update the weakest lesson first and re-test it.
Worked example. Stop rule: if fewer than 10 learners finish in 60 days, rebuild the first two lessons. Result: 14 finished out of 20 who started, a completion rate of 14 / 20 = 70%; learners ask for a second course.

Activity. Run the pilot, write the review and present your plan to three people.

Standard to meet. Task: run an online course pilot. Condition: 60 days, real learners. Standard: results compared with the stop rule and rules re-checked with the provincial body.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the parts of a course.

Model answer: Goal, lessons, practice, feedback and check.

Q2. Why write a learner profile?

Model answer: To design for real needs, barriers and goals.

Q3. Who sets education rules in Canada?

Model answer: The provinces; check yours.

Q4. Why start with outcomes?

Model answer: They decide what lessons, practice and checks you need.

Q5. Which verbs fit an outcome?

Model answer: Action verbs such as explain, apply, build, check.

Q6. What makes a lesson accessible?

Model answer: Captions or text, readable colours, keyboard use and low data size.

Q7. What is a rubric?

Model answer: A table of levels that shows how work is judged.

Q8. What should good feedback include?

Model answer: A strength, a change and a next step.

Q9. Why avoid promises of jobs or income?

Model answer: Claims must be honest and you cannot control the outcome.

Q10. What do you do about copyright?

Model answer: Use your own work or material you have permission or licence for.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
