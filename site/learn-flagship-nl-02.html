Handmade Goods and Craft Business

Turn a craft skill into a small product line that sells online and in person, with honest costs and a story buyers believe.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 120 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Turn a craft skill into a small product line that sells online and in person, with honest costs and a story buyers believe.

  • Understand Explain how design, craft and a buyer fit together.
  • Create Make a small range with consistent quality.
  • Analyse Work out true cost per piece including time.
  • Evaluate Judge which product to repeat or drop.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design, make, photograph, price and sell a small range of handmade goods and record 30 days of sales.

Condition

About 120 hours over 3 to 5 months. Basic tools, a phone camera, a free shop page or marketplace listing, a mentor and peer circle.

Standard

Three finished products; photos and descriptions; full cost sheet; 30-day sales log; at least 70% on the check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (120 h guided)

Module 1 Choose a craft and a buyer 6 hUnderstand

Start with who will buy and why, not with what you enjoy making. In this module you pick one craft you can repeat and describe the buyer for it. You finish with a one-page product idea and a price band.

  • Pick one craft you can make well and repeat at the same quality.
  • Describe the buyer by age, need and price level, in two or three lines.
  • Visit five sellers or listings and write down price, material and quality for each product.
  • Compare the prices, find the range and choose a band where your costs can still earn a profit.
  • A common mistake is to copy the cheapest seller, so decide on value first and price second.
Worked example. A leather card-holder maker notes five prices: EUR 25, 28, 30, 32 and 35. The mean is (25 + 28 + 30 + 32 + 35) / 5 = 150 / 5 = EUR 30. Buyers want slim holders in three colours, so the price band is EUR 25 to 35 with a target of EUR 30.

Activity. Write a one-page product idea with the buyer, price band and three products.

Standard to meet. Buyer, price band and three products named; five sellers noted.

Module 2 Design and prototype 14 hCreate

A prototype is a cheap first version that you make to learn. You test it with real people and change it before you make a range. A design log keeps each change and the reason for it.

  • Sketch three options and choose one with the help of two or three people.
  • Build a prototype from the real materials, not from scrap that behaves differently.
  • Test it with five people and ask them to use it, not only to look at it.
  • Write each change and its reason in a design log with a date.
  • A common mistake is to change many things at once, so change one thing and test again.
Worked example. Prototype 1: 3 of 5 testers say the strap is too stiff, which is 60%. You switch to softer leather. In prototype 2 only 1 of 5 says it is stiff, which is 20%. The log records the change, the reason and a fall of 40 percentage points.

Activity. Make two prototypes and write a design log with at least two changes.

Standard to meet. Design log with at least two dated changes and their reasons.

Module 3 Materials and quality 12 hApply

Quality is repeatable, not lucky. A short written standard lets you judge each piece the same way and shows you when a supplier or a method has changed. You inspect every piece against it.

  • Write a one-page standard for size, finish and fault limits, with numbers wherever you can.
  • Buy materials in batches and keep receipts so you can trace a fault to a batch.
  • Inspect each piece against the standard and record pass or fail with the reason.
  • Calculate the reject rate each week and act when it rises.
  • A common mistake is to sell second-rate pieces without telling the buyer, so mark them clearly or remove them.
Worked example. A candle maker rejects any piece with a crack over 2 mm. Of 20 pieces, 3 are rejected, so the reject rate is 3 / 20 = 15%. After slower cooling, 1 of 20 is rejected, which is 5%. The rate fell by 10 percentage points.

Activity. Make five pieces and inspect each against your standard.

Standard to meet. Inspection record for each piece; reject rate calculated.

Module 4 True cost and price 12 hEvaluate

Most makers forget their time and sell at a loss without knowing it. In this module you add up every cost for one piece, including your time at a fair rate, and set a price from a target margin.

  • Cost = materials + packing + fees + time at a fair hourly rate.
  • Price = cost divided by (1 - target margin), for example divided by 0.6 for a 40% margin.
  • Check the price against the band you found from five sellers.
  • Test the price with five buyers and note who says it is too high.
  • A common mistake is to add margin as cost x 1.4, which gives a smaller margin than 40%, so use the division.
Worked example. Material EUR 6, packing 1, fees 2 and time 1.5 h at EUR 12 (EUR 18) give 6 + 1 + 2 + 18 = EUR 27. Price at a 40% margin is 27 / 0.6 = EUR 45. Check: profit is 45 - 27 = EUR 18, and 18 / 45 = 40%.

Activity. Build the cost sheet for three products and set a price for each.

Standard to meet. Time included; margin calculated twice with the same result.

Module 5 Photos, words and story 10 hCreate

Online buyers cannot touch your work, so the photos and words must do that job. You learn a simple photo method and write a listing that answers questions before they are asked.

  • Use natural light, a plain background and three angles: front, side and in use.
  • Show size with a hand or a ruler in one photo.
  • Write size, material, care and who made it in the first lines of the description.
  • Tell one true story of the product in two or three sentences.
  • A common mistake is to use filters that change the colour, which leads to returns.
Worked example. You compare two listings of the same pot. Listing A has one photo and 100 views with 2 sales, which is 2%. Listing B has three photos, a size and care note and 100 views with 5 sales, which is 5%. At EUR 30 each, B earns 5 x 30 = EUR 150 and A earns 2 x 30 = EUR 60.

Activity. Photograph and write listings for three products.

Standard to meet. Photos clear; size and care given; story true.

Module 6 Selling online and in person 20 hApply

Using two channels lets you compare. An online listing reaches many people slowly, while a stall or friend sale gives fast feedback. You track each channel for two weeks and learn which suits the product.

  • Open one online shop page or listing and one market, stall or friend sale.
  • Track views, questions and sales for each channel on one sheet.
  • Reply to each message within one day.
  • Note which products sell where and what buyers ask.
  • A common mistake is to compare channels by total sales only, so also compare sales per visitor.
Worked example. Online: 120 views and 4 sales, which is 4 / 120 = 3.3%. Stall: 90 visitors stopped and 18 bought, which is 18 / 90 = 20%. Together 22 sales at about EUR 30 each is 22 x 30 = EUR 660. The stall works better for gifts.

Activity. Run both channels for two weeks and keep the sheet.

Standard to meet. Sales log shows both channels with views or visitors.

Module 7 Packing, post and returns 8 hApply

The parcel is part of the product. A good pack protects the item and shows care, and a clear postage and returns note stops disputes. You test with one parcel before you sell.

  • Pack to survive a one-metre drop, using double wrap for glass or ceramic.
  • Weigh the packed parcel and check the postage price before you list.
  • Show postage clearly at checkout or in the listing.
  • Write a short returns note with the number of days and who pays, and check the legal minimum return period for online sales with your local authority.
  • A common mistake is to ignore postage in the price, which can wipe out the margin.
Worked example. A product costs EUR 27 to make and sells at EUR 45. If you pay postage of EUR 6.50 yourself, profit is 45 - 27 - 6.50 = EUR 11.50, and margin is 11.50 / 45 = 25.6% instead of 40%. Showing postage at checkout keeps the 40% margin.

Activity. Pack and send one test parcel and record the weight and postage.

Standard to meet. Parcel arrives undamaged; postage is shown in the listing.

Module 8 Review and grow 38 hEvaluate

At the end you decide which products to keep, change or drop, using numbers from your own sales. You also ask buyers for feedback and plan the next batch.

  • Compare sales, margin and effort for each product.
  • Calculate profit per product as profit per piece times pieces sold.
  • Ask three buyers what they liked and what they would change.
  • Plan the next batch with quantities that match recent sales.
  • A common mistake is to keep a product because you like it, so let the numbers lead.
Worked example. Product A: price EUR 45, margin 42%, so profit per piece is 45 x 0.42 = EUR 18.90, and 12 sold gives 12 x 18.90 = EUR 226.80. Product B: price EUR 20, margin 15%, so profit per piece is EUR 3.00 and 2 sold gives EUR 6.00. Drop B and repeat A.

Activity. Present results and a plan for the next batch.

Standard to meet. A decision for each product with numbers. Meets the standard when your record shows: compare sales, margin and effort for each product; every number has its currency or unit; a peer has read it and a mentor can follow your reasoning.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: your records, numbers and reflections. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet" and at least 70% on the check.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why describe the buyer first?

Model answer: Because price, style and sales channel all follow from who the buyer is. (Covered in module 1: Choose a craft and a buyer. To pass that module: Buyer, price band and three products named; five sellers noted.)

Q2. What does a design log record?

Model answer: Each change and the reason for it. (Covered in module 2: Design and prototype. To pass that module: Design log with at least two dated changes and their reasons.)

Q3. Why write a quality standard?

Model answer: So each piece is judged the same way, by you and by anyone who helps. (Covered in module 3: Materials and quality. To pass that module: Inspection record for each piece; reject rate calculated.)

Q4. What price gives a 40% margin on a cost of EUR 27?

Model answer: 27 / 0.6 = EUR 45. (Covered in module 4: True cost and price. To pass that module: Time included; margin calculated twice with the same result.)

Q5. Why add size and care to a listing?

Model answer: Fewer returns and fewer questions from buyers. (Covered in module 5: Photos, words and story. To pass that module: Photos clear; size and care given; story true.)

Q6. Why use two channels?

Model answer: To compare results and learn which channel suits the product. (Covered in module 6: Selling online and in person. To pass that module: Sales log shows both channels with views or visitors.)

Q7. Why price postage before you list?

Model answer: So it does not wipe out the margin. (Covered in module 7: Packing, post and returns. To pass that module: Parcel arrives undamaged; postage is shown in the listing.)

Q8. How do you decide to drop a product?

Model answer: When margin and sales are both low compared with the effort it takes. (Covered in module 8: Review and grow. To pass that module: A decision for each product with numbers. Meets the standard when your record shows: compare sales, margin and effort for each product; every number has its currency or unit; a peer has read it and a mentor can follow your reasoning.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Design, make, photograph, price and sell a small range of handmade goods and record 30 days of sales. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Three finished products; photos and descriptions; full cost sheet; 30-day sales log; at least 70% on the check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

where next

Ladder L1 to L7, slide by slide with notes

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic, First words, tools and safety. Speaker note: Learner can say what Handmade Goods and Craft Business is about and name the safety rule. Use module 1, Choose a craft and a buyer, as the first short unit (about 2 to 6 hours). Say this rule aloud: Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money. No record is needed at this level.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Awareness, What the field is and who it serves. Speaker note: Learner explains the idea in plain words: Explain how design, craft and a buyer fit together. Shared with schools and community groups. Note for the guide: ask one learner to explain it to a neighbour and check the words are right.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Foundation, Guided tasks with a checklist. Speaker note: Learner does guided tasks from the first three modules (Choose a craft and a buyer; Design and prototype; Materials and quality) with a checklist. Evidence is a small set of dated records. Note for the guide: sit beside the learner for the first record, then step back.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Practitioner, Run the full task alone (this course). Speaker note: Task: Design, make, photograph, price and sell a small range of handmade goods and record 30 days of sales. Standard: Three finished products; photos and descriptions; full cost sheet; 30-day sales log; at least 70% on the check. Note for the guide: a mentor reviews the records; two peers mark with the rubric above.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Specialist, One narrow area to depth. Speaker note: Learner picks one area, for example Review and grow (the longest module at 38 h), and goes deeper than this course asks. Learner then teaches a peer circle of three or four. Note for the guide: check the teaching, not only the work.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Advanced, Lead a team and review evidence. Speaker note: Learner leads a small team or designs a service in this field and marks other people's evidence against the rubric. Roles this points to: Craft maker; Product designer assistant; Online shop owner. Note for the guide: sample three records from the team and compare marks.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Super expert, Set and audit the standard. Speaker note: Learner writes the method for Handmade Goods and Craft Business as a short guide, audits other learners' evidence, and publishes worked examples back to learners. Note for the guide: worked examples keep currency and units on every number and are checked by a second expert.

Notes: credits stack and you can step in and out. This course is L4. Aligned with, not accredited by, national credit frameworks.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
