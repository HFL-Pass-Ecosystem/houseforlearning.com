Grade 12 Humanities: Psychology

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5Post-secondary4 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Understand
  • Understand Describe intelligence and personality.
  • Understand Explain theories of personality.
  • Understand Describe types of disorders and therapy.
  • Understand Explain attitude change and group behaviour.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Humanities Psychology, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Variations in psychological attributes Weeks 1 to 9Understand

In this unit you work on 'Variations in psychological attributes', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe intelligence and personality. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Variations in psychological attributes' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe intelligence and personality. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Variations in psychological attributes'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe individual differences. Intelligence is the ability to learn from experience, reason, solve problems and adapt to the environment. Some theories name a single general ability, others several, such as verbal, spatial, logical and social abilities. Describe intelligence as measured performance, not worth.
  2. Step 2, Compute a ratio IQ: mental age divided by chronological age, times 100. A child aged 10 who performs like an average 12-year-old has IQ = 12 / 10 x 100 = 120. A child aged 8 who performs like an average 8-year-old scores 8 / 8 x 100 = 100, which is average.
  3. Step 3, Describe the normal curve. Test scores are set so the mean is 100 and the standard deviation 15. About 68 percent score between 85 and 115, and about 95 percent between 70 and 130. Only about 2 percent score above 130. Most people cluster near average.
  4. Step 4, Describe limits. Tests sample some abilities, can be affected by language, culture, practice and anxiety, and do not measure creativity or emotional skills. Scores can shift with schooling, so they should never be used to label a child for life.
  5. Step 5, Describe personality: stable patterns of thinking, feeling and acting. It can be assessed by questionnaires, interviews and observation. The five-trait model measures openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. If a scale has 10 items rated 1 to 5, totals run from 10 to 50 and a score of 38 is high.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: find the IQ of a child with mental age 15 and chronological age 12, and give the range containing 68 percent of scores. Answer: 15 / 12 x 100 = 125; the middle 68 percent lies between 85 and 115.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe intelligence and personality: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Self and personality Weeks 10 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'Self and personality', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain theories of personality. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Self and personality' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain theories of personality. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Self and personality'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain three ideas about the self. Self-concept is what you believe you are, such as 'I am a good listener'. Self-esteem is how you value yourself. Self-efficacy is your belief that you can succeed at a task. A student with high self-efficacy for maths keeps trying after a bad mark.
  2. Step 2, Explain type and trait theories. A type theory sorts people into a few groups, such as introvert or extravert. A trait theory places people on scales: on a 10-point extraversion scale a student may score 3 (quiet) while a friend scores 8 (outgoing). Types are simple but traits are more precise.
  3. Step 3, Explain the psychoanalytic theory. The id wants pleasure at once, the superego is the conscience, and the ego deals with reality. A child sees a friend's sweet. The id says take it, the superego says that is wrong, and the ego decides to ask for a share. Much of this happens in the unconscious.
  4. Step 4, Explain the humanistic theory: people are basically good and aim for growth and self-actualisation, helped by acceptance and unconditional regard. Explain the social-learning view: personality is learned by observing others, rewards, and expectations of what we can do. Compare: one stresses inner growth, the other learning from experience.
  5. Step 5, Weigh the theories. Type theories are simple but put people in boxes. Trait theories can be measured but describe rather than explain. Psychoanalytic ideas are rich but hard to test. Humanistic ideas are hopeful but hard to measure. Use more than one lens to explain one student's behaviour.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: explain how the id, ego and superego act when a student is tempted to copy an answer. Model answer: the id wants easy marks, the superego says copying is dishonest, and the ego chooses to try and ask the teacher for help.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain theories of personality: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Psychological disorders and therapies Weeks 19 to 27Understand

In this unit you work on 'Psychological disorders and therapies', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe types of disorders and therapy. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Psychological disorders and therapies' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe types of disorders and therapy. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Psychological disorders and therapies'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe what a psychological disorder is: a pattern of thoughts, feelings or behaviour that causes distress, interferes with daily life, is unusual for the culture and lasts. Ordinary sadness after a loss is not a disorder. Use respectful language: a person has a disorder, they are not 'mad'.
  2. Step 2, Describe main types. Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear or worry, including panic. Mood disorders include depression, with low mood most of the day for at least two weeks. Psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia involve hallucinations and delusions. Others affect eating, attention or substance use.
  3. Step 3, Describe causes. Biological: genes and brain chemicals. Psychological: unhelpful thinking and past trauma. Social: stress, loss, isolation. These often combine, as in the idea that a vulnerability plus a stressful event can trigger a disorder. No single cause explains every case.
  4. Step 4, Describe therapy. Talking therapies help people change patterns. In cognitive behavioural therapy the thought 'I will fail' leads to anxiety and avoiding study; it is replaced with 'I can prepare three chapters this week', which leads to action. Doctors may also prescribe medicines. Support groups and rehabilitation help recovery.
  5. Step 5, Describe who helps. A clinical psychologist assesses and treats with therapy; a psychiatrist is a medical doctor who can also prescribe medicine; a counsellor offers guidance. Early help works best. Do not diagnose yourself or friends from reading, and contact a helpline or doctor if someone seems at risk.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: state which professional can prescribe medicine and rewrite the thought 'I always fail' as a helpful thought. Answer: a psychiatrist; 'I did badly on one test, so I will review it and try a new plan.'

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe types of disorders and therapy: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Attitudes, social influence and group processes Weeks 28 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Attitudes, social influence and group processes', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain attitude change and group behaviour. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Attitudes, social influence and group processes' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain attitude change and group behaviour. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Attitudes, social influence and group processes'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain what an attitude is: a lasting evaluation of a person, object or idea. It has three parts: affective (feelings), behavioural (actions) and cognitive (beliefs). For recycling, a student may believe it helps (cognitive), feel good about it (affective) and sort waste at home (behavioural).
  2. Step 2, Explain attitude change. Attitudes come from family, experience and media. A message works better with a credible source, clear facts and a listener who is ready to hear it. In cognitive dissonance, a student who thinks cheating is wrong but copies once may change the belief to 'everyone does it' to reduce discomfort.
  3. Step 3, Explain social influence. Conformity is yielding to real or imagined group pressure. In a classic line-judging experiment, when every other member gave a wrong answer, about one answer in three was also wrong. Compliance is agreeing to a request; obedience is following an order from an authority.
  4. Step 4, Explain group processes. Groups have norms, roles and cohesiveness. Social facilitation: others watching can improve a simple task. Social loafing: effort drops in a team. In an invented rope-pull each person alone pulls 60 units, but three together pull 150 in all, so 150 / 3 = 50 each, 83 percent of the solo effort.
  5. Step 5, Explain bystanders. The more people who see an emergency, the more each may assume someone else will help; this is diffusion of responsibility. Naming one person, 'You in the blue shirt, call an adult', breaks it. Leaders and clear roles also improve group decisions.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: alone each person pulls 40 units; a group of 4 pulls 120 in total. Find each person's pull and the percentage drop. Answer: 120 / 4 = 30 each; 30 / 40 = 75 percent, a 25 percent drop, showing social loafing.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain attitude change and group behaviour: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: What psychology studies

On the slide: L1 Basic · What psychology studies

Notes: Name behaviour, thoughts and feelings as the things psychology studies. Notes: watch a short school-yard clip; learners write one thing they saw, one thing they think was thought and one feeling they infer, and say how sure they are.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Memory and learning basics

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Memory and learning basics

Notes: Explain short-term memory and why practice helps recall. Notes: read seven digits aloud, then ten, at one digit per second; learners write down what they recall and count correct digits; compare class results, then try grouping the ten digits in threes and see why chunking helps.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Research methods and ethics

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Research methods and ethics

Notes: Describe an experiment, name its variables and calculate mean, median and mode. Notes: scores 4, 6, 8, 8, 9 have a mean of 7, median 8 and mode 8; learners state consent and withdrawal rules for the study.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Approaches and explanations

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Approaches and explanations

Notes: Apply two approaches, such as learning and cognitive, to the same behaviour and compare them. Notes: in a case where 18 of 60 people conform, which is 30 per cent, learners give one explanation from each approach and one test that would separate them.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Correlation and study design (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Correlation and study design

Notes: Analyse a correlation coefficient and judge validity and sampling. Notes: r = 0.6 means r squared of 0.36, so 36 per cent of variance is shared, not caused; learners name one third variable and redesign the study to test cause.

This course: Grade 12 Humanities Psychology is at this level. Its units include Variations in psychological attributes; Self and personality; Psychological disorders and therapies; Attitudes, social influence and group processes.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Effect size and statistical power

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Effect size and statistical power

Notes: Calculate a standardised effect size and plan a sample size. Notes: group means of 52 and 46 with SD 12 give d = 0.5; for 80 per cent power at the 5 per cent level, plan about 64 per group; learners explain why small samples mislead.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Replication, Bayes and measurement

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Replication, Bayes and measurement

Notes: Evaluate a psychology finding using replication evidence, Bayesian updating and measurement error. Notes: a prior of 10 per cent, a test with 90 per cent sensitivity and 10 per cent false positives gives a posterior of 50 per cent; learners report what a preregistered study adds.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Mexico
