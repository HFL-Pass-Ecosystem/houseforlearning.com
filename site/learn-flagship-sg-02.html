Freight and Courier Services

Plan, price and run a small courier or freight service with tracking, proof of delivery and honest costs. You learn the parcel journey, route planning, safe packing, restricted goods, cost per drop, proof of delivery and how to review failed deliveries.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 130 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Plan, price and run a small courier or freight service with tracking, proof of delivery and honest costs. You learn the parcel journey, route planning, safe packing, restricted goods, cost per drop, proof of delivery and how to review failed deliveries.

  • Understand Explain pickup, transit and delivery steps.
  • Apply Plan a route and run drops.
  • Analyse Analyse cost, time and failures.
  • Create Create a price list and promise.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and run a 30-day trial courier route for one partner client, record every delivery with proof, and report cost per drop, first-time delivery rate and claims handled.

Condition

About 130 hours over 3 to 5 months. A defined route area, free map and spreadsheet tools, a partner client who agrees to the trial, a mentor and a peer circle.

Standard

Route plan with times; price list with cost sheet; delivery log with proof for every drop; cost per drop; claims record; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (130 h guided)

Module 1 The parcel journey 8 hUnderstand

Every parcel passes through the same chain of steps from the sender to the receiver. Each handover between steps is a place where a parcel can be delayed, damaged or lost. Seeing the whole chain helps you decide where to put checks.

  • The steps are pickup, sorting, transit, delivery and proof, and you should be able to say who holds the parcel at each step.
  • A handover is any moment the parcel changes hands or place, and handovers are where most losses happen.
  • A tracking update should be recorded at every step so you can say where a parcel was last seen.
  • A parcel needs a clear label with name, address, phone number and a reference number before it leaves the sender.
  • A common mistake is to scan a parcel only at pickup and delivery, which leaves the middle of the journey invisible.
Worked example. Out of 200 parcels in a week, 3 go missing at the sorting step. That is 3 divided by 200, or 1.5%, and because all 3 were last scanned before sorting, the sorting step is where a new check is needed.

Activity. Draw the parcel journey for your own route as a flow chart, mark every handover and add one check you would put at each handover.

Standard to meet. Your chart shows all five steps in order, marks every handover and gives a specific, practical check at each.

Module 2 Route planning 16 hApply

A good route uses fewer kilometres and causes fewer failed drops. You group stops by area, respect delivery time windows and leave a buffer for the unexpected. A route that looks perfect on paper but has no buffer will fail on the first busy day.

  • Group the drops by area on a map so the vehicle does not cross the same road several times.
  • Order the stops by their time windows first and by distance second.
  • Estimate stop time and drive time for each drop, using a test run for real numbers instead of guesses.
  • Add a buffer of about 20% of the total time for traffic, parking and failed attempts.
  • A common mistake is to plan for the fastest possible day and then promise times that only work with no delays.
Worked example. You plan 10 drops. Each stop takes 8 minutes and each drive between stops takes 10 minutes, so each drop costs 18 minutes and 10 drops cost 180 minutes. A 20% buffer adds 36 minutes, so the planned route takes 216 minutes, or 3.6 hours.

Activity. Plan a route of at least 10 real drops with stop times, drive times, a buffer and a printed schedule, then drive it once to test it.

Standard to meet. Your schedule shows times for every stop, a buffer of about 20%, and the test run finishes within 10% of the planned time.

Module 3 Packing and handling 12 hApply

The right packing protects the goods and keeps costs fair. Heavy or fragile items need specific care. Good labelling stops wrong deliveries.

  • Choose a box just big enough for the item so it cannot move and fill the gaps with paper or other filling.
  • Place heavy items at the bottom and light items on top, and mark fragile parcels clearly on two sides.
  • Calculate the size weight of a parcel as length times width times height in cm divided by 5,000 to get kilograms, and charge on whichever of actual weight or size weight is higher.
  • Put the address label flat on the largest side, with a second copy inside the parcel in case the outer label tears.
  • A common mistake is to use an oversized box, which raises the size weight and the cost.
Worked example. A box measures 30 cm by 20 cm by 15 cm, which is 9,000 cubic cm. Divided by 5,000, the size weight is 1.8 kg. The actual weight is 1.2 kg, so the billed weight is 1.8 kg because it is the higher figure.

Activity. Pack three different test parcels, one heavy, one fragile and one oddly shaped, and complete a table of actual weight, size weight and billed weight for each.

Standard to meet. All three parcels survive a one-metre carry and tilt test without damage, and your weight calculations are correct.

Module 4 Rules and dangerous goods 14 hApply

Some goods are banned or restricted, such as certain batteries, liquids and sharp items. You must not carry anything you cannot identify. Rules differ by place, so you always confirm them.

  • Keep a written list of banned and restricted goods taken from the official source and review it before every new client.
  • Ask the sender what is inside every parcel and write the answer on the booking record.
  • Do not carry any item you cannot identify or that the sender will not describe, and hold it until it is checked.
  • Keep records of what you refused and why, with the date and sender, so you can show good practice.
  • Check with your local authority for the current rules before you accept any new type of goods.
Worked example. In a batch of 40 parcels, 3 are flagged by the list as restricted. They are held for checking, which is 3 divided by 40, or 7.5% of the batch, and the other 37 go out on time.

Activity. Write a one-page checklist for accepting parcels, including questions to ask senders and the steps to take when an item is unclear, and confirm it with your local authority.

Standard to meet. Your checklist has at least eight steps, includes a hold-and-check step for unknown items and shows evidence that you asked your local authority.

Module 5 Price and cost 16 hEvaluate

Your price must cover what each drop really costs. Cost per drop is the number that decides whether you make or lose money. Knowing it lets you set a price and a margin with confidence.

  • List the costs of one day: fuel, vehicle wear, insurance share, your time and packing materials.
  • Calculate cost per drop as the total cost for the period divided by the number of drops in the period.
  • Add a margin on top of cost so the price covers surprises, and state the margin as a percentage of the price.
  • Use all your real figures from the trial route instead of rough guesses, and update them each week.
  • A common mistake is to leave out your own time and vehicle wear, which makes the service look cheaper than it is.
Worked example. A day costs S$76 in total for 20 drops, so cost per drop is 76 divided by 20, which is S$3.80. A price of S$5 leaves S$1.20 per drop, and 1.20 divided by 5 is a 24% margin.

Activity. Complete a cost sheet for your route showing every cost, the cost per drop, a proposed price and the margin percentage.

Standard to meet. Your cost per drop and margin are calculated correctly from recorded figures, and a mentor can follow each number back to a receipt or note.

Module 6 Proof and tracking 12 hCreate

Proof of delivery ends arguments about whether a parcel arrived. It should be quick for you and clear for the customer. Tracking updates keep customers informed and reduce calls.

  • Choose a proof method such as a photo at the door or a signature, and agree it with the client before you start.
  • Record the time and place with every proof so a dispute can be checked.
  • Send a short status message to the receiver at pickup and again when you are about 15 minutes away.
  • Store proofs safely for an agreed period and delete them afterwards, because they can show private addresses.
  • A common mistake is to take a photo that does not show the parcel and the door number together.
Worked example. On a test day of 50 drops, 46 have a clear photo with a time stamp. That is 46 divided by 50, or 92%, and the 4 without proof are all noted as handed to a neighbour with a name.

Activity. Design a proof process on one page with the method, the data you record, how you store it and how you handle a missing proof, then test it on 10 drops.

Standard to meet. Your process is tested on at least 10 drops, at least 90% of them have clear proof, and the privacy handling is written down.

Module 7 30-day trial 40 hApply

You now run the route for 30 days and keep a complete record. The record shows what works and what does not. Honest numbers matter more than perfect ones.

  • Log each drop with the time, the result, the proof and the cost, and keep the log up to date every day.
  • Record every failed attempt with the cause, such as nobody home or wrong address.
  • Record every claim for damage or loss with the date, the amount and how it was settled.
  • Calculate first-time delivery rate as successful first attempts divided by total drops.
  • A common mistake is to remove failed deliveries from the log, which hides the real success rate.
Worked example. Of 50 drops in one week, 47 are delivered on the first attempt. First-time delivery rate is 47 divided by 50, which is 0.94, or 94%, and the 3 failures are logged with causes.

Activity. Run your route for 30 days and submit the full delivery log, the failure list, the claims record and a weekly first-time delivery rate.

Standard to meet. The log covers all 30 days, every failed attempt has a cause and your first-time delivery rates are calculated correctly.

Module 8 Review 12 hEvaluate

The review turns 30 days of records into a short list of fixes. You look at what failed, why it failed and what would stop it happening again. Small fixes that tackle the biggest cause give the best return.

  • Group failed deliveries by cause and count each group.
  • Work out which cause accounts for the largest share and focus your first fix there.
  • Estimate the saving from each fix in drops or money so you can compare them.
  • Choose three fixes, give each an owner and a date, and decide how you will measure success.
  • A common mistake is to fix the loudest complaint instead of the most frequent cause.
Worked example. Over the trial, 3 of 50 drops fail, which is 6%. Two were nobody home and one was a wrong address. An SMS alert that halves the nobody-home failures saves 1 drop, taking failures to 2 of 50, or 4%.

Activity. Present a ten-minute review with a causes table, three fixes with dates and a revised price list if costs have changed.

Standard to meet. Your causes table adds up to the total failures and each fix names the cause it targets, a date and a measure.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Where do parcels most often get lost, and why?

Model answer: At handovers, because responsibility changes and a missed scan or label error means nobody knows where the parcel is.

Q2. Why do you add a buffer to a route?

Model answer: Traffic, parking and failed attempts are normal, so a buffer keeps your promised times honest.

Q3. Why is a second label placed inside the parcel?

Model answer: The outer label can tear or fall off, and an inside label still lets someone identify and deliver the parcel.

Q4. What should you do with a parcel when you are unsure of the contents?

Model answer: Do not carry it, hold it, and get the contents and rules checked first.

Q5. What is the cost per drop when a day costs S$76 and you make 20 drops?

Model answer: 76 divided by 20 gives S$3.80 per drop.

Q6. Why do you take proof of delivery?

Model answer: It settles disputes by showing who received the parcel, where and when.

Q7. What is the first-time delivery rate if 47 of 50 parcels are delivered at the first attempt?

Model answer: 47 divided by 50 is 0.94, so the rate is 94%.

Q8. What reduces failed attempts most cheaply?

Model answer: Alerts to the receiver and agreed time windows, because they target the most common cause of missed deliveries.

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Plan and run a 30-day trial courier route for one partner client, record every delivery with proof, and report cost per drop, first-time delivery rate and claims handled.

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Route plan with times; price list with cost sheet; delivery log with proof for every drop; cost per drop; claims record; at least 70% on the final check.
