flagship course · GL-23

E-Commerce Inventory Management

Keep the right stock at the right time: count, forecast, reorder, store and value inventory so an online shop neither runs out nor ties up cash.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Keep the right stock at the right time: count, forecast, reorder, store and value inventory so an online shop neither runs out nor ties up cash.

  • Understand Explain stock terms: lead time, safety stock, reorder point, turnover.
  • Apply Run a stock count and fix differences.
  • Analyse Analyse sales to forecast demand and find slow movers.
  • Create Create reorder rules and a stock-control routine.
  • Evaluate Evaluate stock health with turnover, stock-outs and holding cost.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Set up and run stock control for 20 products for 4 weeks, with counts, reorder rules and a health report.

Condition

A real or practice catalogue of 20 items, a spreadsheet, labels, a counting routine, a mentor with operations experience and a peer circle. About 8 to 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Count accuracy of 98% or better after fixes; reorder point set for every item; stock-outs under 2% of order lines; slow movers listed with actions; every number carries its currency or unit.

Who it is for: Shop owners, warehouse staff, operations assistants, students. Requirements: Basic spreadsheet skills. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Store goods safely: stable shelves, no blocked exits, correct storage for goods that need it.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Why inventory matters 4 hUnderstand

Stock is cash on a shelf. You will set par levels and review the costliest items first, seeing stock as tied-up cash that deserves regular attention.

  • Too little loses sales; too much ties up cash and spoils.
  • Costs: purchase, storage, loss, capital.
  • Goal: enough, not extra.
  • Know your lead time.
  • Set a written par level for every item, for example 20 units of a fast seller and 4 of a slow one, so anyone on shift can see at a glance what counts as low.
  • Review the five costliest items weekly and the rest monthly, because a few lines usually hold most of the money tied up in stock.
  • Write down your top five sellers and their lead times.
  • Mark shelves with item codes and picture labels.
Worked example. 500 of goods sits 90 days; holding cost 2% a month ties up 30.

Activity. List holding costs for three items.

Standard to meet. Costs with currency.

Common mistake. Ignoring holding cost.

Second worked example. A bakery keeps 1,200 of flour and tins for 60 days; at 1.5% a month the holding cost is 36, so it buys smaller lots every two weeks.

Check yourself. What do you hold too much of?

Module 2 Item master and locations 6 hUnderstand

Every item needs one name, one code and one place. You will give each item one code and each shelf one label, so every product has a single name and a findable place.

  • Unique code, name, size, unit, supplier, cost.
  • Locations: shelf, row, bin labels.
  • One item, one home.
  • Keep the list in one spreadsheet.
  • Give every item one unique code and one description, such as BOLT-M8-50 for a box of fifty, so the same product is never counted under two names.
  • Record each shelf, bin and bay with a short label like A-02-3 and mark it physically, so a new team member can find any item in under a minute.
  • Count one shelf each day instead of everything at once.
  • Record each stock difference and its cause.
Worked example. Code TB-40-NAT for tote bag, 40 cm, natural; shelf B2.

Activity. Create the item list of 20 products.

Standard to meet. No duplicates; all fields filled.

Common mistake. Two codes for one item.

Second worked example. A shop lists 'Mug blue' and 'Blue mug 300ml' under two codes; they are merged to MUG-300-BLU on shelf C1 and the list drops from 31 lines to 30.

Check yourself. Any duplicates?

Module 3 Counting and accuracy 8 hApply

Counts keep records honest. You will run daily cycle counts of one aisle and trace the cause of each difference, keeping records trustworthy without a shutdown.

  • Count by shelf, one person counts, one checks.
  • Compare with the record.
  • Investigate differences before adjusting.
  • Accuracy = correct lines / all lines.
  • Run a cycle count on one aisle each day instead of a full yearly shutdown, comparing the shelf to the record and noting every difference above two units.
  • Investigate each difference by checking the last three receipts and issues before changing the number, and write the cause beside the correction.
  • Plan extra stock before big sales dates.
  • Check supplier delivery times quarterly.
Worked example. 19 of 20 lines match: 95%. Two are fixed after finding a mis-pick; recount 20 of 20.

Activity. Do a full count and record accuracy.

Standard to meet. Accuracy calculated; causes written.

Common mistake. Adjusting the record without finding the cause.

Second worked example. A stationery shop counts 40 lines and 36 match: 90%. A swapped pen box is found; the recount gives 39 of 40, 97.5%, and a label change fixes the last one.

Check yourself. What caused your count gap?

Module 4 Forecast demand 8 hApply

Look at the past to plan the near future. You will forecast next month from twelve months of sales, adjust for known events, and measure the miss so the method improves.

  • Average weekly sales over 8 weeks.
  • Adjust for known events.
  • Do not trust a single week.
  • Round to pack sizes.
  • Use the last twelve months of sales by month, then adjust for known events such as a seasonal peak, to estimate next month rather than copying last month alone.
  • Compare forecast with actual each month and calculate the gap as a percentage; if you miss by more than 20 percent twice, change the method.
  • Keep damaged goods apart and logged.
  • Photograph shelf layouts before changes.
Worked example. Sales 12, 9, 14, 11 over 4 weeks: average 11.5; next week plan 12.

Activity. Forecast demand for 10 items.

Standard to meet. Forecast explained.

Common mistake. Using one week to forecast.

Second worked example. A cafe sells 80, 95, 70, 85 and 90 cups over 5 weeks: average 84; it orders 90 to cover a small rise.

Check yourself. How many weeks do you average?

Module 5 Reorder rules 8 hAnalyse

Order when stock reaches the reorder point. You will calculate a reorder point from usage, lead time and safety stock, and write the rule where staff can follow it.

  • Reorder point = daily use x lead time + safety stock.
  • Safety stock covers surprises.
  • Order quantity: pack size, price breaks, space.
  • Review monthly.
  • Calculate reorder point as daily usage times supplier lead time plus a safety margin; for example 6 a day times 5 days plus 12 spare gives a trigger of 42.
  • Write the rule on the shelf label or in the sheet, with the order quantity and the supplier, so the reorder does not depend on one person's memory.
  • Set a maximum stock level for each item.
  • Review prices if stock is slow.
Worked example. Use 4 a day, lead time 6 days, safety 8: reorder point = 24 + 8 = 32.

Activity. Set reorder points for 20 items.

Standard to meet. Formula applied correctly.

Common mistake. Ignoring supplier delays.

Second worked example. Phone cases sell 10 a day; stock arrives 3 days after ordering, and 12 are held as a buffer. Order when 42 are left (30 + 12).

Check yourself. What is your lead time range?

Module 6 Storage and handling 10 hCreate

Good storage cuts loss. You will arrange shelves by weight and speed and apply first in, first out, cutting damage, expiry loss and picking time.

  • First in, first out for dated goods.
  • Heavy low, light high; label clearly.
  • Protect from damp, heat, pests.
  • Keep damaged and returned stock apart.
  • Store heavy and fast-moving goods between knee and shoulder height, and keep a clear aisle of at least one metre so picking is quick and safe.
  • Use first in, first out by placing newer deliveries behind older ones and marking dates on cartons, so older stock leaves before it expires or fades.
  • Use a simple traffic-light status for stock.
  • Train new staff on counting rules.
Worked example. Dated jars: oldest in front; one month saves 6 jars from expiry.

Activity. Reorganise a shelf and photograph.

Standard to meet. Layout meets the safety checks.

Common mistake. Stacking heavy items high.

Second worked example. A pet shop puts tins with the nearest date at the front; in one quarter, 14 tins that would have expired are sold instead of thrown away.

Check yourself. Is your shelf safe?

Module 7 Slow movers and shrink 6 hEvaluate

Spot stock that is not selling or vanishing. You will flag items with no movement in ninety days and measure shrink by cause, deciding what to discount, return or write off.

  • Turnover = sales cost / average stock value.
  • Slow movers: no sales in 60 days.
  • Actions: discount, bundle, return, stop buying.
  • Shrink: damage, loss, theft; track causes.
  • Flag any item with no movement in 90 days and list it with its cost; decide to discount, bundle, return or write it off within a set month.
  • Track shrink as a percentage of stock value each month, and compare breakage, miscount and theft causes so you fix the largest cause first.
  • Keep a record of returns by reason.
  • Share the monthly report with the team.
Worked example. Sales cost 2,400 in 8 weeks; average stock 1,200: turnover 2.0 over 8 weeks.

Activity. List slow movers and actions.

Standard to meet. Each slow mover has a dated action.

Common mistake. Letting slow movers sit.

Second worked example. Goods sold cost 900 over 4 weeks and the average stock was worth 600, so turnover is 1.5. A scarf unsold for 70 days is bundled with a coat.

Check yourself. Which item is slowest?

Module 8 Report and improve 4 hCreate

Show owners the picture. You will build a one-page monthly stock report and pick one improvement with a target, so owners see the picture and trends.

  • One page: accuracy, stock-outs, turnover, cash tied up.
  • Three improvements.
  • Share with the peer circle.
  • Plan 90 days.
  • Build a one-page monthly report with stock value, turnover, stock-outs and shrink, and keep the same layout each month so trends are easy to see.
  • Choose one improvement per month, such as fixing the top five stock-outs, state a target number and check the result at the next review.
  • Test your reorder rule on last month's data.
  • Update the plan after each stock-out.
Worked example. Stock-outs 3% of lines: raise safety stock on two fast items.

Activity. Write the health report.

Standard to meet. Report uses four measures.

Common mistake. Reporting without actions.

Second worked example. A report shows count accuracy 97%, stock-outs on 5% of lines and 4,000 tied up in slow items; the owner sets a target of 2% stock-outs by next quarter.

Check yourself. What is your top action?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The stock-out

A best seller runs out for 5 days.

Question: Cause and fix?

Model answer: Reorder point too low; recompute with real lead time and raise safety stock.

Case 2. The pile of boxes

Boxes unsold for 6 months.

Question: Options?

Model answer: Bundle, discount, return to supplier or write off; stop reordering.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is lead time?

Model answer: Days from order to arrival. (Covered in module 5: Reorder rules. To pass that module: Formula applied correctly.)

Q2. Reorder point formula?

Model answer: Daily use times lead time plus safety stock. (Covered in module 5: Reorder rules. To pass that module: Formula applied correctly.)

Q3. What is safety stock for?

Model answer: To cover surprises in demand or delivery. (Covered in module 5: Reorder rules. To pass that module: Formula applied correctly.)

Q4. Define count accuracy.

Model answer: Matching lines divided by all lines. (Covered in module 3: Counting and accuracy. To pass that module: Accuracy calculated; causes written.)

Q5. What is FIFO?

Model answer: First in, first out. (Covered in module 6: Storage and handling. To pass that module: Layout meets the safety checks.)

Q6. Define turnover.

Model answer: Cost of sales divided by average stock value. (Covered in module 7: Slow movers and shrink. To pass that module: Each slow mover has a dated action.)

Q7. What is a slow mover?

Model answer: An item with no or very few sales over a set period. (Covered in module 7: Slow movers and shrink. To pass that module: Each slow mover has a dated action.)

Q8. Why investigate before adjusting?

Model answer: The cause may be a process error to fix. (Covered in module 3: Counting and accuracy. To pass that module: Accuracy calculated; causes written.)

Q9. Why one home per item?

Model answer: It prevents lost or duplicated stock. (Covered in module 2: Item master and locations. To pass that module: No duplicates; all fields filled.)

Q10. Why average several weeks?

Model answer: One week may be unusual. (Covered in module 4: Forecast demand. To pass that module: Forecast explained.)

Your place

Storage rules, taxes on stock and import duties differ by place; check them with a local adviser. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a Brainport-area small firm.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC). Partner to practise with: SEBRAE small-business support, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or a SkillsFuture-linked training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a TAFE or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Mexico (Central America)
