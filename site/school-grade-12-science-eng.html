Grade 12 Science: English

5 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Science English, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 5 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

5 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Prose and poetry from world writers Weeks 1 to 9Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Prose and poetry from world writers', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: compare two authors' treatment of one theme. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Prose and poetry from world writers' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: compare two authors' treatment of one theme, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Simile: 'The moon is like a silver coin.' Metaphor: 'The classroom was a zoo.' Imagery: 'the dew-wet grass' appeals to sight and touch. Say what feeling each creates. Method: Model run for 'Prose and poetry from world writers'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, State the theme in a sentence. Extract A (prose): 'Ravi lay on the riverbank for an hour, counting the ripples as if they were sums that would one day add up.' Extract B (poem): 'The microscope shows me a drop of pond: / a whole crowded city nobody planned.' Theme: patient looking turns nature into wonder.
  2. Step 2, Gather evidence from A. 'Counting the ripples' shows slow, careful observation, like a scientist taking measurements. The simile 'as if they were sums' links nature with mathematics and suggests Ravi believes the pattern will eventually make sense. 'One hour' shows how patient he is.
  3. Step 3, Gather evidence from B. 'A drop of pond' is tiny, but the metaphor 'a whole crowded city' makes it huge and busy. 'Nobody planned' suggests life organises itself without a designer. The tone is amazed. The microscope, a tool, is what reveals the wonder.
  4. Step 4, Build a comparison grid. Similarity: both characters discover meaning by observing closely. Difference: Ravi observes with his eyes and patience, and still waits for understanding; the speaker uses an instrument and is already astonished. Choose linking words: both, whereas, by contrast.
  5. Step 5, Write one comparative paragraph. 'Both writers show that careful observation reveals order in nature. In A, ripples are "sums that would one day add up", so understanding is still to come; in B, the pond is already "a whole crowded city", so wonder is immediate. Both use comparison to make nature feel familiar.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: compare the simile in A with the metaphor in B in two sentences. Model answer: A's simile 'as if they were sums' makes ripples a puzzle to solve, while B's metaphor 'a crowded city' makes the pond a place to marvel at. Both comparisons turn something small into something full of meaning.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Compare two authors' treatment of one theme: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Grammar and editing Weeks 10 to 18Apply

In this unit you work on 'Grammar and editing', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: edit and transform text with precision. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Grammar and editing' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: edit and transform text with precision, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Passive: 'The cat chased the mouse' becomes 'The mouse was chased by the cat.' Reported: 'I will come,' she said becomes She said that she would come. Modals: 'should' for advice, 'must' for rules. Agreement: 'The list of items is long.' Edit: 'He go to school' becomes 'He goes to school.' Method: Model run for 'Grammar and editing'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Edit for scientific style. Draft: 'We heated the water to 60 degrees and we wore goggles.' Method sections use the passive and the past tense: 'The water was heated to 60 °C. Safety goggles were worn and the beaker was handled with tongs.' The passive keeps the focus on the procedure, and safety is part of the method.
  2. Step 2, Edit units and numbers. '5ml' becomes '5 mL', with a space and a correct symbol. 'sixty degrees' becomes '60 °C'. Always write the unit once after the number, keep one style throughout, and use figures for measurements. Check each quantity against the data table.
  3. Step 3, Edit agreement and fewer/less. 'The data shows that less samples dissolved.' Formal usage: 'The data show that fewer samples dissolved.' Data is treated as plural in formal science writing, and 'fewer' is used for countable things such as samples, while 'less' is used for uncountable things such as water.
  4. Step 4, Edit certainty. 'This proves that heat speeds up dissolving.' One experiment rarely proves anything, so hedge: 'This suggests that higher temperature increases the rate of dissolving.' Use may, suggests, indicates or is consistent with when the evidence is limited.
  5. Step 5, Transform by combining. 'The solution was stirred. It was left for ten minutes.' Joined: 'The solution was stirred and then left for ten minutes.' Or with a relative clause: 'The solution, which was stirred first, was left for ten minutes.' Pick the version that keeps the order of events clear.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: edit 'We add 5ml of acid and the data shows less bubbles.' Answer: 'Five millilitres of acid were added (or 5 mL of acid was added) and the data show fewer bubbles.' Use the past passive, spell out the number at the start or write the unit with a space, and use 'fewer'. Acid needs goggles and gloves.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Edit and transform text with precision: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Writing: reports, letters and articles Weeks 19 to 27Apply

In this unit you work on 'Writing: reports, letters and articles', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: write formal, persuasive and analytical text. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Writing: reports, letters and articles' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: write formal, persuasive and analytical text, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Formal letter: 12 November 2026; The Principal; Subject: request for a library card; 'Respected Sir, I am a student of Class 8...'; Yours faithfully, Aman. Informal: Dear Ria, ... Your friend, Aman. Report: title, date, place, what happened, result, writer's name. Method: Model run for 'Writing: reports, letters and articles'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Plan a lab report. Sample data from a class test: time for a spoonful of sugar to dissolve in 100 mL of water was 90 seconds at 20 °C, 55 seconds at 40 °C and 30 seconds at 60 °C. Safety: warm water only, safety goggles, hair tied back, and an adult pours anything hot. Headings: Aim, Method, Results, Conclusion.
  2. Step 2, Write Results analytically. 'As the temperature rose from 20 °C to 60 °C, the dissolving time fell from 90 s to 30 s, a decrease of 60 s or 66.7 per cent.' Quote the figures from the table and describe the trend in one clear sentence before you explain it.
  3. Step 3, Write a hedged conclusion. 'The results suggest that higher temperature shortens the time sugar takes to dissolve, possibly because particles move faster. One trial per temperature limits reliability, so the test should be repeated.' Formal science writing hedges and names a limitation.
  4. Step 4, Write a persuasive letter to a council. 'Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to propose a weekly river-testing project for local schools. Students would measure temperature and clarity, wearing gloves and goggles, and report findings.' State the proposal, the benefit and the safety plan, then close with 'Yours faithfully'.
  5. Step 5, Draft an article for a school magazine. Headline: 'Why Every School Needs a Weather Station'. Hook: 'What if tomorrow's forecast came from your playground?' Add a fact from your records, such as rain on 12 of 30 days (40 per cent), and end with a clear call to action.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write one results sentence for 25 s at 70 °C compared with 90 s at 20 °C. Answer: The time fell by 65 s, which is 72.2 per cent (65/90). 'Raising the temperature from 20 °C to 70 °C reduced the dissolving time from 90 s to 25 s, a fall of 72.2 per cent.' Hot water needs adult help.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Write formal, persuasive and analytical text: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Reading: unseen passages Weeks 28 to 32Remember

In this unit you work on 'Reading: unseen passages', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: read critically in timed conditions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Reading: unseen passages' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: read critically in timed conditions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Passage: 'The old banyan tree gave shade to the whole village.' Q: what does 'shade' suggest? Protection from the sun. Inference: the tree matters to the villagers. Answer with a quote as evidence. Method: Model run for 'Reading: unseen passages'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Plan the timing. For a 45-minute unseen section, allow 5 minutes to skim, 30 to answer and 10 to check. Passage: 'A school weather station recorded rain on 12 of 30 days. The rainfall total was 96 mm. A student wrote that this clearly proves the town is getting wetter every year.'
  2. Step 2, Skim and mark the claim. Circle 12, 30 and 96 mm, and underline 'clearly proves'. The main idea is that the data are being used to support a strong claim about climate. A scientific reader checks whether thirty days of data can support that claim.
  3. Step 3, Answer with working. Rainy days: 12 out of 30 is 40 per cent. Average rainfall per day: 96 / 30 = 3.2 mm. Average per rainy day: 96 / 12 = 8 mm. Write each answer with its unit, and quote 'rain on 12 of 30 days'.
  4. Step 4, Read critically. 'Clearly proves' is too strong. A single month cannot show a yearly trend; the student would need many years of records for comparison. Fact: 96 mm in 30 days. Opinion or claim: 'wetter every year'. Say so and cite the evidence.
  5. Step 5, Use context for vocabulary and summary. 'Proves' means 'shows beyond doubt', so the writer implies certainty. Summary in 30 words: 'A school weather station recorded rain on 12 of 30 days, totalling 96 mm, but a student wrongly claimed this proves yearly change.' Keep facts and the writer's attitude.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: work out the percentage of dry days and say why the claim is weak. Answer: Dry days are 30 - 12 = 18, which is 60 per cent. The claim is weak because one month of data cannot show a trend across years. 'Proves' is too strong.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Read critically in timed conditions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 5 Speaking and listening Weeks 33 to 36Apply

In this unit you work on 'Speaking and listening', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: deliver an argued presentation. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Speaking and listening' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: deliver an argued presentation, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Retell in order with first, next, then, last. Ask: 'What happened next?' Reply politely: 'Thank you, I agree because...' Method: Model run for 'Speaking and listening'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Frame the motion. "Governments should fund basic research even without immediate applications." Thesis: "Basic research drives unpredictable breakthroughs that applied research alone cannot reach." Reason: the claim is arguable and specific.
  2. Step 2, Write an opening with a hook. "No one funded early work on electricity to power phones, yet our devices depend on it. Curiosity often reaches destinations planners never imagined." Reason: a historical pattern supports the thesis.
  3. Step 3, Build argument one with PEEL. Point: basic research creates foundations. Evidence: many technologies grew from studies done without a product in mind. Explain: applications need a pool of tested knowledge. Link: funding it is investing in the future.
  4. Step 4, Meet the counter-argument. "Opponents say money should address current problems. Yet current solutions often depend on earlier discoveries, so neglecting basic research starves tomorrow's solutions." Reason: show dependency, not just disagreement.
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Weak: "Research is always beneficial so we should fund all of it." Better: "Basic research has a strong record of long-term benefit, so a balanced portfolio of funded projects is justified." Avoid absolute words like always.
  6. Step 6, Respond to a question. Q: "How would you measure success?" A: "By published findings, trained researchers and later uses, though the value may take decades to appear." Reason: show you understand limits as well as benefits.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Write a thesis and a rebuttal for "Nuclear power should be part of a low-carbon plan". Model answer: "Thesis: Nuclear power gives steady, low-carbon electricity." "Rebuttal: Waste is a challenge, but modern storage and recycling reduce long-term risk."

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Deliver an argued presentation: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Letters, sounds and first reading

On the slide: L1 Basic · Letters, sounds and first reading

Notes: Match letters to sounds and read short words and simple sentences aloud. Notes: use the sounds s, a, t, p, i, n; learners blend them into sat, pin and tap, then read a three-word sentence and clap the syllables.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Sentences and simple writing

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Sentences and simple writing

Notes: Write a clear sentence with a capital letter and full stop, and read a short story with understanding. Notes: give a picture prompt; learners say the sentence first, write it, and check capital, spacing and full stop with a partner before reading it aloud.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

Notes: Write a paragraph of five connected sentences and identify nouns, verbs and adjectives in a text. Notes: colour nouns blue and verbs red in a short passage; learners then write about their street, using at least two adjectives and one linking word.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Essays, speaking and responding to texts

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Essays, speaking and responding to texts

Notes: Write a 300-word structured essay and give a short talk, quoting evidence from a text to support a view. Notes: model Point, Evidence, Explain; learners write one paragraph about a character, quote one line, and explain what it shows, then speak it aloud.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Analysis, argument and style (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Analysis, argument and style

Notes: Analyse a writer's choices of language and structure and write a sustained argument of 600 words. Notes: take two openings of different short stories; learners compare tone, sentence length and imagery in a table, then argue which opening works better and why.

This course: Grade 12 Science English is at this level. Its units include Prose and poetry from world writers; Grammar and editing; Writing: reports, letters and articles; Reading: unseen passages and more.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Literary and linguistic study

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Literary and linguistic study

Notes: Evaluate texts through a critical lens, tracing how language changes across time and context in a researched essay. Notes: compare an older and a modern passage; learners list five differences in vocabulary and syntax and argue in 1,500 words what each reveals about its period.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research, editing and authorship

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research, editing and authorship

Notes: Produce an original piece of research or a polished long-form text, with a literature review and defended method. Notes: pick a narrow question about language in one genre; build a corpus of fifty samples, analyse patterns, write 5,000 words, and revise after external review.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
