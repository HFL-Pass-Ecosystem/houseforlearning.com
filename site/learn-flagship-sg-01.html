Telecommunications Services

Plan, install-check and support a small home or office network service and measure service quality. You learn how a signal travels, how to survey a site, how to secure and test a network, how to find faults in a sensible order and how to explain fixes to non-technical users.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 140 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Plan, install-check and support a small home or office network service and measure service quality. You learn how a signal travels, how to survey a site, how to secure and test a network, how to find faults in a sensible order and how to explain fixes to non-technical users.

  • Understand Explain how a signal gets from provider to device.
  • Apply Set up and test a small network.
  • Analyse Analyse speed, delay and faults.
  • Create Create a support guide for users.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Survey, set up and support one small network for a real user for 30 days and report uptime, speed, delay and faults, with a written support guide the user can follow without help.

Condition

About 140 hours over 3 to 6 months. Router, network cables, free speed-test and ticket-log tools, a friendly site with a willing user, a mentor and a peer circle.

Standard

Site survey table; working and secured network; fault log with causes and fixes; 30-day quality report with uptime percentage; safety and data checks signed; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (140 h guided)

Module 1 How the network works 10 hUnderstand

A network is a chain of links from the provider to the exchange, the wider network and finally the device in your hand. Each link can be slow, late or unreliable, and a user feels each problem differently. Knowing the chain lets you point at the weak link instead of guessing.

  • The path runs from the line or wireless link into the router and then to each device, and you should be able to name every part on that path.
  • Speed means how much data arrives each second and is measured in megabits per second, written Mbps.
  • Delay means how long one small message takes to make a round trip, measured in milliseconds, written ms.
  • Reliability means how often the link stays up and keeps packets without loss, and a fast link that drops out is still a poor service.
  • A common mistake is to blame speed for every problem when the real cause is high delay or packet loss.
Worked example. A home has a link of 100 Mbps down and 20 Mbps up. A video call needs about 3 Mbps each way, so speed is plenty. The call still stutters because delay is 150 ms, well above the 50 ms that feels smooth, so the weak link is delay and not speed.

Activity. Draw a labelled diagram of one real network, from the provider line to three devices, and write one sentence under each part saying what it does and what could go wrong there.

Standard to meet. Your diagram names at least six parts in the correct order, and you correctly state which of speed, delay or reliability each part most affects.

Module 2 Survey the site 14 hApply

A site survey finds where the signal is strong and weak before you place any equipment. Walls, floors, mirrors and other devices all weaken a wireless signal. A short, careful walk with a test tool saves hours of fixing later.

  • Walk every room with the same device and the same speed-test tool, and record the result at the same height in each room.
  • Note the wall material, distance from the router and any metal, water tank or microwave oven near the test spot because these reduce signal.
  • Test each spot three times and use the middle value so one odd reading does not mislead you.
  • Choose the router position that gives the weakest room an acceptable result, usually central and raised off the floor, and write down why.
  • A common mistake is to test only beside the router, which hides the rooms where the user actually has trouble.
Worked example. A living room beside the router reads 90 Mbps and a corner bedroom behind two walls reads 45 Mbps. The bedroom gets 45 divided by 90, which is 50%, so it gets half the speed. Moving the router 4 metres towards the middle lifts the bedroom to 70 Mbps.

Activity. Produce a heat table listing every room, its three test readings, the middle value, the wall type and your chosen router position with one sentence of reasoning.

Standard to meet. Your table covers every room with three readings each, shows the middle value correctly, and your router choice is backed by at least one measured improvement.

Module 3 Set up safely 20 hApply

A new network is only safe if it is secured from the first minute. Default passwords and open settings are the most common way networks get misused. Careful setup and clear labels also make later support much easier.

  • Change the default router password and the default network name on day one, and store the new password somewhere the owner can find it.
  • Turn on the strongest encryption the router offers and choose a passphrase of at least 14 characters that is not a name or a date.
  • Create a separate guest network for visitors and smart devices so they cannot reach the laptops and phones on the main network.
  • Label both ends of every cable and record the router model, firmware version and settings in a one-page configuration sheet.
  • A common mistake is to leave remote management switched on, which lets outsiders try to reach the router settings.
Worked example. A household has 12 devices. Moving 3 visitor devices and 2 smart plugs to the guest network leaves 7 devices on the main network, so 5 of 12 devices, about 42%, are kept apart from the private files.

Activity. Set up a small network with a secured main network and a guest network, then write a configuration sheet and tick each item of a safety checklist that you create.

Standard to meet. Your sheet lists every setting changed, all default passwords are replaced, a guest network works and a mentor can reproduce the setup from your sheet alone.

Module 4 Test and measure 16 hAnalyse

Numbers beat feelings when you judge a service. You will take the same three tests at several times of day and compare them with what the provider promised. A pattern over time shows problems that one test would miss.

  • Run a speed test, a delay test and a packet-loss test, which are the three core measures of a connection.
  • Take each test at three times, for example morning, afternoon and evening, because shared links slow down at busy times.
  • Log each result in a table with the date, time, device and whether it was wired or wireless.
  • Compare the averages with the promised speed and state the gap as a percentage.
  • A common mistake is to run one test on a busy evening and call the whole service bad.
Worked example. The plan promises 100 Mbps. The morning test gives 96 Mbps and the evening test gives 58 Mbps, so the average is (96 + 58) divided by 2, which is 77 Mbps. The gap against the promise is 100 minus 77, which is 23, divided by 100, or 23%. The evening reading is also 38 below the morning one, about 40% lower, so the service slows at busy time.

Activity. Run all three tests at three times of day over three days and complete the results table, then write two sentences describing the pattern.

Standard to meet. Your table has at least 27 readings, the average and the percentage gap against the promise are calculated correctly, and your pattern statement matches the numbers.

Module 5 Fault finding 20 hApply

Most faults have simple causes, so you check the simple things first and only then dig deeper. Working in a fixed order stops you from changing many things at once and not knowing what fixed it. Every fault should end with a written record.

  • Start with the simplest checks in order: power light, cable seated at both ends, restart of the router and then the device.
  • Isolate the fault by swapping one part at a time, such as a different cable or a different device, and test after each swap.
  • Check the configuration last, because a setting that was changed earlier is a likely cause when nothing physical is wrong.
  • Write each fault in the log with the symptom, the cause found, the fix and the time it took.
  • A common mistake is to reset the router to factory settings straight away, which erases the secure setup you made.
Worked example. A user reports drops every few minutes. Out of 200 test packets, 8 are lost, which is 8 divided by 200, or 4% loss. Reseating a loose network cable brings loss to 0 of 200, which proves the cable was the cause.

Activity. Find and fix three faults, which may be real or planted by your mentor, and log each with symptom, cause, fix and time taken.

Standard to meet. All three faults are logged with a measured before and after result, and the fix for each was found by a one-change-at-a-time method.

Module 6 Support users 16 hCreate

Good support is clear writing and calm speaking. Users are not technical, so jargon makes them feel lost. A short guide and a simple call script help every user fix small problems without waiting for you.

  • Write a one-page guide in plain words with numbered steps and a picture of the router lights, so a user can follow it without help.
  • Prepare a short call script that follows four steps: ask what happened, check the basics, fix or guide the fix, and confirm it works.
  • Define escalation steps that say when a problem goes to a more skilled person and what information to pass on.
  • Avoid jargon and, where a technical word is needed, explain it in one short phrase the first time.
  • A common mistake is to say it should be fine now without asking the user to confirm the fault has actually gone.
Worked example. Over a trial week you handle 12 support calls and resolve 9 on the first contact. That is 9 divided by 12, which is 75% first-contact resolution, and the 3 escalated calls are all written up with the details the next person needs.

Activity. Write the one-page user guide and the call script, then test them by asking a person outside the field to follow the guide and fix a planted fault.

Standard to meet. The test user fixes the planted fault using only your guide in under 10 minutes, and your guide has no unexplained technical words.

Module 7 30-day service 40 hApply

You now run the service for real for 30 days. The aim is to keep it working and to record everything honestly, including outages. Honest records are what turn the work into evidence.

  • Do a short daily check of the router lights and one speed test, and write the result in the log.
  • Open a ticket for every complaint or outage and record the start time, end time, cause and fix.
  • Write a short weekly report with uptime, average speed, number of tickets and the main lesson.
  • Calculate uptime as the minutes the service was available divided by the total minutes in the period.
  • A common mistake is to leave out short outages, which makes the uptime figure look better than it really was.
Worked example. A 30-day period holds 30 times 24 times 60, which is 43,200 minutes. One outage of 6 hours is 360 minutes. Uptime is 43,200 minus 360, which is 42,840, divided by 43,200, about 99.2%.

Activity. Run the service for 30 days and submit the daily log, the ticket log and four weekly reports, plus a final uptime calculation.

Standard to meet. All 30 days are logged, every outage is in the ticket log and the uptime percentage is correct to one decimal place.

Module 8 Review 4 hEvaluate

A review compares what you achieved with what you aimed for and decides what to change. It is short, honest and ends with dated actions. A plan with no dates is only a wish.

  • List your targets from the start of the project, such as uptime, average speed and time to fix, next to what you actually achieved.
  • Work out the gap for each target as a number or a percentage.
  • Write three lessons, each linked to a piece of evidence from your logs.
  • Choose the improvements that give the most benefit for the least effort and set a date for each.
  • A common mistake is to list improvements without saying how you will know they worked.
Worked example. The target was 99.5% uptime and you reached 99.2%, a gap of 0.3 points. A 99.5% target allows 0.5% of 43,200 minutes, which is 216 minutes of outage, and you used 360, so you were 144 minutes over. A backup link is chosen as the main fix.

Activity. Present a five-minute review to your mentor and peer circle with a targets-against-results table, three lessons and a dated improvement plan.

Standard to meet. Your table shows each gap correctly, each lesson cites a log entry and each improvement has an owner, a date and a way to measure success.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is the difference between speed and delay, and which one would make a video call stutter on a fast link?

Model answer: Speed is the amount of data delivered each second, measured in Mbps, while delay is how long a message takes to travel there and back, measured in ms. A fast link with high delay makes a video call stutter because each piece of the conversation arrives late even though plenty of capacity exists.

Q2. Why do you test each room three times and use the middle value?

Model answer: One reading can be thrown off by a passing device or a brief interruption. Three readings with the middle value give a result you can trust and show the real condition of the room.

Q3. Why create a separate guest network?

Model answer: It keeps visitor and low-trust devices apart from the main devices, so a problem on a visitor phone cannot reach private laptops or files.

Q4. Name the three tests you run and say what each one tells you.

Model answer: Speed shows how much data arrives each second, delay shows how long a message takes to travel, and packet loss shows how much data goes missing on the way.

Q5. What do you check first when a network fault is reported, and why?

Model answer: Check power, cables and a restart first because simple causes are the most common and are quick to rule out before deeper work.

Q6. Why must a support guide use plain words?

Model answer: Users are usually not technical, so plain words let them act correctly and stop them from giving up or making the fault worse.

Q7. What is the uptime for one outage of 6 hours in a 30-day month?

Model answer: The month has 43,200 minutes and the outage is 360 minutes, so uptime is 42,840 divided by 43,200, which is about 99.2%.

Q8. What should you do right after finishing a service report?

Model answer: Choose the most valuable improvements, give each a date and decide how you will measure whether it worked.

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Survey, set up and support one small network for a real user for 30 days and report uptime, speed, delay and faults, with a written support guide the user can follow without help.

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Site survey table; working and secured network; fault log with causes and fixes; 30-day quality report with uptime percentage; safety and data checks signed; at least 70% on the final check.

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Mexico (Central America)
