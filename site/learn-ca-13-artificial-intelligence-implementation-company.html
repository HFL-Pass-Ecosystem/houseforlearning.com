Artificial intelligence implementation company

Run a real artificial intelligence implementation company pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small artificial intelligence implementation company offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Find processes where AI pays 6 hUnderstand

AI implementation means putting working models into daily processes.

  • Good candidates: repeat decisions with data, such as triage, forecasting, document checks, quality inspection.
  • Poor candidates: rare events, no data, high harm if wrong without a person checking.
  • Score ideas on value, data readiness and risk; pick the top one.
  • Write the success measure before building.
  • List the decisions the team makes each week, how often, how long each takes, and what data is already stored for it.
  • Check that data exists in usable form: count the records, the share with missing fields, and the past examples with a known right answer.
  • Ask who will use the output and what they will do with it; an accurate model nobody acts on has no value.
Worked example. Invoice checking: 4,000 invoices a month, 6 minutes each by hand; target 70% auto-checked with human review of the rest. Hand time now is 4,000 x 6 = 24,000 minutes, or 400 hours a month. At 70% auto-checked, 2,800 invoices are automated and 1,200 stay with people.

Activity. Score three processes and choose one.

Standard to meet. Scores have reasons.

Module 2 Know privacy, AI and contract rules 6 hApply

Privacy, sector and contract rules all shape what an AI project may do with data.

  • Privacy law covers personal data used for training and decisions; get consent or another lawful basis.
  • Automated decisions about people may need explanation and human review; check the federal and provincial rule and the client policy.
  • Contracts: data ownership, model ownership, performance claims, liability.
  • Sector rules such as health or finance add duties.
  • Write a data map: what personal data you hold, where it came from, where it is stored, who sees it and when you delete it.
  • Ask whether the data can be reduced or de-identified before training, and use less personal data when the task allows.
  • Check whether data will leave Canada, and read the provincial rule and the client contract before it does.
Worked example. Row: 'Use client customer data to train | privacy law | consent and purpose limit | before training | written data agreement'. Second row: 'Use a model to screen applicants | privacy law and provincial rules | human review and explanation | before launch | written process'. Check the provincial rule before you automate any decision about a person.

Activity. Build a rules table for one client.

Standard to meet. Rows sourced and dated.

Module 3 Prepare data and build a baseline 10 hApply

Data work is most of the work.

  • Collect, clean and label data; record where each field comes from.
  • Set a baseline: how well does the current process or a simple rule do?
  • Split data: train, validate, test; never test on training data.
  • Report accuracy, false alarms and misses.
  • Write a data dictionary listing each field, its meaning, unit, allowed range and how many values are missing.
  • When the process changes over time, split by time: train on older months and test on the latest month.
  • Try the simplest model first, and keep a complex one only if it beats it on the test set by a margin you set beforehand.
Worked example. Rule-only baseline catches 61%; model catches 83% with 9% false alarms on held-out test data. Suppose the test set has 1,000 cases with 200 real problems. The rule catches 0.61 x 200 = 122 and the model catches 0.83 x 200 = 166, which is 44 more; 9% false alarms on the 800 other cases is 72.

Activity. Build a baseline and a simple model on a public data set.

Standard to meet. Test results on held-out data.

Module 4 Price a project and show return 8 hEvaluate

Return = savings minus cost.

  • Price the work in parts: discovery, build, integration, training and support.
  • Estimate the customer saving as hours saved times cost per hour, plus error cost avoided.
  • Express payback in months: the price divided by the monthly saving.
  • Offer a staged price with a decision point.
  • List the monthly cost of running the model: hosting, monitoring time, retraining and support hours.
  • Quote three tiers, discovery only, discovery plus pilot, and full build, with a go or no-go point between them.
  • Ask the client to confirm the hourly cost and minutes per task using a sample of real tasks, not guesses.
Worked example. Project price 22,000. Saving: 4,000 invoices x 70% auto-checked x 6 minutes = 16,800 minutes = 280 hours a month; at 30 an hour = 8,400 a month. Payback = 22,000 / 8,400 = about 2.6 months. If only half the saving is real, payback is about 5 months. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build a payback model with a 50% savings case.

Standard to meet. Cases shown.

Module 5 Deploy, monitor and maintain 8 hApply

Model accuracy falls over time as the data changes, so plan to watch it.

  • Pilot with people checking the model's output before it acts.
  • Monitor accuracy and inputs weekly.
  • Retrain when accuracy falls.
  • Document the model so the support team can run and fix it.
  • Log each prediction with its inputs, output, confidence and the person's final decision, so accuracy can be measured later.
  • Set two alert levels, a warning and a stop level, and name the person who acts at each.
  • Store each model version with its training data date and test result, and keep a rollback step.
Worked example. Accuracy fell from 83% to 74% after a form change; retrained on new data. A drop of 83 - 74 = 9 points was caught because the warning level was set at 78%. After retraining on new examples, accuracy returned to 82%.

Activity. Design a monitoring sheet.

Standard to meet. Alert levels set.

Module 6 Sell and manage change 6 hCreate

People adopt what helps them.

  • Start with one small process where the model can show a quick, clear win.
  • Train staff on the new tool and listen to their concerns.
  • Report results in plain numbers the client's managers can read in a minute.
  • Ask satisfied clients for referrals to similar teams.
  • Run a one-hour workshop: show the process map, run the model on 10 real cases, and ask staff to mark where they disagree.
  • Name a champion on the client team who owns adoption and reports use every week.
  • Update the checklist or job description so staff know what changed in their work.
Worked example. Funnel: 20 talks, 6 workshops, 3 pilots. Talk to workshop is 6 / 20 = 30%, workshop to pilot is 3 / 6 = 50%, and overall 3 / 20 = 15%. At 15%, 40 talks are needed for 6 pilots.

Activity. Run a workshop for a real team.

Standard to meet. Feedback recorded.

Module 7 Manage bias, errors and trust 6 hEvaluate

Models make mistakes.

  • Test for unequal error across groups.
  • Provide a route for people to appeal a model decision to a human.
  • Keep logs of inputs and decisions so errors can be traced.
  • Be honest with clients about what the model cannot do.
  • Agree with the client which groups to check, using fields it holds lawfully, and what gap triggers action.
  • Write an appeal route: how a person asks for human review, who answers, and the time limit.
  • Publish a one-page plain note on what the model does, what data it uses and its known limits.
Worked example. Error gap between two groups was 11%; fixed by rebalancing data. An error rate of 20% in group A and 9% in group B is a gap of 11 points. After rebalancing the data, errors were 13% and 11%, a gap of 2 points.

Activity. Write a fairness check.

Standard to meet. Check has numbers.

Module 8 Pilot and scale 8 hCreate

Prove value.

  • Run the pilot for 90 days.
  • Measure saving, accuracy and user satisfaction every week.
  • Stop rule: if accuracy stays under the baseline, stop the pilot.
  • Plan the roll-out only if the pilot meets its targets.
  • Run the pilot with a control: compare time and error against a matched group not using the model.
  • Record weekly saving, accuracy, staff overrides and complaints.
  • Plan the roll-out in stages, one team, then two, then all, with a checklist for each stage.
Worked example. Saving 6,100 a month; proceed. Planned saving was 8,400, so 6,100 / 8,400 = about 73% was reached. Payback at 6,100 is 22,000 / 6,100 = about 3.6 months, so continue to roll-out.

Activity. Run pilot review.

Standard to meet. Review has decision.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a baseline?

Model answer: Current method or simple rule used for comparison. (Covered in module 3: Prepare data and build a baseline. To pass that module: Test results on held-out data.)

Q2. Why test on held-out data?

Model answer: To avoid inflated accuracy. (Covered in module 3: Prepare data and build a baseline. To pass that module: Test results on held-out data.)

Q3. Name two good AI process types.

Model answer: Triage and forecasting (or document checks and quality inspection). (Covered in module 1: Find processes where AI pays. To pass that module: Scores have reasons.)

Q4. Why keep a human in the loop at first?

Model answer: To catch errors. (Covered in module 7: Manage bias, errors and trust. To pass that module: Check has numbers.)

Q5. What is drift?

Model answer: Model accuracy falling as data changes. (Covered in module 5: Deploy, monitor and maintain. To pass that module: Alert levels set.)

Q6. What is payback?

Model answer: Cost divided by monthly saving. (Covered in module 4: Price a project and show return. To pass that module: Cases shown.)

Q7. Why check fairness?

Model answer: Errors may hit groups unequally. (Covered in module 7: Manage bias, errors and trust. To pass that module: Check has numbers.)

Q8. What data rights matter?

Model answer: Consent and purpose for personal data. (Covered in module 2: Know privacy, AI and contract rules. To pass that module: Rows sourced and dated.)

Q9. When stop a pilot?

Model answer: If accuracy is below baseline. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and scale. To pass that module: Review has decision.)

Q10. What goes in a contract?

Model answer: Data and model ownership, claims and liability. (Covered in module 2: Know privacy, AI and contract rules. To pass that module: Rows sourced and dated.)

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
