Emerging market internet development company

Run a real emerging market internet development company pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small emerging market internet development company offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Understand connectivity gaps 6 hUnderstand

Many communities lack affordable, reliable internet. Your work is to find where, why and what would fix it.

  • Measure before you build: run speed tests at 10 spots at different times, noting device, place and time.
  • Causes of gaps include distance from fibre, cost per household, cost of devices, language and skills, and power cuts; note which apply.
  • Choose a first community and list who already serves it.
  • Write a need statement with numbers: households, current speed, target speed, monthly price they can pay.
  • Ask household heads what they pay now for mobile data and what they would pay for a home line; record the answers in a table.
  • Map the village on paper: mark homes, school, clinic and hall, find the nearest fibre or tower, and measure each distance in km.
  • Identify households with no device or no one who can set it up, and estimate how many need a loan device or training.
Worked example. A village of 300 households has 2 Mbps shared at peak. Target 25 Mbps at under 40 a month per home. Ten tests give 1.8, 2.1, 2.0, 1.9, 2.2, 2.0, 1.7, 2.3, 2.0 and 2.0 Mbps; the sum is 20.0, so the average is 2.0 Mbps. If all 300 homes shared that equally at peak, each would get under 0.01 Mbps.

Activity. Run speed tests at 10 spots and write a need statement.

Standard to meet. Tests have times and devices recorded.

Module 2 Know telecom and licensing rules 6 hApply

Telecom is regulated.

  • The federal spectrum authority licenses radio spectrum and the federal telecom regulator sets rules for carriers; licence-exempt bands have power limits.
  • Tower and mast siting needs municipal approval and may involve federal rules or consultation; check both.
  • Customer privacy and emergency-call duties may apply to service providers; check the federal and provincial rules.
  • Rural and Indigenous community connectivity programmes have funding conditions; read them early.
  • Decide whether your service counts as a carrier or reseller under federal rules, and ask the regulator or a telecom adviser before you charge customers.
  • Keep a list of each funder's conditions: who can apply, matching funds, reporting dates, and what happens if you miss a target.
  • Check the provincial rule on pole access and rights of way before you hang cable on utility poles or dig along roads.
Worked example. Row: 'Use a radio link | federal spectrum rules | stay within licence-exempt limits | before install | equipment certificate'. Second row: 'Mount an antenna on a mast | municipal approval, possibly federal rules | check the provincial and municipal rule | before building | written approval on file'. Write the date you read each source.

Activity. Build a rules table for a small community network.

Standard to meet. Rows sourced and dated.

Module 3 Design a network a community can run 10 hApply

Keep it simple and repairable.

  • Choose the last-mile type: fibre, fixed wireless, or satellite backhaul with local Wi-Fi.
  • Plan backhaul capacity as households times peak use per household.
  • Plan battery or solar power for outages.
  • Document the network so that local people can fix common faults.
  • Draw a map of the link route with each hop's length in km and a line-of-sight check for wireless hops, noting trees and hills.
  • Select equipment rated for local winter cold and summer heat, and keep one spare of each key part.
  • Size a battery: add up the watts the equipment draws, multiply by the hours of outage you want to cover, and add 20% margin.
Worked example. 100 households x 1.5 Mbps peak each = 150 Mbps if every home used its peak at once; with 3:1 sharing the backhaul needed is 150 / 3 = 50 Mbps. A site drawing 120 W that must run 6 hours needs 120 x 6 = 720 Wh; with 20% margin that is 720 x 1.2 = 864 Wh.

Activity. Draw a network plan and compute backhaul.

Standard to meet. Calculation shown.

Module 4 Cost it and set affordable prices 8 hEvaluate

Costs per household drive the model.

  • Work out capital cost per household passed and per household connected.
  • List monthly costs: backhaul, power, support and a replacement fund.
  • Set price tiers and decide how a community subsidy would work.
  • Calculate payback and list the funding sources.
  • Separate the cost to pass a home (cable or signal reaches it) from the cost to connect it (drop line, router, labour), and budget both.
  • Offer a low-income tier or a shared-hotspot option and show who pays the difference, such as a community fund.
  • Run three take-up cases (40%, 60% and 80%) and show monthly margin and payback for each.
Worked example. Teaching figures in CAD. Build cost about 42,000 (an estimate; get real quotes) for a site of 150 homes; monthly cost per household 25; price 35; margin 10 per home. If all 150 homes pay, margin is 10 x 150 = 1,500 a month. At 60% take-up, 150 homes become 90 paying homes, so margin is 90 x 10 = 900 a month. Payback on 42,000 is 42,000 / 900 = about 47 months; at 40% take-up, 60 homes give 600 a month and payback is 42,000 / 600 = 70 months.

Activity. Build the model with 60% take-up.

Standard to meet. Cases shown.

Module 5 Operate and support 8 hApply

Reliability makes trust.

  • Monitor uptime and speed continuously.
  • Have a local support person with a spare kit.
  • Publish your response times.
  • Track every complaint and its cause.
  • Set up a monitor that checks each radio every 5 minutes and texts the support person when two checks in a row fail.
  • Keep a labelled spares box with a count, and reorder when any item falls to one.
  • Write a one-page fault guide: symptom, first check, fix, and when to call for help.
Worked example. Uptime 98%; fixed within 1 day in 90% of cases. A 30-day month has 720 hours, so 98% uptime allows 720 x 0.02 = 14.4 hours down. If there were 20 faults, 90% fixed within a day means 18 fixed in a day and 2 slower.

Activity. Keep a 4-week ops log.

Standard to meet. Faults and fixes logged.

Module 6 Train users and grow adoption 6 hCreate

Access is not use.

  • Offer short classes in the local languages.
  • Teach safe use, banking and learning platforms.
  • Partner with schools and libraries.
  • Measure use before and after each class.
  • Build a short lesson plan for each skill, such as sending a message, joining a video call with a clinic or applying online, with a hands-on task and a 5-question check.
  • Train two local helpers and give them a printed guide, so help stays in the community.
  • Run classes at times suited to shift workers, elders and parents, and record attendance by group to see who is missing.
Worked example. After 8 classes, weekly users rose from 90 to 210. Weekly users rose by 210 - 90 = 120, or 120 / 90 = about 133%. Eight classes of 15 learners reached 8 x 15 = 120 people, which matches the gain.

Activity. Run one class and survey.

Standard to meet. Survey results recorded.

Module 7 Manage safety, privacy and equity 6 hEvaluate

Do no harm.

  • Protect user data and do not read customers' traffic.
  • Offer child-safety tools.
  • Keep access and pricing fair.
  • Follow climate and tower safety practices.
  • Write a privacy notice in plain words and local languages and give it to each new subscriber.
  • Offer a content filter for homes with children and explain that parents choose whether to use it.
  • Check the federal and provincial rules on what you must hand over if police ask, and keep a record of any request.
Worked example. Policy: 'We log connection times for 30 days only.' With 150 homes making 20 connections a day, the log holds at most 150 x 20 x 30 = 90,000 entries before the oldest are deleted.

Activity. Write privacy and fair-use notes.

Standard to meet. Plain words.

Module 8 Pilot and sustain 8 hCreate

Prove one site.

  • Run the pilot for 90 days.
  • Measure take-up, uptime and satisfaction.
  • Set a stop rule: if take-up is under 30% after 90 days, redesign.
  • Plan how the network will sustain itself.
  • Hold a review at day 30, 60 and 90 using the same four numbers: take-up, uptime, complaints, and money in versus money out.
  • Ask 10 subscribers and 10 non-subscribers why, and record the reasons in a table.
  • Write a handover plan: who owns the equipment, who collects fees, who keeps spares, and how the community votes on price changes.
Worked example. Take-up 46%. Of 150 homes, 46% take-up is 150 x 0.46 = 69 homes. The stop rule of 30% is 45 homes, so the pilot passes with 24 homes to spare.

Activity. Run pilot review.

Standard to meet. Review has decision.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why test speed at different times?

Model answer: Peak-hour congestion differs from off-peak. (Covered in module 1: Understand connectivity gaps. To pass that module: Tests have times and devices recorded.)

Q2. How find backhaul need?

Model answer: Households times peak use, adjusted for sharing. (Covered in module 1: Understand connectivity gaps. To pass that module: Tests have times and devices recorded.)

Q3. Why use batteries?

Model answer: Outages stop service. (Covered in module 3: Design a network a community can run. To pass that module: Calculation shown.)

Q4. Who licenses spectrum?

Model answer: The federal spectrum authority; the federal telecom regulator separately sets rules for carriers. (Covered in module 2: Know telecom and licensing rules. To pass that module: Rows sourced and dated.)

Q5. Why train users?

Model answer: Access without skill does not create use. (Covered in module 6: Train users and grow adoption. To pass that module: Survey results recorded.)

Q6. What is take-up?

Model answer: Share of homes that subscribe. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and sustain. To pass that module: Review has decision.)

Q7. Why keep logs short-term?

Model answer: To protect privacy. (Covered in module 7: Manage safety, privacy and equity. To pass that module: Plain words.)

Q8. What triggers redesign?

Model answer: Under 30% take-up in 90 days. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and sustain. To pass that module: Review has decision.)

Q9. Why a local support person?

Model answer: Fast repair and trust. (Covered in module 5: Operate and support. To pass that module: Faults and fixes logged.)

Q10. Name one funding source type.

Model answer: Public connectivity programmes or a community fund; read each funder's conditions first. (Covered in module 2: Know telecom and licensing rules. To pass that module: Rows sourced and dated.)

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
