flagship course

Electronics Assembly and Testing

Build and test electronic circuits and boards, read schematics, solder safely, and report faults with measured proof.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 39 h lessons plus practice

Build and test electronic circuits and boards, read schematics, solder safely, and report faults with measured proof.

  • Define voltage, current, resistance and power with units.
  • Apply safe soldering and assembly to a written standard.
  • Analyse a circuit with a multimeter and an oscilloscope.
  • Evaluate a board for fit to its specification.
  • Create a small tested product and its build record.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Assemble a small powered board from a schematic, test it against its specification, and keep a build and test record.

Condition

About 10 weeks part-time. A soldering station, multimeter, bench power supply, oscilloscope or logic probe, a kit of parts, a mentor.

Standard

Joints meet the written solder standard, every test reading has a unit and a limit, faults are proved by measurement, and the build record lets another person repeat the work.

Who it is for: School leavers, ITI and diploma students, repair technicians, makers and small manufacturing staff. Requirements: None; basic arithmetic. Materials: lesson text, templates, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Bench safety and basic quantities 4 hDefine
  • Define voltage in volts, current in amperes, resistance in ohms and power in watts, and give the symbol for each.
  • Wear eye protection when soldering and cutting leads, and work with ventilation or a fume extractor.
  • Set the iron in its stand, never on the bench, and switch it off when you leave.
  • Use an earthed mat and wrist strap when handling chips that static can damage; never work on mains-powered circuits with the supply connected.
Worked example. A 5 V supply drives a 250 ohm resistor. Current is 5 / 250 = 0.02 A, or 20 mA, and power is 5 x 0.02 = 0.1 W, so a quarter-watt resistor is safe.

Activity. Work out voltage, current and power for three resistor circuits.

Standard to meet. All three answers carry correct units and match a meter reading within 5%.

Module 2 Read schematics and components 5 hExplain
  • Learn the symbols for resistor, capacitor, diode, LED, transistor and chip.
  • Read resistor colour bands and capacitor markings, and check with a meter.
  • Follow a signal from input to output on a schematic with a finger or a highlighter.
  • Note polarity for diodes, LEDs and electrolytic capacitors before fitting.
Worked example. A resistor with brown, black, orange, gold bands reads 10 x 1,000 = 10 kilo-ohm, plus or minus 5%, so acceptable readings are 9.5 to 10.5 kilo-ohm.

Activity. Identify twelve parts on a kit board and list value, polarity and position.

Standard to meet. All twelve are identified and values agree with a meter within the stated tolerance.

Module 3 Solder and assemble 6 hApply
  • Heat the pad and lead together for about 2 seconds, then feed solder; a good joint is shiny and shaped like a small cone.
  • Fit parts in order of height: resistors, diodes, chips sockets, capacitors, connectors.
  • Trim leads flush after soldering and clean flux residue with isopropyl alcohol.
  • Use lead-free solder where the product must meet RoHS and keep the work area clean.
Worked example. A 40-joint board is built in 55 minutes; an inspection with a magnifier finds 2 cold joints and 1 bridge, which are reflowed; first-pass yield is 37 of 40, or 92.5%.

Activity. Assemble a board and inspect each joint under a magnifier.

Standard to meet. At least 95% of joints pass the written standard on first inspection.

Module 4 Measure with a multimeter 5 hApply
  • Set the meter to the correct mode and range before touching the circuit.
  • Measure voltage in parallel and current in series; resistance only with power off.
  • Use continuity beep to check tracks and joints.
  • Write each reading with its unit and the expected range.
Worked example. A 9 V battery feeds a 5 V regulator. Input reads 8.7 V and output 4.98 V, which is within 5% of 5 V, so the regulator is healthy.

Activity. Take ten readings on a powered board and compare each with its expected value.

Standard to meet. Each reading has a unit, an expected range and a pass or fail.

Module 5 Test with an oscilloscope 5 hAnalyse
  • Set volts per division and time per division so the wave fills the screen.
  • Read amplitude, period and frequency from the display; frequency is one divided by period.
  • Use a x10 probe and compensate it before measuring.
  • Compare an input and an output wave to see gain, delay or distortion.
Worked example. A square wave spans 5 divisions at 2 ms per division, so period is 10 ms and frequency is 1 / 0.01 = 100 Hz; the height of 2.5 divisions at 2 V per division gives 5 V peak to peak.

Activity. Capture and measure a waveform on a timing circuit.

Standard to meet. Period, frequency and amplitude are calculated and match the design within 10%.

Module 6 Find and prove faults 5 hAnalyse
  • Start with a visual check for burnt parts, cracks and lifted pads.
  • Measure the supply first, then follow the signal stage by stage.
  • Change one thing at a time and record the result.
  • Prove the fault with a reading before replacing the part.
Worked example. A board has no output. The 5 V rail reads 0 V; the input fuse shows no continuity (open); replacing the 1 A fuse restores 5.02 V and the output works.

Activity. Diagnose a board with a seeded fault and write the proof.

Standard to meet. The fault is located by measurement and the repair is confirmed by a second reading.

Module 7 Check against specification 5 hEvaluate
  • Turn the specification into a test sheet with limits for each requirement.
  • Test under normal and worst-case supply, for example 4.5 V and 5.5 V.
  • Record pass, fail or marginal, and flag marginal results for review.
  • Decide to accept, rework or reject, and give the reason.
Worked example. A 5 V supply board must give 4.75 to 5.25 V at 0.5 A load. Tests give 4.98 V at 0.1 A and 4.80 V at 0.5 A, so it passes but the low end is marginal.

Activity. Write a test sheet and test one board against it.

Standard to meet. Every requirement has a limit, a reading and a decision.

Module 8 Capstone: build, test, document 4 hCreate
  • Choose a small product such as a regulated power supply or an LED timer.
  • Build it from the schematic and test it fully.
  • Write the build record with parts, photos, readings and faults.
  • Present the product and answer questions.
Worked example. A power supply is built in 3 hours with 48 joints, 2 reworked, 12 tests all passed, and a one-page record signed off by the mentor.

Activity. Present the build and test record in five minutes.

Standard to meet. The record is complete enough for another person to repeat the build.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: the work product from each module, kept in one folder with dates. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is the current through 100 ohms at 5 V?

Model answer: 0.05 A, or 50 mA.

Q2. What does the gold band mean?

Model answer: A tolerance of plus or minus 5%.

Q3. What does a dull, grainy joint show?

Model answer: A cold joint, which can fail; reheat and add fresh solder.

Q4. Why is current measured in series?

Model answer: The meter must carry the circuit current to measure it.

Q5. How do you find frequency from the period?

Model answer: Divide one by the period.

Q6. Why change only one thing at a time?

Model answer: Otherwise you cannot tell which change fixed the fault.

Q7. What is a marginal result?

Model answer: A reading that passes but sits close to the limit.

Q8. What belongs in a build record?

Model answer: Parts, steps, readings, faults found and the final decision.

Q9. Name the first check before powering a new board.

Model answer: Inspect for bridges and wrong polarity, then check resistance across the supply.

Q10. Why use a fume extractor?

Model answer: Flux smoke irritates the lungs and eyes.

seven levels

Your ladder, L1 to L7

This course sits at L4 Practitioner. Each level adds to the one below.

  1. L1 Basic. Know the safety rules and basic quantities.
  2. L2 Awareness. Read schematics and identify components.
  3. L3 Foundation. Solder and assemble simple boards under supervision.
  4. L4 Practitioner. Test and repair boards alone.
  5. L5 Specialist. Handle complex faults and specification tests.
  6. L6 Advanced. Lead a production line and set test methods.
  7. L7 Super expert. Design test systems and standards for a manufacturer.
