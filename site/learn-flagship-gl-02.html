flagship course · GL-02

Robotics Fundamentals

Learn how a small robot senses, decides and moves; build and test a safe low-voltage robot; and understand the jobs and the limits of robotics.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5 Specialist66 h of lessons plus practiceLong Module, 1-3 monthsUSD 39 per learner per month, or USD 79 with mentor review

Learn how a small robot senses, decides and moves; build and test a safe low-voltage robot; and understand the jobs and the limits of robotics.

  • Understand Explain the sense-think-act loop and name the parts of a simple robot.
  • Apply Wire a battery, motor driver, motors and sensors on a low-voltage kit with correct polarity and a safety switch.
  • Analyse Calculate battery life and motor current from the datasheet values.
  • Create Program a robot to follow a line or avoid an obstacle and record test results.
  • Evaluate Find and fix faults with a written test plan, and judge where robots help and where they should not be used.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Build and test a small low-voltage robot that follows a line or stops before an obstacle, and document the build and test results.

Condition

A commercial education kit or parts under 12 V, a laptop, free programming tools, a safe table, eye protection, a trained supervisor for any soldering, two peers and a mentor. About 60 hours.

Standard

The robot completes the test course in 3 of 5 runs; wiring diagram matches the build; a safety switch cuts motor power; the log shows at least three faults found and fixed; every calculation shows units.

Who it is for: Students, technicians, makers and teachers moving into automation. Requirements: basic arithmetic. No electronics experience needed. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Low-voltage battery kits only (12 V or less). No mains wiring and no industrial or heavy robots in this course; those need trained, licensed staff and the machine-safety rules of your country. Wear eye protection, keep hair and sleeves away from moving parts, use a switch to cut power, and never charge batteries unattended.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What a robot is: sense, think, act 6 hUnderstand

A robot is a machine that senses its surroundings, makes a decision with a program, and acts with motors or other parts. A remote-controlled toy is not a robot; a vacuum that turns at a wall is.

  • Sense: sensors turn the world into numbers (distance, light, touch, tilt).
  • Think: a controller runs a program of rules (if the front is blocked, turn).
  • Act: actuators such as motors, servos, lights and grippers.
  • Feedback: the robot senses the effect of its action and corrects, like a thermostat.
  • Industrial, service and educational robots differ mainly in size, power, safety rules and cost.
Worked example. Obstacle robot loop, 20 times a second: read distance; if distance < 15 cm then stop and turn; else drive forward. That is sense (distance), think (rule), act (motors).

Activity. Draw the sense-think-act diagram for three machines around you (for example a lift, an automatic door, a washing machine).

Standard to meet. Each diagram names one sensor, one rule and one actuator.

Module 2 Safety first 6 hApply

Most robot accidents come from skipping a simple check. Make safety a habit before the first motor turns.

  • Low voltage only: keep every supply at or below 12 V in this course.
  • Check polarity (plus and minus) before connecting; reversed batteries can damage parts and heat wires.
  • Fit a hard power switch in the battery line and an easy stop in the code.
  • Never charge or leave lithium batteries unattended; use the charger made for that battery; stop if a pack swells or smells.
  • Test with wheels lifted off the table first. Keep fingers, hair and sleeves clear of moving parts.
  • Wear eye protection when cutting, soldering or testing; solder only with a trained supervisor.
Worked example. Safety card for the line robot: 7.4 V pack, 2 A fuse in series, rocker switch, wheels lifted for first test, supervisor present for soldering.

Activity. Write a one-page safety card for your build and sign it with your supervisor.

Standard to meet. Card lists voltage, fuse or protection, switch, charging rule and first-test method; supervisor signs.

Module 3 Motors and movement 6 hUnderstand

Choose the right motor for the job. Three types cover most small robots.

  • DC gear motor: spins continuously; speed rises with voltage; gearing trades speed for pushing force (torque).
  • Servo: turns to an angle and holds it; good for arms and steering.
  • Stepper: moves in fixed steps; good for exact positions without feedback.
  • A motor driver board switches the motor power; a controller pin cannot drive a motor directly.
  • PWM (pulse width modulation) changes average power by switching fast: 50% duty is about half power.
Worked example. A line robot uses two DC gear motors with a driver. PWM 70% on the left and 40% on the right makes it curve right. A servo sweeps a distance sensor from 0 to 180 degrees.

Activity. Test motor speed at 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% duty and record distance covered in 5 seconds.

Standard to meet. Table complete with units; the speed rises with duty; any stall or heat is noted.

Module 4 Sensors: how a robot knows 8 hApply

Sensors are never perfect. Learn what they measure, their range and how they mislead.

  • Distance (ultrasonic or infrared): range from a few centimetres to a few metres; soft or angled surfaces confuse them.
  • Line sensors: reflected light differs on dark and light surfaces; ambient light changes readings, so calibrate.
  • Touch switches: simple and reliable bumpers.
  • Tilt and motion (IMU): gives rotation and acceleration; needs filtering.
  • Calibrate: record readings on black and white, then set the threshold half way. Test again at the room's real light.
  • Noise: average several readings to smooth jumps.
Worked example. Line sensor reads 820 on white and 210 on black. Threshold = (820 + 210) / 2 = 515. Under 515 means line.

Activity. Calibrate two sensors and record raw readings in three lighting conditions.

Standard to meet. Readings table complete; threshold calculated; behaviour tested and noted in each light.

Module 5 The controller and your first program 8 hCreate

A small controller board runs your rules. Three ideas cover most beginner programs: variables, conditions and loops.

  • Variables store readings and settings, for example speed = 150.
  • Conditions choose actions: if left sensor sees the line then turn left.
  • Loops repeat forever: read, decide, act.
  • Write in small steps: first read and print a sensor value, then move one motor, then combine.
  • Add comments and names that say what a thing means.
  • Test one change at a time; keep a version of the working program.
Worked example. Pseudocode: loop forever { read L, R sensors; if L on line and R off then turn_left(); else if R on line and L off then turn_right(); else if both off then stop(); else forward(); }.

Activity. Write, run and comment a program that prints sensor values, then one that drives straight for 2 seconds and stops.

Standard to meet. Programs run without error; comments explain each block; a version is saved.

Module 6 Power and wiring: voltage, current, battery life 8 hAnalyse

Every build needs enough power. Use three ideas: voltage (push), current (flow) and capacity (storage).

  • Ohm's law: V = I x R. A 6 V supply across a 12 ohm load gives 0.5 A.
  • Power P = V x I. 6 V x 0.5 A = 3 W.
  • Battery runtime (ideal) = capacity in mAh divided by current in mA. Real runtime is about 70% of ideal because of losses and ageing.
  • Use wire thick enough for the current and a fuse above normal current but below the wire rating.
  • Keep motor power wiring away from sensor signal wires to cut noise.
  • Draw the wiring diagram before you build and check each connection against it.
Worked example. Two motors draw 0.5 A each, controller and sensors 0.2 A. Total 1.2 A = 1200 mA. Battery 2000 mAh: ideal 2000 / 1200 = 1.67 h; real about 1.67 x 0.7 = 1.17 h, roughly 70 minutes.

Activity. Calculate current, power and runtime for your robot and compare with a timed run.

Standard to meet. Calculations show units; measured runtime is within 30% of the prediction, or the gap is explained.

Module 7 Build and test a line follower or obstacle avoider 14 hCreate

Build in stages and test at every stage. Write down what you expect before each test.

  • Stage 1: chassis, motors, wheels, switch and fuse; test with wheels lifted.
  • Stage 2: sensors; read and print values.
  • Stage 3: simple rules; run on a taped course.
  • Stage 4: tune speed and thresholds one at a time.
  • A test plan has: goal, setup, steps, expected result, actual result, fix.
  • Run each test five times; a robot that works once is not yet reliable.
Worked example. Test 1 goal: follow a 2 m black tape loop. Expected: finish in 20 s. Actual: leaves the line at the right corner. Fix: reduce speed from 180 to 130 and lower right sensor by 3 mm. Result: 4 of 5 runs succeed.

Activity. Build, tune and test; record at least five tests in a table.

Standard to meet. The robot passes 3 of 5 final runs; the test table shows the fixes made; the safety switch is verified.

Module 8 Debug, document, ethics and jobs 10 hEvaluate

Good robot builders find faults fast, write them down and ask what the machine should and should not do.

  • Debug in order: power, wiring, sensor readings, code logic, mechanics. Change one thing at a time.
  • Document: parts list with cost, wiring diagram, program, test table, photos, lessons learned.
  • Ethics and safety: who could be hurt, who is replaced, who is watched. Keep humans in charge of anything risky.
  • Jobs: technician, maintenance, integration, programming assistant, education, field service.
  • Limits: this course does not qualify anyone to work on industrial robots; that needs formal training and machine-safety knowledge.
Worked example. Fault log entry: "Left motor does not turn. Checked battery 7.3 V (ok), driver light on, wire from driver to motor loose at screw terminal. Re-tightened. Fixed." Cost sheet: kit USD 38, battery USD 9, spare parts USD 6, total USD 53.

Activity. Complete your build file and present it for 10 minutes to a peer circle, including one ethics question.

Standard to meet. File contains all six items; presentation covers one fault found and one limit; two peers give written feedback.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: safety card, wiring diagram, calculations, program, test table, fault log, cost sheet and a recorded run. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the three steps of the robot loop.

Model answer: Sense, think (decide), act. (Covered in module 1: What a robot is: sense, think, act. To pass that module: Each diagram names one sensor, one rule and one actuator.)

Q2. What does a motor driver do?

Model answer: It switches the motor's power under control of a small signal; controller pins cannot supply motor current. (Covered in module 3: Motors and movement. To pass that module: Table complete with units; the speed rises with duty; any stall or heat is noted.)

Q3. A 5 V circuit has 10 ohm resistance. What is the current?

Model answer: I = V / R = 5 / 10 = 0.5 A. (Covered in module 6: Power and wiring: voltage, current, battery life. To pass that module: Calculations show units; measured runtime is within 30% of the prediction, or the gap is explained.)

Q4. A 2400 mAh battery supplies 800 mA. What is the ideal runtime?

Model answer: 2400 / 800 = 3 hours (real about 2.1 hours at 70%). (Covered in module 6: Power and wiring: voltage, current, battery life. To pass that module: Calculations show units; measured runtime is within 30% of the prediction, or the gap is explained.)

Q5. Why put a switch in the battery line?

Model answer: To cut all motor power quickly in a fault or for safe handling. (Covered in module 2: Safety first. To pass that module: Card lists voltage, fuse or protection, switch, charging rule and first-test method; supervisor signs.)

Q6. Line sensor reads 900 on white and 300 on black. What is the threshold?

Model answer: (900 + 300) / 2 = 600. (Covered in module 4: Sensors: how a robot knows. To pass that module: Readings table complete; threshold calculated; behaviour tested and noted in each light.)

Q7. What does 50% PWM duty mean?

Model answer: The output is on half the time, giving about half the average power. (Covered in module 3: Motors and movement. To pass that module: Table complete with units; the speed rises with duty; any stall or heat is noted.)

Q8. Give two rules for lithium battery safety.

Model answer: Use the matching charger; never charge unattended; stop use if the pack swells or heats. (Covered in module 2: Safety first. To pass that module: Card lists voltage, fuse or protection, switch, charging rule and first-test method; supervisor signs.)

Q9. Why test with wheels lifted first?

Model answer: So an unexpected movement cannot drive the robot off the table or hurt someone. (Covered in module 2: Safety first. To pass that module: Card lists voltage, fuse or protection, switch, charging rule and first-test method; supervisor signs.)

Q10. Why does this course forbid industrial robots?

Model answer: They need formal training, guarding and machine-safety rules; a beginner course cannot qualify anyone for that. (Covered in module 8: Debug, document, ethics and jobs. To pass that module: File contains all six items; presentation covers one fault found and one limit; two peers give written feedback.)

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Parts of a robot

On the slide: L1 Basic · Parts of a robot

Notes: Name the sense, think and act parts of a simple robot. Notes: use a toy or a picture; learners label sensor, controller and motor with no powered kit.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Safe handling

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Safe handling

Notes: Follow low-voltage and battery safety rules and say why wheels are lifted for the first test. Notes: run a walk-through of the switch, the charger match and the lifted-wheel test before any power is applied.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Wire and drive a motor

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Wire and drive a motor

Notes: Wire a motor driver and make a motor turn both ways at two speeds. Notes: check the 50 per cent duty setting gives about half the speed; learners record voltage and current.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Line follower or obstacle stopper

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Line follower or obstacle stopper

Notes: Build and test a small robot and write its threshold from real readings. Notes: practice case is 900 on white and 300 on black, threshold 600; learners repeat with their own sensor.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Debug and improve

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Debug and improve

Notes: Find faults using a test log and improve reliability and battery life. Notes: use 2400 mAh at 800 mA, which gives 3 hours ideal and about 2.1 hours real; learners compare their robot to the estimate.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Mentor a build group

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Mentor a build group

Notes: Supervise a group build, check safety and mark each log. Notes: use a safety sign-off sheet; learners must not allow industrial machines in the course.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Specify a robot project

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Specify a robot project

Notes: Write a project specification with safety case, test plan and budget for a larger low-voltage robot. Notes: review risks, parts and a test plan; the work stays under the 12 V and no-mains limits of this course.
