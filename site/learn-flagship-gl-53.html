flagship course

Alternative Protein Food Products

Plan a small food product built on plant, fungal or insect protein: recipe, safety, labelling, costing and honest claims.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner39:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain protein sources and the nutrition and allergy basics.
  • Apply Scale a recipe and plan a safe process.
  • Analyse Compare taste, cost and nutrition against a normal product.
  • Evaluate Judge labels and claims.
  • Create Run a tasting test and write a launch plan.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Develop one alternative-protein product to a tested recipe with hygiene plan, label draft, cost sheet and tasting results.

Condition

A clean kitchen or partner food unit, scales, a spreadsheet, 10 tasters, two peers and a food-trade mentor. Local food rules are checked.

Standard

Recipe is repeatable within 5% weight; allergen list is complete; hygiene plan passes; label meets local rules; cost per pack is shown; 10 tasters' scores recorded; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Food entrepreneurs, cooks, product developers, hospitality staff. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Protein sources and what they give 4 hUnderstand

Protein can come from pulses, grains, soy, nuts, seeds, fungi, algae and insects. A maker must know what each gives and costs.

  • Read each pack label and write protein per 100 g, noting dry or cooked.
  • Combine sources that fill each other's gaps: grains are low in lysine, pulses in methionine.
  • Compare by cost per 10 g of protein, not price per kilo alone.
  • Follow the supplier's safe method for any source needing soaking or boiling.
Worked example. Dry lentils show 24 g protein per 100 g at 120 INR per kg (prices are estimates; check your market); rice shows 7 g at 60 INR. Cost per 10 g protein is 5.0 INR for lentils and 8.6 INR for rice, so lentils lead and rice balances the amino acids.

Activity. Build a table of six sources with protein per 100 g, cost per kg, cost per 10 g protein and the label date.

Standard to meet. All six rows show values from labels or recognised tables with a date; cost per 10 g protein is calculated correctly.

Module 2 Nutrition and allergens 5 hAnalyse

A protein food must be nutritious and safe for allergic buyers. This is not health advice; ask a qualified dietitian and the food-safety office.

  • Compare with a normal product on protein, iron, fibre, salt and fat per 100 g.
  • List allergens from your local list, such as soy, nuts, gluten, milk, egg and sesame; insect protein can cross-react with shellfish.
  • Map cross-contact risks (scoops, fryers, benches) and write one control for each.
  • Give buyers no medical advice; refer special diets to a qualified adviser.
Worked example. A cricket-flour cracker shares a bench with peanuts. The maker cleans the bench, uses a colour-coded scoop and writes "Contains insect; may contain peanuts; avoid if shellfish allergic". The table shows 14 g protein and no B12, which is noted.

Activity. Write the allergen statement and a cross-contact control table.

Standard to meet. Every allergen named; each risk has a control.

Module 3 Recipe development and scaling 6 hCreate

A recipe is a method others can repeat. Test small, change one thing at a time and record every batch.

  • Weigh every ingredient in grams, not cups, and write time, temperature and equipment beside each step.
  • Make three test batches and compare weight, texture and taste; change only one variable between batches.
  • Scale by a factor (new weight = old weight x factor) and test the batch, as times change.
  • Write a batch number and date on each batch, and keep notes of what you changed and why.
Worked example. Batch 1 is too salty. Batch 2 cuts salt from 2.0% to 1.6% and weighs 0.98 kg. Batch 3 adds 2 minutes of rest and weighs 1.01 kg. The 3% spread is inside 5%, so the recipe scales by 4.

Activity. Write a recipe card with weights, steps and limits, make three batches and record the weight of each.

Standard to meet. Three batches weigh within 5% of each other; each has a batch number, date and tasting note.

Module 4 Hygiene and food safety controls 5 hApply

Safe food comes from written controls at the right steps. Local food-safety rules set exact limits; confirm them with the authority.

  • Draw the flow from receiving to sale and mark steps that remove hazards, such as cooking and cooling.
  • For each control point write a limit, a measure, a record and a fix.
  • Check thermometers in iced water (0 degrees Celsius) and log it.
  • Keep raw and cooked apart, wash hands, and keep logs of cleaning and training.
Worked example. A patty plan sets example limits (confirm local rules): core 75 degrees Celsius or more; chill from 60 to 21 degrees Celsius within 2 hours, then to 5 degrees Celsius within 4 more hours. The log shows 78 degrees Celsius at 14:10 and 5 degrees Celsius at 19:40. A failed limit means the batch is discarded.

Activity. Write a control plan with four control points and a log sheet.

Standard to meet. Each control point has a limit, a measure, a record and a corrective action; a trained person signs the log.

Module 5 Labels and honest claims 5 hEvaluate

A label is a legal, honest statement. Check your draft against local rules and a qualified adviser.

  • List name, ingredients by weight, emphasised allergens, net weight, date, storage and maker details.
  • Judge each claim against a rule: "high protein" needs a minimum amount under local law.
  • Use "vegan" or "meat-free" only after checking every ingredient.
  • Ask an adviser or the local office to review the draft, and record who said what and on which date.
Worked example. A draft says "high protein" but shows 9 g per 100 g, below the local threshold, so it becomes "9 g protein per 100 g". Allergens go in bold and net weight 250 g is added.

Activity. Draft a label with all required items and a claims check table for every claim on it.

Standard to meet. Every claim has evidence and a rule reference; an adviser check is dated; no claim is untrue or unproved.

Module 6 Costing and pricing 5 hAnalyse

Know the true cost of one pack, including waste, before choosing a price. Profit is price minus all costs, not just ingredients.

  • List ingredients, packing, fuel, labour, loss, overhead, labels and testing, and cost each per batch.
  • Cost per pack = total batch cost divided by good packs sold, so count only the packs that pass.
  • Set a price above cost with a margin, and compare it with the nearest normal product on the shelf.
  • Test a 10% rise in your biggest cost and recalculate the margin.
Worked example. A batch of 40 packs costs 1,800 INR, but 4 are lost, so 36 good packs give 50 INR each. At 80 INR the margin is 30 INR or 37.5%. A 10% lentil rise adds 60 INR to the batch, making cost 51.7 INR and margin 28.3 INR.

Activity. Build a cost sheet with all items, a loss rate, a price and a 10% cost-rise test.

Standard to meet. Totals are correct; currency is shown on every number; loss is counted; margin is stated.

Module 7 Taste testing and feedback 4 hEvaluate

Real tasters give you evidence. Their scores show what to change and what to keep.

  • Recruit at least 10 tasters, give each a number, and get consent and allergy answers before they eat.
  • Ask them to score taste, texture, appearance and value from 1 to 5, and ask one open question.
  • Record scores and comments without defending the product, then calculate the average for each item.
  • Change one thing, test again with the same method, and compare the averages.
Worked example. Ten tasters score texture 3.2. The maker cuts mixing time by 1 minute and retests. Texture now averages 4.1, but taste falls from 4.3 to 4.0, so the maker keeps the new time and adds a pinch of spice.

Activity. Run a blind tasting with 10 people and record the sheet, the averages and one change.

Standard to meet. At least 10 scores per item; consent and allergy answers kept; averages calculated; one change retested.

Module 8 Launch plan and review 5 hCreate

Start small, learn from sales and complaints, and grow only when the product is safe and repeatable.

  • Choose one channel, such as a market stall, a cafe or online, and list what it requires: licence, cold storage, delivery.
  • Plan a first run of 100 packs with a cost budget and a stock-chilling plan that matches your control limits.
  • Choose reusable or recyclable packaging and check it is safe for food.
  • Set targets for sales, complaints and waste, and set a review date.
Worked example. A weekend market stall plans 60 packs, 80 INR each. It sells 52 in two days, collects 25 email sign-ups and receives 1 complaint about salt. Revenue is 4,160 INR against a batch cost of 2,700 INR. The maker reviews at day 7.

Activity. Write a one-page launch plan with channel, budget, targets and review date.

Standard to meet. Plan has costs, rules checked, safety controls, targets and a dated review.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: recipe card, hygiene plan, label draft, cost sheet, tasting record. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

Marking rubric (Not yet, Meets, Exceeds)

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why combine protein sources?

Model answer: Different sources lack different amino acids, so a grain with a pulse gives a fuller profile than either alone, as rice with lentils does. (Covered in module 1: Protein sources and what they give. To pass that module: All six rows show values from labels or recognised tables with a date; cost per 10 g protein is calculated correctly.)

Q2. Name three allergens a protein product may contain.

Model answer: Soy, nuts and gluten are common; milk, egg, fish, shellfish and sesame are also major; insect protein can cross-react with shellfish allergy. (Covered in module 2: Nutrition and allergens. To pass that module: Every allergen named; each risk has a control.)

Q3. How do you scale a recipe safely?

Model answer: Multiply every weight by the same factor, then test the larger batch, because mixing, cooking and cooling times can change. (Covered in module 3: Recipe development and scaling. To pass that module: Three batches weigh within 5% of each other; each has a batch number, date and tasting note.)

Q4. Why weigh ingredients instead of using cups?

Model answer: Weights are exact and repeatable, so three batches can be compared within 5% and a fault can be traced. (Covered in module 3: Recipe development and scaling. To pass that module: Three batches weigh within 5% of each other; each has a batch number, date and tasting note.)

Q5. What is a control point and what must it have?

Model answer: A step such as cooking or cooling where a hazard is removed; it needs a limit, a measure, a record and a corrective action. (Covered in module 4: Hygiene and food safety controls. To pass that module: Each control point has a limit, a measure, a record and a corrective action; a trained person signs the log.)

Q6. How should cooked food be cooled?

Model answer: Quickly and within the local rule, for example from 60 to 21 degrees Celsius within 2 hours, then to 5 degrees Celsius; confirm the rule with the authority. (Covered in module 4: Hygiene and food safety controls. To pass that module: Each control point has a limit, a measure, a record and a corrective action; a trained person signs the log.)

Q7. How many tasters does this course need, and what else?

Model answer: At least 10 tasters, with consent and allergy answers, scoring taste, texture, appearance and value from 1 to 5. (Covered in module 7: Taste testing and feedback. To pass that module: At least 10 scores per item; consent and allergy answers kept; averages calculated; one change retested.)

Q8. Name four items in a cost per pack.

Model answer: Ingredients, packing, fuel, labour, loss and overhead; divide the total by good packs sold, not by packs made. (Covered in module 6: Costing and pricing. To pass that module: Totals are correct; currency is shown on every number; loss is counted; margin is stated.)

Q9. When can you call a product meat-free or vegan?

Model answer: Only after you have checked every ingredient and the cross-contact controls and know the claim is true under local rules. (Covered in module 5: Labels and honest claims. To pass that module: Every claim has evidence and a rule reference; an adviser check is dated; no claim is untrue or unproved.)

Q10. What records should you keep?

Model answer: Batch numbers and dates, temperatures, cleaning, training, tasting scores and label checks, so every claim can be shown. (Covered in module 4: Hygiene and food safety controls. To pass that module: Each control point has a limit, a measure, a record and a corrective action; a trained person signs the log.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Food laws, labelling and licences differ by place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.
