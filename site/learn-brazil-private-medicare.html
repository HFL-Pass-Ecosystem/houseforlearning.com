Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan, start and run private medicare in São Paulo: serve a patient or a payer, deliver a private clinic or plan service, keep records, and show a working month of numbers.

Condition

About 8 weeks part-time. Own phone or computer, real customers or a real practice site, a peer and a mentor for the final review. Local rules and licences checked against official sources.

Standard

Every module standard is met; all claims are true and backed by records; peers and a mentor mark the evidence 'Meets' or better.

Who it is for: students, graduates, career changers, owners, workers and returners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, tracker, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know your customer and market 4 hAnalyse

Every sound business in private medicare begins with one clear customer and one clear problem. In São Paulo and the wider state, demand is real but crowded, so a small, well-described customer group wins before a broad one.

  • In private medicare: Patients choose by waiting time, trust and cost; map the payer mix.
  • Write the customer in one sentence: a patient or a payer. Add the place, the moment of need and what they use today.
  • List five rivals or substitutes within reach. For each, record price, strength, weakness and how people reach it.
  • Interview at least eight real customers with open questions. Record exact words, not your summary.
  • Count the market in units: how many consultations could one area buy in a month? Use two public sources and your own count, then take the lower figure.
  • Choose one first segment and say why the others wait.
Worked example. Weak: 'Everyone needs a private clinic or plan service.' Strong: 'Our first customer is a patient or a payer within 8 km of the centre, who today pays R$ 385 per consultation and complains about delay. We target 300 consultations a month by month three.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: ten customers are interviewed for private medicare. Seven say price, two say speed, one says trust. Question: which first message do you write, and which interview record backs it? Model answer: lead with the consultation price and speed promise, keep trust proof as the second line, and cite the seven price answers by date.

Activity. Write the one-sentence customer, a five-row rival table and eight interview records. Share them with a peer for a challenge round.

Standard to meet. Show that customer is specific; rival table has prices; every claim traces to an interview or a source.

Module 2 Rules, registration and data duties in Brazil 4 hUnderstand

Brazil asks every trader to be visible and accountable. Know which register, tax regime and sector rules apply before the first sale, and check each against the current official text.

  • In private medicare: Clinics need health authority licences and registered clinicians; billing to health plans follows regulator rules (ANS).
  • Choose a legal form: micro-entrepreneur (MEI) only if your activity appears on the official MEI list, or a company registered with a CNPJ. Regulated activities need their sector authorisation first. Check the current limits and lists on the official portal.
  • Learn the tax regime that fits your size and write down which taxes you will collect and pay, and when.
  • Find the sector rules for private medicare: licences, inspections, professional registers. List each with the office that issues it and the renewal date.
  • Personal data of customers falls under Brazil's data protection law (LGPD). Collect only what you need, say why, keep it safe, and name who answers questions.
  • Consumer law gives buyers clear rights on information, returns and complaints. Put your terms in plain Portuguese.
Worked example. Checklist for a new private medicare trader: 1) legal form (CNPJ, or MEI only if your activity is on the official list); 2) city operating licence; 3) sector licence or register; 4) tax set-up; 5) privacy notice; 6) written terms. Each has an owner and a date.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a neighbour says registration can wait until the first R$ 1.750 of sales. Question: what do you do? Model answer: do not delay; write the compliance table first, confirm the register, tax and sector licence with the official office, and start only where the table shows no open blocker.

Activity. Build your own six-line compliance table for your first location. Mark what is done, what is pending and who confirms it.

Standard to meet. Show every line cites an official source; nothing is assumed; pending items have dates. Local law always wins over this course.

Module 3 Design the offer 4 hCreate

An offer states what the customer gets, how well, by when and at what price. For a private clinic or plan service, the offer must make the quality visible and the next step easy.

  • In private medicare: Offer a clear fee list and appointment promise.
  • Describe the core offer in three lines: what is delivered (a private clinic or plan service), the standard it meets, and the time it takes.
  • Add one proof for each promise: a sample, a test result, a guarantee or a customer record.
  • Price from cost upward and from customer value downward, then pick a price inside both.
  • Write a simple one-page terms sheet: what is included, what is extra, how to cancel.
  • Create two options at most, so choice stays easy.
Worked example. Offer: 'A private clinic or plan service, delivered within agreed time, checked against a written standard, price R$ 350 per consultation. Proof: a dated record the customer can check and sign. Changes or cancellation by written agreement before work begins.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a customer asks for a discount from R$ 350 to R$ 315 per consultation. Question: decide with numbers. Model answer: contribution falls from R$ 160 to R$ 125 per consultation; offer an extra of value (for example faster service) before cutting price, and accept only if the lower contribution still clears break-even.

Longer notes. Name the service your practice will give, such as check-ups, vaccination counselling or home visits, and who is qualified to perform each. List the rooms, equipment, cleaning steps and records needed before the first patient. Write the patient journey from booking to follow-up, with a consent form and a privacy notice for health data. Prepare a price list for consultations and exams and show it at booking. Explain what you will not treat and when you refer on. Confirm the clinic licence and each professional's registration with the proper health bodies, and lock patient files. Never promise cures or results.

Extra worked example. A practice sees 25 patients a day for 20 days, so 500 consultations; the pace is a worked-example figure. At R$ 350 per consultation the income is 500 x R$ 350 = R$ 175.000. Doctor fees, supplies and lab costs are R$ 190 per consultation, or R$ 95.000, leaving R$ 80.000. Fixed cost for rent, staff and equipment is R$ 60.000, so profit is R$ 20.000. Break-even is R$ 60.000 / R$ 160 = 375 consultations, which the doctor reaches only at about 19 patients a day over 20 days.

Activity. Write the offer card, the proof list and the terms sheet. Test them with three customers and note every point where they hesitated.

Standard to meet. Show that promise, proof and price are consistent; terms fit one page; three customer reactions are recorded.

Module 4 Run the work step by step 5 hApply

Good operations make results repeatable. For private medicare, write each step so a new person could follow it on their first day, and measure each step.

  • In private medicare: Flow: booking, check-in, consult, tests, follow-up, billing; one record per patient.
  • Map the process from first contact to paid and closed. Typical stages: enquiry, quote, preparation, delivery of the consultation, check, payment, follow-up.
  • Name the key resource at each stage: registered clinicians and records. Note what happens if it is missing.
  • Write a one-page standard procedure per stage: who, what, tool, time, record.
  • Choose three measures: speed, quality (errors per 100) and customer rating. Review them every week.
  • Fix the cause of a defect, not the symptom. Ask 'why' five times and record the answer.
Worked example. Stage record: 'Prepare. Owner: lead. Time: 30 min. Tool: checklist. Record: signed sheet. Defect found on 1 of 125 consultations last week; cause: missing check on registered clinicians and records; fix: add check at start, review next Friday.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: of 500 consultations last month, 30 had a defect traced to registered clinicians and records. Question: what is the defect rate and what is your first action? Model answer: about 6 defects per 100; stop the cause (add a check at the start), correct affected customers, log the cause, and re-measure next week.

Activity. Draw the process map and write procedures for the two riskiest stages. Run each once and time it.

Standard to meet. Show that procedures are followable by a stranger; measures have weekly numbers; one defect is traced to a root cause.

Module 5 Safety, quality and risk 4 hEvaluate

The most serious risk in this field is a missed diagnosis or a billing error. A calm, written risk routine protects people first and the business second.

  • In private medicare: Misdiagnosis, infection and billing errors are risks; peer review and audit.
  • List ten things that could go wrong. Score each for how likely (1 to 5) and how serious (1 to 5). Multiply and sort.
  • For the top five, choose a control: remove the hazard, replace it, guard it, train people, or use protective gear, in that order of preference.
  • Write who does what in an emergency, and rehearse it once a quarter.
  • Keep an incident and near-miss log. Near-misses are free lessons.
  • Check that insurance and legal duties match the risk you actually carry.
Worked example. Risk row: 'Hazard: a missed diagnosis or a billing error. Likelihood 3, severity 5, score 15. Control: written check before each consultation, trained staff, log sheet. Owner: site lead. Review monthly.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a near-miss occurs: a missed diagnosis or a billing error nearly happened but no one was hurt. Question: what do you do in the next 24 hours? Model answer: make safe, record the event, ask the five whys, add or tighten one control, brief the team, and set a review date; near-misses count as lessons, not blame.

Activity. Build a ten-row risk register and a one-page emergency plan. Run a ten-minute drill with a peer and note two improvements.

Standard to meet. Show that top risks have controls ranked by preference; drill done; log started. Where a regulation applies, follow the official rule.

Module 6 People, skills and service 4 hApply

People deliver the promise. Define roles, train to a standard and give feedback in the open.

  • In private medicare: Train front desk and clinicians on privacy and consent.
  • Write a role card for each person: purpose, five tasks, the standard for each, and who to ask for help.
  • Train by showing, doing with support, then doing alone with a check. Record sign-off per task.
  • Set service rules for answering customers: reply times, tone, how to apologise and fix.
  • Hold a ten-minute team check each day: yesterday's problem, today's plan, one risk.
  • Build a growth path: what a new person can do in week 1, month 3 and month 12.
Worked example. Training record: 'Task: process a complaint. Shown 2 May; done with support 4 May; done alone 8 May, checked: apology, fix, follow-up call. Signed by lead.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a new hire completes training but a customer complaint shows a skipped step. Question: whose gap is it? Model answer: first the system: check the role card, the training sign-off and the checklist; then coach the person and fix the document so the step cannot be skipped.

Longer notes. Assemble the clinic team: the professional, a receptionist, a cleaner and a person who keeps records. Write a role sheet for each, with consent checks at reception and a stop rule when a patient shows alarming symptoms. Teach hand hygiene and instrument cleaning with a printed sequence and a timer. Rehearse an emergency: call the ambulance, bring the kit, note the times. Train the front desk to confirm name, birth date and permission before giving results. Lock paper files and give each person a computer password. Hold a fifteen-minute huddle each morning and record accidents and near misses in a book.

Extra worked example. Of the R$ 60.000 fixed cost, front-desk payroll is R$ 6.800: R$ 2.800 for reception, R$ 1.800 for cleaning and R$ 2.200 for records. These wages and the R$ 18 hourly value below are worked-example figures, not market data. Training takes 4 hours for each of 3 people, so the cost is 3 x 4 x R$ 18 = R$ 216. If fewer booking errors save 2 consultations a month, the margin gained is 2 x R$ 160 = R$ 320, more than the training costs. Repeat the drill after six months.

Activity. Write two role cards and a training plan for one new hire. Role-play a hard customer conversation twice and review.

Standard to meet. Show that role cards have measurable standards; training shows sign-off steps; service rules are written in plain words.

Module 7 Money: cost, price and break-even 5 hAnalyse

A business lives or dies on a few numbers. Know your contribution per unit, your fixed costs and the volume you need.

  • In private medicare: Collect from plans and patients; track denied claims and days to pay.
  • Contribution per consultation = price minus variable cost. Fixed costs are paid whether you sell or not.
  • Break-even units = fixed cost divided by contribution. Plan to pass it by a safe margin.
  • Make a 12-month cash plan. Cash timing matters as much as profit: customers may pay late, suppliers may want money first.
  • Keep money for business and money for the owner apart.
  • Test the plan: what if sales fall by a quarter, or cost rises by a tenth?
Worked example. Worked example, all figures in reais: price R$ 350, variable cost R$ 190, contribution R$ 160 per consultation. Fixed cost R$ 60.000 a month. Break-even = R$ 60.000 / R$ 160 = 375 consultations a month. At 500 consultations a month, profit = R$ 160 x 500 - R$ 60.000 = R$ 20.000. These are teaching figures that belong to this example only; later examples and the case study use their own figures. Use your own.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: sales fall to 375 consultations a month. Question: what is the monthly result? Model answer: R$ 160 x 375 - R$ 60.000 = R$ 0; compare with break-even of 375 consultations and decide whether to cut fixed cost or raise volume first. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build your own table: price, variable cost, contribution, fixed cost, break-even and a downside case at 75% of sales. Show it to a peer or mentor.

Standard to meet. Show that arithmetic is correct; every figure has a source or is marked as an assumption; a downside case is shown.

Module 8 Grow, measure and renew 4 hEvaluate

Growth should follow proof. Measure, learn, then add; and keep returning to the customer.

  • In private medicare: Grow by waiting time and outcome measures.
  • Pick one growth lever at a time: new customers, a higher repeat rate, a better price, or a new offer.
  • Set one measurable target for 90 days, such as 'raise repeat customers from 20% to 30%'.
  • Ask each customer how they found you, and record it. Spend more where the record shows results.
  • Review monthly: numbers, defects, complaints, cash. Write three decisions each time.
  • Plan the renewal of licences, equipment and skills a year ahead.
Worked example. 90-day plan: 'Lever: repeat customers. Action: follow-up call after every consultation. Target: repeat rate from 20% to 30%. Check: weekly count. Review date: day 45 and day 90.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: you can add 100 consultations a month by one of two levers: a follow-up call to past customers or a price rise of R$ 35 to R$ 385. Question: how do you choose? Model answer: test the cheaper reversible lever first (the call), measure for 30 days, and only then try the price change, so you can tell which one worked.

Longer notes. Track waiting time, missed appointments and return visits each month. Phone patients who missed and note whether the cause was cost, distance or forgetfulness. Send a reminder the day before and compare no-show counts across two months. Compare real consultation length with the slot to see if slots are too short. Read complaints and thank-you notes together and list three fixes. Ask patients, without pressure, how they found you. Consider evening hours only after checking that demand justifies them. Never use patient names or photos in promotion without written permission, and keep every advertising claim to services the clinic actually provides.

Extra worked example. In this worked example, of 640 booked visits, 128 are missed, a no-show rate of 20%. The 512 completed consultations bring profit of 512 x R$ 160 - R$ 60.000 = R$ 21.920. After day-before reminders, 64 are missed, or 10%, and 576 are completed, with profit of R$ 32.160. The booking counts are invented, not market data. The recovered slots add 64 x R$ 160 = R$ 10.240. Keep the reminder only if the change repeats for a second month, and tell patients how their phone number is used.

Activity. Write a 90-day plan with one target, three actions and a review date. Present it in five minutes and take questions.

Standard to meet. Show that target is measurable and dated; actions are small enough to start this week; review date is in the calendar.

case study

A case to work through

Situation. A private clinic in Tatuape sees about 500 consultations a month and charges R$ 350 per consultation. Doctor fees, supplies and lab work cost R$ 190 per consultation, and rent, staff and equipment cost R$ 60.000 a month. Patients are confused about which services their health plan covers. Last month 45 patients disputed a bill, and one paid R$ 540 for a consultation and an exam that she thought were covered. The clinic does not give a written price and coverage sheet, and staff answer differently. The dispute count and the R$ 540 bill are invented for the exercise. The owner wonders if the clinic truly covers its costs, and whether unclear prices are driving patients away. She wants a clear method to fix this using her own numbers, without rushing into new equipment.

Task. Say what you would do first, next and last, and what record you would keep.

Model answer. First, compute the month: income 500 x R$ 350 = R$ 175.000; variable cost 500 x R$ 190 = R$ 95.000; margin R$ 80.000; fixed cost R$ 60.000; profit R$ 20.000. Each consultation leaves R$ 160, so break-even is R$ 60.000 / R$ 160 = 375 consultations. Next, compute the disputes: 45 of 500 is 9%. If 50 patients left because of unclear prices, margin lost is 50 x R$ 160 = R$ 8.000, and profit falls to R$ 12.000. Then write a one-page sheet listing each service, its price and what a plan usually does or does not cover, and ask each plan, in writing, to confirm co-payments and waiting periods. Train the front desk to read from the sheet so every patient hears the same answer. After a month, count disputes again: under 3% of consultations, fewer than 15, means the sheet is working.

levels L1 to L7

Where this sits on the ladder

Seven levels, one slide each. Read the slide, then read the speaker notes under it. Credits stack and you can step in and out; this course is L4.

Slide 1 of 7: L1 Basic - Private healthcare

  • Define patient, referral, record and consent
  • Recall privacy basics
  • Name common clinic roles
Notes for slide 1

Learners work with dummy records: a patient registers, is referred, signs a consent form, and a record is opened and closed. They practise privacy habits, such as not leaving screens open, not discussing cases in lifts and verifying identity. They name roles like receptionist, nurse, doctor and billing clerk. Hazard: a record seen by the wrong person. Evidence is a role chart and a privacy quiz.

Slide 2 of 7: L2 Awareness - Private healthcare

  • Describe how private clinics and plans work
  • Compare clinic, lab and home services
  • Identify patient needs in Sao Paulo
Notes for slide 2

Learners explain how a private clinic earns from self-paying patients and health plan reimbursements, and compare a consulting room, a diagnostic lab and home visits. They interview one patient about getting an appointment, waiting and paying. Evidence is a two-page needs summary for a district such as Mooca or Pinheiros, with sources; needs are described, never medical claims.

Slide 3 of 7: L3 Foundation - Private healthcare

  • Map a patient visit from booking to follow-up
  • Build a simple price and schedule sheet
  • List the health licences and data rules to check
Notes for slide 3

A patient journey map follows booking, reminder, arrival, consultation, payment, results and follow-up call, with timing at each step. Learners build a service sheet listing durations and a weekly slot grid, plus a checklist of health authority licences and data protection rules to read in official text. Hazard: double booking. Evidence: map, sheet and checklist; a role-played visit runs on time.

Slide 4 of 7: L4 Practitioner - Private healthcare

  • Plan and open a private health service in Sao Paulo
  • Set staff, rooms, booking and billing
  • Run a pilot clinic week and review
Notes for slide 4

A practitioner plans the rooms, equipment, staffing and opening hours for a small clinic, sets up booking software, billing codes and reminders, and runs a pilot week with volunteer patients. Records are the booking log, waiting times and satisfaction cards. Hazards: no-shows and billing errors. Evidence is a pilot report with fixes; every patient is followed up and every charge reconciled.

Slide 5 of 7: L5 Specialist - Private healthcare

  • Improve waiting time and patient experience
  • Negotiate with labs and suppliers
  • Track quality and safety measures
Notes for slide 5

Specialists time each step of a visit to cut waiting, redesign the front desk flow and send reminders to lower no-shows. They compare lab and supplier quotes on turnaround and quality control, and follow indicators such as infection incidents, complaint counts and repeat visits. Evidence is a before-and-after waiting chart, a supplier comparison and an indicator dashboard showing improvement.

Slide 6 of 7: L6 Advanced - Private healthcare

  • Lead a multi-service clinic team
  • Model income and cost per service
  • Manage audits, incidents and complaints
Notes for slide 6

Clinic managers coordinate several services, such as consultations, imaging and minor procedures, working out income and cost per service and per room-hour. They prepare for audits by keeping licences, equipment calibrations and training records current, and log incidents with root-cause reviews. Complaints are answered in writing. Evidence is an audit file and a service result sheet; nothing overdue.

Slide 7 of 7: L7 Super expert - Private healthcare

  • Set strategy for a healthcare group
  • Mentor clinical and business leaders
  • Advise on ethics, growth and patient trust
Notes for slide 7

Group leaders decide which services to open, how doctors and managers are supported, and how to protect patient trust when mistakes happen. They coach clinical and business leaders through ethics cases, for instance pressure to over-treat or conflicts of interest. Evidence is a governance framework, a case review log and a growth plan; patient interest is recorded first.

ten locations and global

Your place

Local rules change licences, taxes, data duties and customer rights. This course is written for São Paulo. Check the current official text for your own place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registration: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registration: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registration: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registration: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registration: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registration: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registration: registration with ACRA. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registration: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registration: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.
