Patient Advocacy

Run a real patient Advocacy pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small patient Advocacy offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: health, care, wellness and education. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know what patient advocacy is 6 hUnderstand

A patient advocate helps a person get clear information, be heard and make their own choices in health care. This course is education, not medical or legal advice; consult a licensed professional for your own care.

  • Explain that an advocate supports a patient and does not decide for them.
  • Name the kinds of help: explaining, organising papers, going to visits, speaking up, finding services.
  • Explain the difference between an advocate, a doctor, a lawyer and a social worker.
  • Explain that the patient's wishes come first.
  • Name who may need an advocate: older adults, people with disabilities, newcomers and anyone feeling overwhelmed.
  • Describe three promises of an advocate: respect, privacy and honesty about limits.
  • List five situations where an advocate can help and one where the advocate should refer.
Worked example. A man gets a hard diagnosis and many forms. The advocate helps him write his questions, sits in the visit and writes notes, but he decides which treatment to ask about. The advocate does not argue for a different choice.

Activity. Write a one-page glossary of ten terms and a short note on what an advocate does and does not do.

Standard to meet. Task: define the role. Condition: one page, own words. Standard: terms are correct and the limits of the role are clear.

Module 2 Understand the Canadian health system and patient rights 6 hApply

Health care in Canada is run mostly by provinces. Know the basics before you help.

  • Explain that each province runs its own public health insurance and that rules, wait times and covered services differ; check the current rule locally.
  • Explain common paths: family doctor or clinic, specialist referral, hospital, home care.
  • Explain that patients have the right to information and to consent or refuse; check the current rights statement for your province.
  • Explain complaint paths: the hospital patient relations office, a provincial patient ombudsman or advocate office where one exists, or the college that regulates the professional.
  • Explain that coverage for newcomers and for drugs varies; check the current rule locally.
  • Explain that patients may ask for a second opinion; check the rule in your province.
  • Find the name and phone number of the patient relations office at your local hospital.
Worked example. A patient is unhappy about a six-hour wait. The advocate finds the hospital patient relations office and helps write a clear, dated, polite note. The note gives the date and times so the office can look into it.

Activity. Make a map of your province's care paths and complaint routes with the official source for each.

Standard to meet. Task: map the system. Condition: official pages only. Standard: each path has a name, a purpose and a dated source; nothing is guessed.

Module 3 Protect privacy and get consent 10 hApply

Health information is deeply private. Without consent you cannot see or share it.

  • Explain that provincial health privacy laws and federal privacy law apply; check the one for your province.
  • Get written consent from the patient before you see records or speak to the care team for them.
  • Record what you may share, with whom and until when.
  • Keep notes safe and share them only as agreed.
  • Explain what to do if the patient cannot consent: the legal substitute decision-maker; check the local rule and ask a licensed professional.
  • Go through the consent form with the patient and check each line is understood before signing.
  • Write down when consent ends and set a date to review it.
Worked example. A daughter wants to help her mother. The mother signs a form naming the daughter and the advocate and stating what can be discussed. The form is kept in a locked folder and reviewed after 90 days.

Activity. Write a one-page consent form and a privacy promise in plain words.

Standard to meet. Task: draft consent and privacy forms. Condition: plain words. Standard: they state who, what, why, how long and how to withdraw.

Module 4 Listen and clarify what the patient wants 8 hEvaluate

Good advocacy starts with listening. Help the person name what matters to them.

  • Ask open questions: what worries you, what do you hope for, what do you need to know?
  • Repeat back what you heard to be sure you understood.
  • Write a short list of the patient's goals and questions.
  • Notice language, culture, hearing or reading needs and arrange help such as an interpreter.
  • Never push your own opinion; give options and let the patient choose.
  • Use plain words and avoid jargon; ask the patient to say the plan back in their own words.
  • Ask what a good outcome looks like for the patient.
Worked example. A patient says 'I do not understand the plan'. The advocate asks which part. It is the medicine schedule. The goal is one clear page, made with the pharmacist; if there are 4 medicines each taken twice a day, the page shows 4 x 2 = 8 doses a day.

Activity. Role-play a listening session and produce a goals-and-questions page.

Standard to meet. Task: clarify needs. Condition: a role-play partner. Standard: the page shows the patient's own words and the partner agrees it is accurate.

Module 5 Prepare for and attend visits 8 hApply

A prepared patient gets more from a short visit.

  • Before the visit: write top three questions, list medicines, symptoms and dates.
  • Bring a notebook and ask to repeat anything unclear.
  • During the visit: note what was said, what was decided and who does what next.
  • After the visit: read the notes back to the patient and fix errors.
  • Make a follow-up list with dates and phone numbers.
  • Plan transport, timing and who can come with the patient.
  • Ask the care team how to reach them with questions after the visit.
Worked example. The visit page lists three questions. The doctor answers two. The advocate notes the third was not answered and asks it before they leave. If it is still unanswered, it goes on the follow-up list with a date to ask again.

Activity. Prepare a visit page, attend or role-play a visit and write a follow-up list.

Standard to meet. Task: support a visit. Condition: a real or role-play visit. Standard: questions were asked, decisions were written and a follow-up list was made.

Module 6 Keep records and organise information 6 hCreate

Many problems come from lost papers. Simple records protect the patient.

  • Keep a health file: contact list, medicines, test dates, referrals and letters.
  • Write a timeline of events with date, who and what.
  • Keep a log of calls: date, person, what was said, next step.
  • Store records safely and back them up with the patient's consent.
  • Explain how a person can ask for their records; check the current process in your province.
  • Date and sign every entry.
  • Keep copies of letters sent and the replies.
Worked example. A timeline shows a referral made on 3 March with no reply by 1 May. From 3 March to 1 May is 28 + 30 + 1 = 59 days. That fact lets the advocate call the clinic with exact dates.

Activity. Build a health file template and a call log and use them for a practice case.

Standard to meet. Task: organise records. Condition: a practice case. Standard: dates, names and next steps are complete and easy to find.

Module 7 Speak up, solve problems and know your limits 6 hEvaluate

Advocacy is firm and polite. Know when to stop and refer.

  • Write short, clear messages: what happened, what you want, by when.
  • Use the proper complaint route and keep a dated copy.
  • Stay calm and respectful; the goal is a fix, not a fight.
  • Do not give medical or legal advice; refer to a licensed professional.
  • Know emergencies: call emergency services when life is at risk.
  • Use a short template: date, issue, what you want, deadline and your contact.
  • List who to contact next if a first complaint gets no answer, following the complaint route.
Worked example. On 10 May a test result was promised within 5 days, so it was due on 15 May. On 16 May the advocate writes: 'The result due on 15 May has not arrived. Please send it or tell us when it will be ready.' A reply comes in two days.

Activity. Write three messages for practice cases and a list of when to refer.

Standard to meet. Task: write messages. Condition: three practice cases. Standard: each states facts, request and date, and no advice outside your role is given.

Module 8 Run an advocacy service for real 8 hCreate

Run it with care: consent, records, boundaries and review. Work under supervision at first.

  • Work with a supervisor, a community group or a mentor for real cases.
  • Keep a case file for each person with consent and notes.
  • Review each case: what the patient wanted, what happened, what you learned.
  • Look after yourself: debrief, limits and breaks.
  • Write a 12-month plan; check whether any local rules apply to charging fees.
  • Sort case outcomes into solved, partly solved and referred.
  • Share lessons with your supervisor with identifying details removed.
Worked example. After 60 days an advocate sees that 6 of 10 cases stall on lost referrals. She builds a referral tracker and shares it with her supervisor. Pricing note: if a paid advocacy hour were priced at 60 CAD with 5 CAD of direct cost for printing and transport, the margin would be 55 CAD an hour, or 1,650 CAD over 30 hours; check the local rules on charging fees before you charge anyone.

Activity. Run a 60-day supervised pilot and present the review to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run an advocacy pilot. Condition: 60 days with supervision and consent. Standard: records are complete, privacy is kept and the review reasons from evidence.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
