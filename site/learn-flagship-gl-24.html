Selling Personal Protective Equipment

Sell protective equipment responsibly: match the right item to the hazard, check standards and labels, advise honestly and keep records.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Sell protective equipment responsibly: match the right item to the hazard, check standards and labels, advise honestly and keep records.

  • Understand Explain how hazards, protection types and standards fit together.
  • Apply Apply a hazard-to-equipment match for common workplaces.
  • Analyse Analyse supplier claims and labels for evidence of conformity.
  • Create Create a buyer guide and a safe-sales checklist.
  • Evaluate Evaluate your advice for accuracy, honesty and limits.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Build a buyer guide and a sales process for five types of protective equipment, with supplier checks and a records system.

Condition

Product samples or catalogues, standards summaries from public sources, a spreadsheet, a mentor with safety experience and a peer circle. About 8 to 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Each product matched to a named hazard; each supplier check shows a dated conformity document; no medical or false protection claims; records kept for batch numbers; mentor confirms advice is within your role.

Who it is for: Shop and trade-counter staff, distributors, safety assistants, small traders. Requirements: None. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Wrong equipment can injure. Sell only items with valid conformity evidence for your place, never overstate protection, and send complex risk questions to a qualified safety adviser.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Hazards and protection 4 hUnderstand

Equipment is the last line of defence, used after the hazard has been removed or reduced. You will be able to name the main workplace hazards and explain why protective gear is the final defence after removing or reducing the risk itself.

  • Order of control: remove the hazard, substitute a safer option, guard it, train people, then protect.
  • Hazards: impact, cut, noise, dust, chemical, heat, falls.
  • Body areas: head, eyes, ears, lungs, hands, feet, body.
  • No item protects against everything.
  • Start every sale by naming the hazard, such as dust, loud noise, splashes or falling objects, before you name a product, so the customer buys for the risk.
  • Before recommending anything, ask three questions: what task the buyer does, for how many hours each day, and what the work area is like, then write the answers on the sale note.
  • Walk a workplace with a checklist and write the hazards by area.
  • Ask workers which tasks cause the most discomfort.
Worked example. A worker cutting tiles: dust and flying chips; protect eyes and lungs, ears if loud.

Activity. Make a hazard-to-body map for three workplaces.

Standard to meet. Each hazard linked to a body area.

Common mistake. Treating protective equipment as the first fix.

Second worked example. A worker cutting tiles faces dust and flying chips. The control order: use wet cutting to cut dust, then eye protection and a suitable mask, ear protection if the saw is loud.

Check yourself. What hazard could be removed before any item is sold?

Module 2 Standards and markings 6 hUnderstand

Markings show tested performance. You will be able to read standard numbers and ratings on labels and match a marking to the task it was tested for.

  • Look for the marking, class and the standard number for your place.
  • Markings on products and boxes; read the notice of use.
  • Expiry dates and life limits.
  • Do not accept a mark you cannot trace.
  • Look for the standard number and rating printed on the item itself, not only on the box, and keep a photo of each marking in your product file.
  • Compare the marking with the hazard: a mask rated for light dust is unsuitable for fine hazardous powder, so always match the rating class to the task.
  • Compare two similar items and explain the difference in markings.
  • Ask suppliers for test report numbers and keep a copy.
Worked example. Gloves marked for cut level C protect better than level A; the number and picture show which risk.

Activity. Decode markings on five samples.

Standard to meet. Each marking explained.

Common mistake. Trusting a mark because it looks official.

Second worked example. A hard hat shows an impact class and a date code; you read the date, see it is 6 years old, and tell the buyer to check the maker's life limit before use.

Check yourself. Which marking on your sample can you not trace?

Module 3 Check suppliers 8 hApply

Trust documents, not promises. You will be able to vet suppliers by checking certificates and delivery history, so the goods you stock rest on evidence, not claims.

  • Ask for conformity declarations (written statements that the item meets its standard) and test reports.
  • Check they match the product and batch.
  • Verify with the issuing body where possible.
  • Keep copies and dates.
  • Ask each supplier for a current test certificate and check its issue date; if the certificate is older than the latest product design change, request an updated one.
  • Keep a supplier file with certificate, contact, delivery record and any returns, and review it each year to drop unreliable sources.
  • Record the date you checked every document.
  • Teach the put-on, check and take-off order for each item.
Worked example. A supplier's certificate names a different model: reject until fixed.

Activity. Review three supplier documents.

Standard to meet. Each review ends with accept, query or reject.

Common mistake. Accepting a certificate for a different model.

Second worked example. A supplier's certificate names model X-1 but the listing is X-2. You reject the listing and ask for the right document, with the date of the check noted.

Check yourself. Which supplier document did you query?

Module 4 Fit and use 8 hApply

Wrong fit means no protection. You will be able to fit an item to a person and demonstrate its use, because a poorly fitted mask or glove gives little protection.

  • Sizes and adjustments.
  • Compatibility: glasses with masks, ear and head gear.
  • Training: put on, check, take off, clean.
  • Replace when damaged.
  • Let the customer try the item on and move around for two minutes, checking for gaps, pressure points and fogging before the sale is completed.
  • Show the customer how to put on, adjust, check and store the item, and hand over a one-page card so they repeat the steps correctly at work.
  • Show how to clean and store equipment.
  • Offer a low-cost and a better option with clear reasons.
Worked example. A mask with a beard gap leaks; advise a different type or a clean shave where allowed.

Activity. Write a fitting guide for two items.

Standard to meet. Guide has check steps.

Common mistake. Ignoring facial hair or head size when fitting.

Second worked example. A half mask leaks on a bearded worker. You advise a loose-fitting hood type, show the fit check and note the limit in the guide.

Check yourself. What fit check did you teach?

Module 5 Advise honestly 8 hAnalyse

Say what an item does and does not do. You will be able to describe each product's limits in plain words, so no buyer assumes cover the item cannot provide.

  • Ask about the task, place, duration.
  • Offer two options with cost.
  • Never promise full safety.
  • Send tricky cases to an adviser.
  • Say plainly what an item does not protect against, for example that a basic dust mask does not stop fumes, and point the buyer to the right product or an expert.
  • Never promise total safety; use wording such as "designed to reduce", and refer complex or high-risk jobs to a qualified safety adviser.
  • Label shelves with batch numbers and dates.
  • Separate damaged returns from good stock.
Worked example. Customer asks for one mask for paint, dust and welding; explain they are different hazards.

Activity. Role-play three customer questions.

Standard to meet. Advice accurate and polite.

Common mistake. Saying "this will keep you safe".

Second worked example. A customer wants cheap ear plugs for a loud sawmill; you explain that the noise level matters, ask for the reading, and offer ear muffs rated for it at 28 instead of plugs at 3 (an estimate; check your market). Amounts are in your currency.

Check yourself. What does your item not protect against?

Module 6 Price, stock and records 10 hCreate

Records protect you and the buyer. You will be able to link batch numbers to sales and price by cost per use, enabling quick recalls and fair comparisons.

  • Batch numbers on invoices.
  • Stock rotation for dated items.
  • Recalls: contact all buyers fast.
  • Fair prices; no panic selling.
  • Record each batch number against the sales it covers so you can contact buyers quickly if a fault is found, and keep the record for the item's expected life.
  • Compare prices by cost per use, for example gloves lasting two weeks against a cheaper pair lasting two days, and show the buyer this comparison honestly.
  • Check platform rules for health claims in adverts.
  • Keep a list of claims you removed.
Worked example. A batch recall: 40 buyers contacted in 24 hours from the sales log.

Activity. Set up a batch log and test a recall.

Standard to meet. Recall test under 24 hours.

Common mistake. Selling expired stock.

Second worked example. A pack of 200 gloves is sold ten at a time; the log lets you find the 3 buyers of a lot with a stitching fault the same afternoon.

Check yourself. How do you rotate dated items?

Module 7 Ethics and rules 6 hEvaluate

Avoid false or illegal claims. You will be able to spot false claims, counterfeit stock and rule breaches, protecting your customers and your shop's name.

  • No health cures or guaranteed protection.
  • Import and sale rules for your place.
  • Warranty and returns for used items.
  • Report unsafe products.
  • Never sell counterfeit or expired items, and remove any item with damaged packaging or a missing marking from the shelf on the day you notice it.
  • Check the product-labelling and selling rules that apply where you trade, keep receipts and warranty terms, and tell each customer how to report a fault or return a product.
  • Add pictures to the buyer guide.
  • Review the guide when standards change.
Worked example. An advert claims "100% safe": rewrite as "tested to the stated standard".

Activity. Review five adverts for claims.

Standard to meet. Each claim fixed or removed.

Common mistake. Copying a competitor's claim.

Second worked example. A leaflet says 'stops all accidents'; you replace it with 'helps protect against the hazards named on the label when worn correctly'.

Check yourself. Which advert claim did you change?

Module 8 Guide and review 4 hCreate

Package your knowledge. You will be able to turn your product knowledge into a one-page advice card and improve it using complaint and return patterns.

  • One-page buyer guide.
  • Safe-sales checklist.
  • Peer review.
  • Plan 90 days.
  • Run a short checklist at the end of each sale: hazard named, item matched, fit checked, advice given and record saved, then count the fully completed sales every week.
  • Review complaints and returns every month, find the most common cause, and then update your advice card or change your supplier choice to remove it.
  • Send complex cases to a qualified safety adviser.
  • Write three lessons and a 90-day plan.
Worked example. Guide cut from three pages to one using pictures.

Activity. Publish and test the guide.

Standard to meet. Three users find the right item.

Common mistake. Writing a guide that hides limits.

Second worked example. The one-page guide has a 'Not for' line for each item; three test users pick the right item in under a minute.

Check yourself. Does each guide entry state a limit?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The wrong mask

A customer buys a dust mask for spray painting.

Question: What do you do?

Model answer: Explain the hazard is vapour, suggest the correct respirator type and send complex cases to a safety adviser.

Case 2. The recall

A maker recalls a batch of gloves.

Question: What steps?

Model answer: Use the batch log to contact buyers within 24 hours, remove stock and keep records.

Glossary

Hazard
Something that can cause harm.
Risk
Chance of harm times how bad it is.
Conformity declaration
The maker's statement that a product meets a standard.
Batch
Items made together and traceable.
Notice of use
Instructions and limits supplied with the item.
Recall
Calling back unsafe products.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is the order of control?

Model answer: Remove, substitute, guard, train, then protect. (Covered in module 1: Hazards and protection. To pass that module: Each hazard linked to a body area.)

Q2. Why check conformity documents?

Model answer: To confirm the product was tested. (Covered in module 3: Check suppliers. To pass that module: Each review ends with accept, query or reject.)

Q3. Can one item protect against everything?

Model answer: No. (Covered in module 1: Hazards and protection. To pass that module: Each hazard linked to a body area.)

Q4. Why record batch numbers?

Model answer: To contact buyers in a recall. (Covered in module 6: Price, stock and records. To pass that module: Recall test under 24 hours.)

Q5. Why not say "100% safe"?

Model answer: It is false and can be unlawful. (Covered in module 7: Ethics and rules. To pass that module: Each claim fixed or removed.)

Q6. What if a customer has a complex risk?

Model answer: Send them to a qualified safety adviser. (Covered in module 5: Advise honestly. To pass that module: Advice accurate and polite.)

Q7. Why check fit?

Model answer: A poor fit lets hazards through. (Covered in module 4: Fit and use. To pass that module: Guide has check steps.)

Q8. Where find limits on use?

Model answer: The notice of use. (Covered in module 2: Standards and markings. To pass that module: Each marking explained.)

Q9. What to do with a mismatched certificate?

Model answer: Reject until corrected. (Covered in module 3: Check suppliers. To pass that module: Each review ends with accept, query or reject.)

Q10. Why question the task?

Model answer: The right item depends on the hazard. (Covered in module 5: Advise honestly. To pass that module: Advice accurate and polite.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Standards, markings and import rules for protective equipment differ greatly by place: use the local regulator's list. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Basics of safety

On the slide: L1 Basic · Basics of safety

Notes: Name the main hazards at work (cuts, dust, noise, chemicals) and the kind of protection for each. Notes: tour a workplace or a home workshop and photograph the hazards. Standard: each hazard has a protection type.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Awareness

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Awareness

Notes: Describe the main protection types and why a wrong match is dangerous. Notes: handle samples under supervision and read the labels. Standard: you can say what each label means.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Foundation

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Foundation

Notes: Match a user's job to the right equipment and explain what the buyer must check (rating, fit, expiry). Notes: use five case cards. Standard: four of five matched with reasons.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Practitioner

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Practitioner

Notes: Run a sale conversation with a practice buyer, check needs, explain limits and refuse an unsafe request. Notes: record the refusals. Standard: no sale made against the rating or the law.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Specialist

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Specialist

Notes: Build a buyer guide and a sales process for five types of protective equipment, with supplier checks and a records system. Notes: records cover batch, expiry, certificate and recall steps. Standard: each product traces to a certificate and each recall step is tested.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Advanced

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Advanced

Notes: Run a supply operation with audits, recall drills and staff training. Notes: add a certificate check at each delivery. Standard: audit findings closed and recall drill within the target time.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Super expert

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Super expert

Notes: Set the hazard-to-protection matching method for selling protective equipment and teach the sellers who use it. Notes: re-read the standards and labelling rules for the place each year and refresh the sales scripts.
