Module 9: Cross-cultural team dynamics and conflict arbitration

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 9

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 16 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Written memo 30%
  • Live interactive simulation 35%
  • Boardroom presentation 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

Weights shown follow the shared pattern for modules 3 to 14, and will be confirmed.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task

Lead a mixed-culture team: measure its health, set decision rules, plan meetings across time zones and arbitrate a conflict.

Condition

Given a team profile, survey scores, work hours for three sites and a conflict case.

Standard

Measures are calculated correctly, a decision rule fits the risk, the meeting plan has real overlap and the arbitration ends in a written agreement.

Who it is for: Team leaders and managers who work with people in several countries.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 How mixed teams form and stall 9 hUnderstand: explain

Teams from different countries pass through stages: meeting, friction, rules, then steady work. Cultural gaps often make the friction stage longer.

  • Expect friction and plan for it
  • Set shared rules early
  • Check the stage every month

Worked example: A team of 8 starts a project. After four weeks, a pulse check asks each person to rate 'we agree how we work' from 1 to 5. The scores are 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 2, 3, which total 22. The mean is 22 / 8 = 2.75. A score under 3.5 (an example cut-off) means the team is still in friction, so the lead holds a rules session before adding new tasks.

Activity: Run a five-question pulse check for a team of at least 6 and decide the stage from the mean.

Standard: The mean is calculated correctly and a stage and next step are named using a stated cut-off.

Part 2 Communication styles across cultures 10 hAnalyse: compare

Some teams say things directly and some hint. Neither is wrong, but a mismatch causes missed messages.

  • Direct and indirect styles
  • High and low context messages
  • Confirm meaning with a short read-back

Worked example: A manager from a direct culture writes 'This report is late.' A team member from an indirect culture hears it as a mild note, because in that culture a stronger sentence would be 'We must finish this today'. Of 10 recent deadline messages, 4 were missed by indirect speakers, a 40% miss rate. After a read-back rule (repeat the task and date in your own words), misses fall to 1 of 10, or 10%.

Activity: Take 10 past messages. Mark which were missed, add a read-back rule and compare the miss rate.

Standard: Miss rates before and after are shown as percentages and the rule is written in one sentence.

Part 3 Building trust at a distance 9 hApply: design

Trust comes from skill (I believe you can do it) and from personal ties (I know you care). Remote teams must build both on purpose.

  • Show reliable delivery
  • Share some personal background
  • Track promises kept

Worked example: A remote team logs promises made in weekly calls. In 4 weeks there were 48 promises and 39 were kept on time. The kept rate is 39 / 48 = 81%. One pair of members kept 6 of 12, which is 50%, so the lead meets them to find the cause. The team goal is 90%, which means at most 4 late promises in 48.

Activity: Log promises for two weeks for a team and work out the kept rate for the team and for one pair.

Standard: Both rates are correct and one action follows from the lowest figure.

Part 4 Meetings across time zones 10 hApply: calculate

Time zones cut the hours when people can meet. A fair plan rotates the inconvenient slot.

  • Find the overlap of working hours
  • Rotate the early and late slots
  • Record decisions in writing

Worked example: Three sites work 9:00 to 17:00. Site A is at UTC+1, site B at UTC+5:30 and site C at UTC-5. In UTC, A works 8:00 to 16:00, B works 3:30 to 11:30, C works 14:00 to 22:00. A and B overlap 8:00 to 11:30, which is 3.5 hours. All three never overlap. With three sites, a call at 14:00 UTC forces B to join at 19:30 local, so the slot rotates every month.

Activity: Take three cities and work out the daily overlap for each pair and name a rotation plan.

Standard: UTC conversion is shown, overlap hours are correct for each pair and a rotation plan is stated.

Part 5 Group decision rules 9 hEvaluate: choose

A team must know how it decides before it decides. Different cultures expect different rules, such as leader decides, vote, or talk until agreed.

  • Name the rule before the meeting
  • Match the rule to the risk
  • Record dissent

Worked example: A team of 7 must choose a supplier. Under majority rule, 4 votes are enough. Under a two-thirds rule, 7 x 2 / 3 = 4.67, so 5 votes are needed. Votes come in at 4 for supplier X and 3 for supplier Y. X wins under majority but fails the two-thirds rule. Because the contract is worth 2 million, the team chose two-thirds and held a second round.

Activity: Pick a decision rule for a team of 9 and state how many votes are needed for a majority and for two-thirds.

Standard: Majority is 5, two-thirds is 6, and the rule is matched to the size of the risk in writing.

Part 6 Types of conflict and how to read them 10 hAnalyse: classify

Conflict about the task is useful. Conflict about people drains a team. A lead must sort which is which before acting.

  • Task, process and relationship conflict
  • Sort each issue in a list
  • Act first on relationship harm

Worked example: A team lists 10 issues in a retro. 5 are about the task (design choices), 3 are about process (who approves) and 2 are about relationships (tone in chat). That is 50%, 30% and 20%. The 2 relationship issues are handled first in private talks. The task issues stay on the agenda because debate on them improves the design.

Activity: Collect 10 issues from a real or case team, sort them and give the percentage in each type.

Standard: Every issue is placed in one type, the percentages add to 100% and one first action is named.

Part 7 Arbitration steps 9 hApply: carry out

Arbitration is a structured process where a neutral person helps two sides reach a fair outcome. Each step has a time limit.

  • Hear each side with no interruption
  • List interests, not just demands
  • Test trades and write the deal

Worked example: Member A wants 14 days to finish a task and member B, who depends on it, needs it in 7. The arbiter asks for interests. A needs time for a data check, and B needs a first draft to start. The deal is a draft at day 7 and the checked version at day 12. That is 2 days less than A asked for and 5 days more than B asked for. Both sign a one-line note.

Activity: Run a mock arbitration with two sides who have different deadlines and write the agreed dates.

Standard: Interests are stated for both sides, the dates fall within the range of the two demands and both agree in writing.

Part 8 Measuring team health 9 hEvaluate: judge

A team should be measured, not guessed. A short survey every month shows trends before a crisis.

  • Use the same 5 questions each month
  • Track the trend, not one score
  • Act when the mean drops by more than 0.5

Worked example: Over three months, the mean trust score for a team is 4.1, 3.8 and 3.2. The drop from month 2 to month 3 is 0.6, which is above the 0.5 limit. The team has 8 people and 3 rated themselves 2 or less, which is 37.5%. The lead calls a session and sets a goal to get back to a mean of 3.8 within 6 weeks.

Activity: Take three monthly means and decide whether action is needed using the 0.5 rule.

Standard: The change is calculated correctly, the rule is applied and a goal with a date is set.

ten checks

Check yourself

  1. Five members rate team agreement 3, 4, 2, 5 and 4. What is the mean?
    18 / 5 = 3.6. (Covered in part 1: How mixed teams form and stall. To pass that part: The mean is calculated correctly and a stage and next step are named using a stated cut-off.)
  2. Site A is at UTC+2 and site B at UTC-3. Both work 9:00 to 17:00. What is their overlap?
    A works 7:00 to 15:00 UTC, B works 12:00 to 20:00 UTC, so the overlap is 12:00 to 15:00, which is 3 hours. (Covered in part 4: Meetings across time zones. To pass that part: UTC conversion is shown, overlap hours are correct for each pair and a rotation plan is stated.)
  3. A team kept 27 of 30 promises. What is the kept rate?
    90%. (Covered in part 3: Building trust at a distance. To pass that part: Both rates are correct and one action follows from the lowest figure.)
  4. How many votes are a two-thirds majority in a team of 12?
    8. (Covered in part 5: Group decision rules. To pass that part: Majority is 5, two-thirds is 6, and the rule is matched to the size of the risk in writing.)
  5. Why use a read-back rule?
    Hearing the task repeated in the listener's own words shows whether the message was understood, even when the style is indirect. (Covered in part 2: Communication styles across cultures. To pass that part: Miss rates before and after are shown as percentages and the rule is written in one sentence.)
  6. Out of 20 issues, 6 are about relationships. What percentage is that?
    30%. (Covered in part 6: Types of conflict and how to read them. To pass that part: Every issue is placed in one type, the percentages add to 100% and one first action is named.)
  7. What does an arbiter ask for after hearing both demands?
    The interests behind each demand. (Covered in part 7: Arbitration steps. To pass that part: Interests are stated for both sides, the dates fall within the range of the two demands and both agree in writing.)
  8. The trust mean falls from 4.0 to 3.4. Is action needed under a 0.5 rule?
    Yes, because the drop is 0.6, which is above 0.5. (Covered in part 8: Measuring team health. To pass that part: The change is calculated correctly, the rule is applied and a goal with a date is set.)
  9. When a team is in friction after four weeks, what should the lead do first?
    Hold a rules session on how the team works before adding new tasks. (Covered in part 1: How mixed teams form and stall. To pass that part: The mean is calculated correctly and a stage and next step are named using a stated cut-off.)
  10. Why rotate the inconvenient meeting slot?
    So the same site does not always join at an unsocial hour, which keeps the load fair. (Covered in part 4: Meetings across time zones. To pass that part: UTC conversion is shown, overlap hours are correct for each pair and a rotation plan is stated.)

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Team stages. A simple line shows meeting, friction, rules and steady work with the typical weeks for each. Speaker note: Say: friction is normal. Check: ask where the team sits now. Mistake: reading friction as failure.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Basic: Styles of speaking. Two sample messages say the same thing in a direct and an indirect way. Speaker note: Say: neither is wrong. Check: ask what each message means. Mistake: judging the indirect speaker as unclear.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Core: Trust and promises. A log shows promises made and kept with a kept rate. Speaker note: Say: count what is kept. Check: work out 18 of 24. Mistake: measuring friendliness instead of delivery.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Core: Time zones. A strip shows three work days in UTC with the overlap shaded. Speaker note: Say: convert to one clock first. Check: find the overlap. Mistake: forgetting daylight saving shifts.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Applied: Decision rules. A table lists majority, two-thirds and consensus with votes needed for 7 and 9 members. Speaker note: Say: pick the rule by the risk. Check: ask the votes for two-thirds of 9. Mistake: choosing the rule after the vote.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Applied: Conflict sort and arbitration. A list of issues is sorted into three types, followed by the four steps of arbitration. Speaker note: Say: sort, then act on relationship harm. Check: sort five sample issues. Mistake: giving a verdict instead of finding interests.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Advanced: Team health over time. A chart shows three monthly means and the 0.5 action line. Speaker note: Say: act on the trend. Check: ask what to do if the mean drops 0.7. Mistake: waiting for a visible crisis.
