Grade 12 Humanities: History

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5Post-secondary4 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Understand, Analyse
  • Analyse Compare societies using sources.
  • Understand Explain colonialism and nationalism.
  • Analyse Analyse partition and modern movements.
  • Understand Interpret sources and write an evidence-based essay.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Humanities History, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Themes in ancient and medieval history Weeks 1 to 9Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Themes in ancient and medieval history', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: compare societies using sources. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Themes in ancient and medieval history' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: compare societies using sources. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Themes in ancient and medieval history'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Compare societies using sources, so list the kinds of source for each. For the Harappan cities, about 2600 to 1900 BCE, we have material remains such as bricks, drains, seals and weights, and a script of around 400 signs that nobody can yet read. For Mesopotamia we have thousands of readable cuneiform tablets.
  2. Step 2, Read the Harappan evidence. Cities such as Mohenjo-daro had streets on a grid, covered brick drains and baked bricks in a ratio of 1:2:4, with standard weights. Inference: the people shared standards and probably some planning. Note the limit: without readable writing, we cannot be sure who ruled.
  3. Step 3, Read the Mesopotamian evidence. Tablets record temple stores, trade in grain and copper, letters and written laws. Inference: scribes and officials ran a bureaucracy, and temples were central. Note the limit: scribes wrote for the rich and powerful, so ordinary farmers appear only indirectly.
  4. Step 4, Make a comparison table with the same rows for both: writing, buildings, trade and government. Writing: readable tablets against an undeciphered script. Trade: tablets mention Meluhha, often linked with the Indus region, and Harappan-style seals have turned up in Mesopotamia, which suggests contact by sea.
  5. Step 5, Weigh each source before you rely on it. Ask who made it, why, for whom and what it leaves out. Write cautious claims: 'the drains suggest planning', not 'the state planned everything'. A claim is stronger when two kinds of source support it, such as seals and tablets.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write a two-sentence comparison of what drains and tablets can each tell us. Model answer: Harappan drains suggest shared planning and care for health, but cannot show who ruled; Mesopotamian tablets name officials and laws, but mostly show the view of scribes.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Compare societies using sources: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Themes in modern history Weeks 10 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'Themes in modern history', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain colonialism and nationalism. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Themes in modern history' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain colonialism and nationalism. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Themes in modern history'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain colonialism using a timeline. A British trading company won at Plassey in 1757 and received the revenue rights of Bengal, the diwani, in 1765. After the revolt of 1857, the Crown took over in 1858. Independence came in 1947, which is 190 years after Plassey and 89 years after Crown rule began.
  2. Step 2, Explain the economic effect. India exported raw cotton, indigo and jute, and imported machine-made cloth, so hand-loom weavers lost work. Land revenue was fixed in money, which forced farmers to sell crops or borrow. Critics described the money sent abroad as a drain of wealth. Link each policy to who lost.
  3. Step 3, Explain how the colonial system produced its own critics. English education and the press spread ideas of liberty and law to a new middle class. Associations formed, and in 1885 an all-India political body was founded. Its early demands were petitions for a share in government, not independence.
  4. Step 4, Explain the growth of mass nationalism. In 1905 Bengal was divided, which brought the swadeshi boycott of foreign goods. Non-cooperation ran from 1920 to 1922. In 1930 a salt march of about 390 km lasted 24 days. In 1942 came the Quit India call. Each stage drew in more people.
  5. Step 5, Explain the colonial response and the link between cause and effect. The government used arrests and also reforms, such as the Acts of 1909, 1919 and 1935, which gave limited elected seats. Protesters saw the reforms as too small. Show the chain: policy, grievance, organisation, protest, response.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: explain how one colonial policy led to a nationalist response. Answer: the salt tax and the state monopoly on salt hurt the poor most, so in 1930 a march to the sea led people to make salt without paying tax, turning a household grievance into mass protest.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain colonialism and nationalism: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Themes in contemporary history Weeks 19 to 27Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Themes in contemporary history', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: analyse partition and modern movements. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Themes in contemporary history' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: analyse partition and modern movements. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Themes in contemporary history'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Analyse the partition's timeline. The plan to divide British India was announced on 3 June 1947. Two states became independent on 14 and 15 August 1947, and Punjab and Bengal were split. The boundary line was published on 17 August, after independence, so many people did not know which country they lived in.
  2. Step 2, Analyse the human scale carefully. Historians' estimates range from about 10 to 20 million people displaced, and from several hundred thousand to about a million deaths. Because the figures differ, give a range and name that it is an estimate. Explain that violence, fear and rumour drove many families to leave.
  3. Step 3, Analyse the kinds of source. Official records give numbers and decisions, but they leave out private experience. Oral histories record what families remember, but memory changes over time. Letters and photographs fill gaps. Write which source best answers 'what happened' and which best answers 'how did it feel'.
  4. Step 4, Analyse the aftermath. Refugees needed camps, homes and work. More than 560 princely states had to choose between the two countries or independence. India's Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. Link each event to the problem it tried to solve.
  5. Step 5, Analyse later movements with one pattern: cause, method, outcome. Linguistic movements led to the States Reorganisation Act of 1956. The Chipko protests of the 1970s used tree-hugging to stop felling in the Himalayan hills. Women's movements campaigned on law and rights. Compare how each movement used democratic means.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: analyse the Chipko protests by cause, method and outcome. Answer: cause, commercial felling that threatened village forests and water; method, villagers hugging trees in non-violent protest; outcome, public attention and restrictions on felling in parts of the Himalayas in the early 1980s.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Analyse partition and modern movements: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Source work and historiography Weeks 28 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Source work and historiography', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: interpret sources and write an evidence-based essay. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Source work and historiography' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: interpret sources and write an evidence-based essay. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Take one source or data set. Ask five questions: who made it, when, why, what does it show, what is missing? Write three sentences using one piece of evidence each, then say what other source you would check. Method: Model run for 'Source work and historiography'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Interpret a source by asking four questions: who made it, when, why and for whom. Take an imagined 1858 report by a British officer on the 1857 revolt. Author: an official of the ruling power. Date: one year after events. Purpose: to explain the revolt to superiors. Audience: government readers.
  2. Step 2, Separate content, inference and limits. Content: the report calls the events 'a mutiny of soldiers'. Inference: officials wished to present the revolt as a narrow military matter. Limit: it leaves out village and civilian participation and the view of Indians. Write all three, never only the content.
  3. Step 3, Cross-check with a second source. A later nationalist writer called 1857 the 'first war of independence'. Later research showed regional differences in who joined and why. Neither label is complete. Put the sources in a table, with columns for the claim, the evidence offered and what each cannot show.
  4. Step 4, Explain historiography, meaning how historians' interpretations change. Colonial writers stressed order and the ruler's view. Nationalist writers stressed resistance. Later writers studied peasants, tribes and workers, who left fewer documents. Each school asks different questions and uses different sources, so describe its question, not only its conclusion.
  5. Step 5, Write an evidence-based paragraph with PEEL: Point, Evidence, Explain, Link. Point: the 1857 revolt was more than a military mutiny. Evidence: civilians joined in many towns, and rebel leaders issued proclamations. Explain: this shows wide grievances. Link: so the official label 'mutiny' is too narrow.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write one PEEL paragraph answering 'Was 1857 only a military event?' Model answer: No. Civilians in several regions joined, which shows grievances beyond the army. Official reports called it a mutiny to limit its meaning, so those reports show the rulers' view, not the whole event.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Interpret sources and write an evidence-based essay: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: My own timeline

On the slide: L1 Basic · My own timeline

Notes: Put three events from your own life in order. Notes: ask learners to draw a baby picture, a first day at school and today, then order them on a string line and use the words before, after and now in a spoken sentence.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Then and now

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Then and now

Notes: Tell how a long-ago object differs from a modern one and use a simple timeline. Notes: compare an oil lamp with an electric bulb; place both on a line marked in 100-year steps and ask learners to say one way daily life changed and one way it stayed the same.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Early civilisations and evidence

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Early civilisations and evidence

Notes: Describe an early river-valley civilisation and say how we know about it. Notes: sort sources into written and object, primary and secondary. Practice: a clay tablet about 4,500 years old, counted back from 2026, dates to roughly 2500 BCE; learners say what it can and cannot tell us.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Cause, effect and change

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Cause, effect and change

Notes: Explain the causes and effects of one major change, such as the spread of printing. Notes: sort causes into short term and long term. Movable-type printing reached Europe around 1450, which is 576 years before 2026; learners list two effects on reading and learning.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Sources and balanced argument (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Sources and balanced argument

Notes: Evaluate two sources on one event for reliability and write a balanced paragraph. Notes: take the Second World War, 1939 to 1945, and compare a diary with an official report; check who wrote it, when and why, then give one point each way before judging.

This course: Grade 12 Humanities History is at this level. Its units include Themes in ancient and medieval history; Themes in modern history; Themes in contemporary history; Source work and historiography.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Historiography and archives

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Historiography and archives

Notes: Analyse competing interpretations and build an argument from primary evidence. Notes: compare two accounts of the causes of one reform or war and note what each leaves out. Practice: in a ledger of 1,200 entries, 15 per cent are damaged, so 180 are unusable; learners state what that does to the claim.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Original research design

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Original research design

Notes: Design an original research question, justify method and place the findings in the wider debate. Notes: write a 300-word proposal naming sources, gaps and bias; if a sample is 80 letters from a collection of 640, that is 12.5 per cent, and learners argue whether it is representative.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
