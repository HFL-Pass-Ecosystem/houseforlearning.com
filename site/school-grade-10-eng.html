Grade 10: English

6 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4Secondary6 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse

  • Analyse Compare two poems on theme and style.
  • Apply Edit and transform sentences accurately.
  • Apply Write from data and for formal audiences.
  • Remember Read critically and answer in full sentences.
  • Understand Speak to a prompt and respond to questions.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 10 English, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 6 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

6 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Prose and drama Weeks 1 to 7Analyse

In this unit, 'Prose and drama', you learn to analyse a longer text and write an essay with evidence. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Prose and drama' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: analyse a longer text and write an essay with evidence, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Main idea in one sentence. Character: what they say and do shows what they are like (she gave away her lunch: generous). Drama: names on the left, stage directions in brackets. Method: Model run for 'Prose and drama'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Choose a focus for a longer text. Imagine a novel in which a young shopkeeper, Nadia, refuses to sell her late grandfather's watch. Essay question: 'How does the writer present Nadia's loyalty?' Underline the key words, here 'how', 'writer' and 'loyalty', and decide on a clear argument.
  2. Step 2, Write a thesis, your main claim in one sentence. 'The writer presents Nadia's loyalty as costly but admirable, using her refusal of a generous offer to show that memory matters more to her than money.' A good thesis answers the question and names a method.
  3. Step 3, Collect evidence from three points in the text: the offer of 5,000 rupees, which she refuses; her habit of winding the watch every morning; and her final decision to keep it despite debts. Note the quotation or event for each and the technique, such as contrast, repetition or symbolism.
  4. Step 4, Build a PEEL paragraph: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link. 'Nadia's loyalty is shown through routine. She winds the watch each morning, which suggests it is a living link to her grandfather. This makes her refusal later feel natural and links to the idea that memory has value.'
  5. Step 5, Organise the essay: an introduction with the thesis, three body paragraphs in order (offer, routine, decision), then a conclusion that returns to the question. Use linking phrases: 'Furthermore', 'In contrast', 'Ultimately'. Keep quotations short and always explain what they show.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write a topic sentence for the paragraph on the final decision. Model answer: 'Nadia's decision to keep the watch despite her debts shows that her loyalty is not a passing feeling but a lasting principle that shapes her choices.'

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Analyse a longer text and write an essay with evidence: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 2 Poetry Weeks 8 to 14Analyse

In this unit, 'Poetry', you learn to compare two poems on theme and style. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Poetry' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: compare two poems on theme and style, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Simile: 'The moon is like a silver coin.' Metaphor: 'The classroom was a zoo.' Imagery: 'the dew-wet grass' appeals to sight and touch. Say what feeling each creates. Method: Model run for 'Poetry'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read two short poems. Poem A: 'My mother's kitchen smells of cloves, / of steam and old brass pans. / I carry it in every town, / a map made without lands.' Poem B: 'I left the house with a single key. / The key still hangs on my chain. / It opens nothing now, / yet it opens everything again.'
  2. Step 2, Find the shared theme. Both poems are about carrying a home inside memory after leaving it. A uses smell; B uses an object, a key. Write a theme sentence that covers both: 'Both poets suggest that home continues to live in the senses and in small things after we leave.'
  3. Step 3, Compare style. Poem A uses the senses (smell of cloves, steam) and a metaphor 'a map made without lands'. Poem B uses a symbol, the key, and a paradox, a key that opens 'nothing' yet 'everything'. A feels warm and sensory; B feels reflective and bittersweet.
  4. Step 4, Compare structure. Poem A has four lines with an ABCB rhyme (pans, lands) and moves from place to travel. Poem B has four lines of uneven length, ending on the striking reversal in the last line. The shorter line 'It opens nothing now' slows the poem before the surprise.
  5. Step 5, Write a comparison using connectives. 'Both poems show home surviving in memory. However, while A relies on the sense of smell, B uses the symbol of a key. Whereas A ends with a calm metaphor, B ends with a paradox that shows sadness and comfort together.' Quote briefly in a full essay.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write one sentence that compares the endings. Model answer: A closes on a gentle metaphor that suggests home is portable, whereas B closes on a paradox that suggests the key is useless yet priceless, so B feels more bittersweet.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Compare two poems on theme and style: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 3 Grammar: tenses, reported speech and editing Weeks 15 to 21Apply

In this unit, 'Grammar: tenses, reported speech and editing', you learn to edit and transform sentences accurately. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Grammar: tenses, reported speech and editing' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: edit and transform sentences accurately, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Formal letter: 12 November 2026; The Principal; Subject: request for a library card; 'Respected Sir, I am a student of Class 8...'; Yours faithfully, Aman. Informal: Dear Ria, ... Your friend, Aman. Report: title, date, place, what happened, result, writer's name. Method: Model run for 'Grammar: tenses, reported speech and editing'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Transform a statement into reported speech. Direct: 'I am tired,' she said. Reported: She said that she was tired. The rule is a backshift: present simple becomes past simple, and the speech marks and first-person pronoun change. Practice: 'We will visit the museum tomorrow' becomes 'They said that they would visit the museum the next day.'
  2. Step 2, Report questions and commands. 'Where do you live?' he asked me becomes 'He asked me where I lived.' The question word stays, and the word order becomes a statement. 'Did you finish the report?' becomes 'She asked whether I had finished the report.' 'Please close the door' becomes 'He asked me to close the door.'
  3. Step 3, Control tenses in editing. Spot the error: 'I am living here since 2019.' Correction: 'I have been living here since 2019.' Since and for need the present perfect. Another: 'He said that he has lost his pen' becomes 'he had lost his pen' after the reporting verb 'said'.
  4. Step 4, Edit for subject-verb agreement. 'The list of items are long.' The subject is 'list', not 'items', so write 'The list of items is long.' 'Each of the students have a book' becomes 'has'. 'Neither the teacher nor the pupils was ready' becomes 'were', matching the nearer subject.
  5. Step 5, Transform sentences without changing meaning. Active to passive: 'The committee approved the plan' becomes 'The plan was approved by the committee.' Combining: 'He was tired. He kept working.' becomes 'Although he was tired, he kept working.' Check that each result has the same meaning and correct grammar.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: report 'I have finished my homework,' said Hira. Answer: Hira said that she had finished her homework. The present perfect 'have finished' backshifts to the past perfect 'had finished', and 'I' and 'my' change to 'she' and 'her'.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Edit and transform sentences accurately: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 4 Writing: data interpretation, letters and articles Weeks 22 to 27Apply

In this unit, 'Writing: data interpretation, letters and articles', you learn to write from data and for formal audiences. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Writing: data interpretation, letters and articles' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: write from data and for formal audiences, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Formal letter: 12 November 2026; The Principal; Subject: request for a library card; 'Respected Sir, I am a student of Class 8...'; Yours faithfully, Aman. Informal: Dear Ria, ... Your friend, Aman. Report: title, date, place, what happened, result, writer's name. Method: Model run for 'Writing: data interpretation, letters and articles'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read the data. A school library records books borrowed: Term 1 had 420, Term 2 had 483 and Term 3 had 462. The task asks you to write from data, so you must report numbers accurately and explain trends in formal language. Look at the highest, the lowest and the overall pattern before writing.
  2. Step 2, Calculate the changes. From Term 1 to 2: 483 minus 420 is 63, and 63 divided by 420 is 15 percent, an increase. From Term 2 to 3: 462 minus 483 is minus 21, and 21 divided by 483 is about 4.3 percent, a decrease. Total is 1,365, an average of 455.
  3. Step 3, Write the data paragraph. 'Borrowing rose by 15 percent from 420 books in Term 1 to 483 in Term 2. It then fell slightly, by about 4 percent, to 462 in Term 3. Overall, the library lent 1,365 books in the year.' Use trend verbs: rose, fell, remained steady.
  4. Step 4, Write a formal letter using the data. Address it to the principal: 'Sir, I am writing to suggest that the library stay open for an extra hour each afternoon, as borrowing has increased significantly this year.' State the problem, give evidence, then request action politely and sign off.
  5. Step 5, Write an article from the same data. Headline: 'Library Borrowing Soars, then Settles'. Opening: 'Students borrowed 1,365 books this year, a clear sign that reading is thriving.' Add a closing comment such as 'If this trend continues, the school may need more shelves.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: Term 4 borrowing was 507. Describe the change from Term 3. Answer: 507 minus 462 is 45, and 45 divided by 462 is about 9.7 percent. 'Borrowing rose by 45 books, or nearly 10 percent, from 462 to 507.'

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Write from data and for formal audiences: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 5 Reading: unseen passages Weeks 28 to 33Remember

In this unit, 'Reading: unseen passages', you learn to read critically and answer in full sentences. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Reading: unseen passages' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: read critically and answer in full sentences, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Passage: 'The old banyan tree gave shade to the whole village.' Q: what does 'shade' suggest? Protection from the sun. Inference: the tree matters to the villagers. Answer with a quote as evidence. Method: Model run for 'Reading: unseen passages'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read critically. Passage: 'The new rule banned phones from school. Principal Rao said it would improve attention. Many students agreed, though a few complained that they could no longer call home after class.' Ask three questions: who says it, what evidence is given and whose view is missing?
  2. Step 2, Separate fact from opinion. Fact: the school banned phones. Opinion: the ban 'would improve attention', which is the principal's belief, not proven. A claim presented as a fact deserves a check. Mark each sentence F or O and note the word that signals each, such as 'said' or 'would'.
  3. Step 3, Answer a direct question in a full sentence. Question: 'Why did some students complain?' Weak answer: 'Because of calling.' Better: 'Some students complained because they could no longer call home after class.' Turn the question into the start of your answer and complete it with the text's facts.
  4. Step 4, Answer a reasoning question. 'Do you think the writer supports the ban?' Look at word choice: 'Many students agreed' and 'a few complained' make the supporters sound larger. Answer: 'The writer seems mostly supportive, because the opposition is described as only "a few" students.'
  5. Step 5, Answer a vocabulary question. 'What does "banned" mean?' It means officially forbidden. Substitute your word to test it: 'The new rule forbade phones from school' still makes sense. For meaning from context, always check that your replacement fits the sentence's grammar and idea.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write a full-sentence answer to 'What did the principal expect from the new rule?' Model answer: The principal expected that banning phones would improve students' attention in school. It is a prediction, which the passage presents as the principal's opinion.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Read critically and answer in full sentences: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 6 Speaking and listening assessment Weeks 34 to 36Understand

In this unit, 'Speaking and listening assessment', you learn to speak to a prompt and respond to questions. You work with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. You see it, say it, then do it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Speaking and listening assessment' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: speak to a prompt and respond to questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Retell in order with first, next, then, last. Ask: 'What happened next?' Reply politely: 'Thank you, I agree because...' Method: Model run for 'Speaking and listening assessment'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Analyse the prompt. Prompt: "Describe a technology that has changed daily life, and explain whether the change is positive." Key words: describe, explain, whether positive. Reason: you must do all three tasks.
  2. Step 2, Plan with a quick frame. Introduction: name the technology. Body: two effects with examples. Judgement: positive or mixed. Close: summary. Reason: a frame stops you from rambling.
  3. Step 3, Start with a strong opening. "One technology that has changed daily life is the smartphone; it connects us to information, people and services in seconds." Reason: a direct opening answers the prompt immediately.
  4. Step 4, Develop with examples and reasons. "It saves time; for example, I can pay bills without going to the bank. However, it can also reduce face-to-face conversation." Reason: balance shows critical thinking.
  5. Step 5, Respond to a follow-up question. Examiner: "Do you think people rely on phones too much?" You: "In many cases, yes. For instance, people use maps rather than learn routes, though this saves time." Reason: give a position, an example and a limit.
  6. Step 6, Fix an error. Weak: "Phones are good. They are good because they are good." Better: "Phones are useful because they give instant access to information." Another error: very short answers. Extend with a reason or example.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Answer in two sentences: "Is online learning as effective as classroom learning?" Model answer: "It can be effective for self-disciplined students because it is flexible. However, classrooms offer discussion and support, so I think a mix is best."

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Speak to a prompt and respond to questions: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Letters, sounds and first reading

On the slide: L1 Basic · Letters, sounds and first reading

Notes: Match letters to sounds and read short words and simple sentences aloud. Notes: use the sounds s, a, t, p, i, n; learners blend them into sat, pin and tap, then read a three-word sentence and clap the syllables.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Sentences and simple writing

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Sentences and simple writing

Notes: Write a clear sentence with a capital letter and full stop, and read a short story with understanding. Notes: give a picture prompt; learners say the sentence first, write it, and check capital, spacing and full stop with a partner before reading it aloud.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

Notes: Write a paragraph of five connected sentences and identify nouns, verbs and adjectives in a text. Notes: colour nouns blue and verbs red in a short passage; learners then write about their street, using at least two adjectives and one linking word.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Essays, speaking and responding to texts (this course)

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Essays, speaking and responding to texts

Notes: Write a 300-word structured essay and give a short talk, quoting evidence from a text to support a view. Notes: model Point, Evidence, Explain; learners write one paragraph about a character, quote one line, and explain what it shows, then speak it aloud.

This course: Grade 10 English is at this level. Its units include Prose and drama; Poetry; Grammar: tenses, reported speech and editing; Writing: data interpretation, letters and articles and more.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Analysis, argument and style

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Analysis, argument and style

Notes: Analyse a writer's choices of language and structure and write a sustained argument of 600 words. Notes: take two openings of different short stories; learners compare tone, sentence length and imagery in a table, then argue which opening works better and why.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Literary and linguistic study

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Literary and linguistic study

Notes: Evaluate texts through a critical lens, tracing how language changes across time and context in a researched essay. Notes: compare an older and a modern passage; learners list five differences in vocabulary and syntax and argue in 1,500 words what each reveals about its period.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research, editing and authorship

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research, editing and authorship

Notes: Produce an original piece of research or a polished long-form text, with a literature review and defended method. Notes: pick a narrow question about language in one genre; build a corpus of fifty samples, analyse patterns, write 5,000 words, and revise after external review.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore
