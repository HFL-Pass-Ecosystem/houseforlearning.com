The energy journey

150 years of light.

Everything in the modern world runs on energy. When it stops, hospitals, classrooms, data centres, kitchens, metros and cars stop with it. This page shows where we came from, where we stand, and where the work is still to be done.

Where we came from

From one lamp to a planet on a grid

People watched amber and lightning for thousands of years before anyone harnessed them. Then the first lamps lit single streets. Within one lifetime, wires carried power across countries. Here are the turning points.

About 600 BCE

Amber and the first spark

The Greek thinker Thales of Miletus is reported to have noticed that rubbed amber attracts feathers and dust. This is the oldest recorded observation of static electricity.

1600

The word “electric” is born

William Gilbert, an English physician, publishes De Magnete, separates magnetism from static electricity and coins the Latin electricus, from the Greek word for amber, elektron.

1752

Lightning is electricity

Benjamin Franklin's lightning experiments show that a thunderstorm is an electrical discharge, which leads to the lightning rod.

1800

The first battery

Alessandro Volta builds the voltaic pile, the first device to give a steady, continuous current.

1831

Motion makes electricity

Michael Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction. Every generator and transformer since is built on it.

1879

The light bulb becomes practical

Thomas Edison and his team make a long-lasting incandescent lamp that ordinary people can use. Others built early lamps first; this one was built to be used and sold.

1882

The first public power stations

Central stations in New York and London sell electricity to streets and homes. The power is direct current, which cannot travel far.

1888

Alternating current: Nikola Tesla's motor and power system

Tesla's patents for polyphase AC motors and systems show that power can be sent far at high voltage and then stepped down for use. This is the idea behind every grid today.

1891

175 km of three-phase power

A demonstration line in Germany carries AC power about 175 km. Long-distance transmission is proven.

1895

Hydropower at Niagara

A large AC hydro plant sends power to a city 40 km away. Mills, trams and homes switch to the grid.

1900s–1930s

Steam turbines, grids and rural wires

Turbines make power cheap. Countries build national networks and, from the 1930s, push wires to farms and villages.

1954

Nuclear power joins the grid; the silicon solar cell is born

The first grid-connected nuclear plant starts in the USSR. The same year, researchers make the first practical silicon solar cell.

1970s

Energy security and efficiency

Oil shocks teach the world that energy supply is also national and economic security. Efficiency standards begin.

1991

The lithium-ion battery reaches the market

Rechargeable power fits in a pocket, and later in a car and a grid-scale store.

2015

The Paris Agreement

Nearly every country agrees to cut emissions. Wind, solar and storage become the fastest-growing sources.

2020s

Everything electrifies

Electric vehicles, heat pumps, data centres and artificial intelligence pull more and more of life onto the grid at the same time.

Dates are widely documented in the history of engineering. See the sources at the end.

Why alternating current won

Send it far, then step it down

Direct current flows one way and loses much of its energy over distance at low voltage. Alternating current swings back and forth, and a transformer can raise its voltage for the long journey and lower it again at the door. That single idea let one power station serve a whole region, and later a whole continent.

The lesson for every learner: the grid is a system. Generation, wires, protection, control and people all have to work together, every second.

Older roots

Fire, lightning and an old question about energy

Long before wires, people in India asked what energy is. Some of what follows is documented. Some is tradition. We label each, so you can trust what you read.

Documented

Tesla meets Swami Vivekananda, 1896

In February 1896 in New York, the inventor Nikola Tesla met Swami Vivekananda, who was bringing Vedanta to the West. Vivekananda wrote that Tesla believed he could show mathematically that force and matter are forms of potential energy, and that Tesla was drawn to the Vedic ideas of akasha (space, the all-pervading) and prana (vital energy). Vivekananda planned to return for the demonstration. No record of it survives.

Documented text, influence debated

Tesla's own words, 1907

In an essay dated 1907, Man's Greatest Achievement, Tesla uses the Sanskrit words Akasha and Prana for matter's primary substance and the force that shapes it. It was not published in his lifetime. Whether it came from Vivekananda is suggested by later writers, not proved.

Tradition

Agni and Vidyut in the Vedas

The Rigveda honours Agni, fire, as the energy behind light and life. Later tradition describes fire in three realms: on earth, in the sun, and in the sky as vidyut, lightning. Calling vidyut “electricity” is a modern reading of an old word; the hymns speak of lightning and of illumination.

Tradition, disputed

The Agastya Samhita “battery”

A text attributed to the sage Agastya describes a copper plate, copper sulphate, damp sawdust and zinc in a clay vessel. Modern attempts to follow it report about 1.1 volts, which is what that chemistry gives (the same family as the 19th-century Daniell cell). The age and authorship of the text are disputed, and whether it was ever used as a battery is not shown.

Why this matters: curiosity about energy is old and shared across peoples. We honour it without claiming more than the evidence shows.

In remembrance

Once upon a time, they imagined it

Thales watched amber. Gilbert named it. Franklin read the sky. Volta stored it. Faraday made it move. Edison lit a room, Tesla sent it across a country. Before them, seers and sages asked what energy is.

None of them saw the data centre, the metro or the electric car. They imagined a world that would be lit, and they gave their lives to it. We live inside what they dreamed.

What we build today will light someone's classroom a hundred years from now. We owe them the same care that those before us gave to us.

“Man is here for the sake of other men.”

“… I must exert myself in order to give in the same measure as I have received and am still receiving.”

Albert Einstein, “The World As I See It” (essay of 1930, in the 1934 collection; wording differs a little between English translations).

Our call to every professional: bring respect, responsibility and determination. Respect for the people and the generations behind the switch. Responsibility for safety, quality and honest work. Determination to finish what we start. Einstein asks us to give as we have received. We ask each other to try to give more.

What we learned, what we did, what is left

Three questions for any journey

What we learned

  • Power travels furthest as high-voltage AC; systems beat single machines.
  • Safety is built in, never added later. Every rule was written after an accident.
  • Reliable energy lifts health, learning and income. Access changes lives.
  • Cheap energy has costs we must count, including air, climate and security.
  • People are the limit. Machines can be built faster than skilled workers can be trained.

The work we have done

  • Built grids that reach about 9 in 10 people.
  • Made solar, wind and batteries cheap enough to compete everywhere.
  • Turned electricity into the carrier for transport, heat and computing.
  • Written standards so equipment from different makers works together safely.
  • Trained generations of electricians, technicians and engineers, one apprenticeship at a time.

The work yet to come

  • Bring power to the people still without it.
  • Rebuild and strengthen grids for much higher demand.
  • Store energy for days, not only hours.
  • Protect energy systems from cyber attacks.
  • Train and certify tens of millions more skilled people, in every country.

The next 150 years

Three horizons. One direction.

Nobody can predict 150 years. These are pathways to plan for and to argue about, not forecasts. What does not change: more of life runs on electricity, and it needs people who know how.

Now to about 2050

Electrify and clean up

Cars, buses, heating and industry move to electricity. Solar, wind and storage lead new supply. Grids are upgraded and made digital. Demand from data centres and AI keeps rising.

Work needed: installers, grid engineers, protection and control specialists, battery and charging teams, cybersecurity.

About 2050 to 2100

A flexible, stored, shared grid

Energy is generated close to where it is used and shared between neighbours, cities and countries. Long-duration storage, clean hydrogen and next-generation nuclear take a larger part. Machines help operate the grid.

Work needed: system operators, storage and hydrogen engineers, data and AI specialists, recyclers, regulators.

About 2100 to 2175

Abundant, clean, invisible

If new sources such as fusion, space-based power and far better storage mature, energy could become plentiful and clean enough that people think about it as little as they do about tap water today. Maintenance of this system would still need skilled hands.

And if humanity grows into an interstellar species, the demand for people who understand energy grows with it: every ship, station and settlement will run on it.

Work needed: skills we cannot name yet, built on the habit of learning.
