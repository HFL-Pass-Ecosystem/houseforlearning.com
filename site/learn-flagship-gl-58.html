Electric Car and EV Basics

Understand how electric vehicles work, charge, cost and stay safe, and how to advise buyers and fleets honestly.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L3 Foundation27:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain motor, battery, charger and range.
  • Apply Estimate running cost and charging time.
  • Analyse Compare EV and petrol costs.
  • Evaluate Judge battery health, safety and resale.
  • Create Write a buyer or fleet advice sheet.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Produce an advice sheet comparing one EV with one petrol car for a given user, with cost, range, charging and safety notes.

Condition

A computer, public specifications, a spreadsheet, two peers and a mentor. No high-voltage work is done.

Standard

Maths is correct and in your currency; assumptions are stated; safety rules for high-voltage parts are included; claims are sourced; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Sales staff, drivers, fleet clerks, technician trainees. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 How an electric vehicle works 3 hUnderstand

An electric car swaps the engine, fuel tank and gearbox for a battery, a motor and a controller. Knowing the chain lets you explain every cost and limit later.

  • Energy flows battery to controller to motor to wheels; braking sends some energy back to the battery.
  • A motor gives full pull from a standstill, so the car feels quick and quiet with one gear.
  • A small battery runs lights, screens and cooling; the traction battery drives the wheels.
  • Fewer moving parts means fewer routine services, but tyres, brakes and suspension still wear.
Worked example. A 60 kWh car uses about 15 kWh per 100 km. The motor turns about 90 per cent of battery energy into motion; a petrol engine turns about 30 per cent of fuel energy into motion.

Activity. Draw the energy chain for one named EV type and mark the loss at each step.

Standard to meet. Complete the chain in order, show losses as percentages, and explain regenerative braking in one sentence.

Module 2 Batteries 3 hUnderstand

The battery is the costliest and most sensitive part. A buyer must read its size, health and warranty.

  • Capacity is in kWh; usable capacity is lower than the headline number.
  • Heat, deep discharge and constant fast charging age the battery faster.
  • Warranty usually states years or kilometres and a minimum health level.
  • Cold weather cuts range temporarily; hot weather speeds ageing.
Worked example. A battery with 60 kWh capacity and 90 per cent health holds 54 kWh. A warranty to 70 per cent health after 8 years is a better promise than a warranty with no health level.

Activity. Read a sample warranty and list capacity, health level, years and kilometre limit.

Standard to meet. Extract all four facts correctly and write one question for the seller.

Module 3 Charging levels and time 4 hApply

Time to charge depends on power. Home, work and route charging suit different users.

  • Time in hours = energy needed / charging power, with about 10 per cent loss.
  • Slow home points give 2 to 7 kW; fast route chargers give 50 kW and more.
  • Cars limit their own intake as the battery fills, so the last 20 per cent is slow.
  • Match the charging place to where the car sits longest.
Worked example. Adding 30 kWh at 7 kW takes 30 / 7 x 1.1 = 4.7 hours, so overnight home charging fits. At 50 kW the same energy takes about 40 minutes.

Activity. Calculate charging time for one user at home and at a route charger.

Standard to meet. Show both times with the formula and the 10 per cent loss; choose the right place and give a reason.

Module 4 Running cost comparison 4 hAnalyse

Compare fairly: energy, servicing, insurance, tax and the price difference.

  • Cost per km = price per unit of energy x energy per km.
  • Add servicing, insurance, tax and parking for both cars.
  • Include purchase price difference and how many years the user will keep the car.
  • State clearly what is a measured number and what is an assumption.
Worked example. A user drives 12,000 km a year (prices are estimates; check your market). Electric: 15 kWh per 100 km at 8 INR per kWh costs 1.2 INR per km, or 14,400 INR a year. Petrol: 6 litres per 100 km at 100 INR per litre costs 6 INR per km, or 72,000 INR a year. The saving is 57,600 INR a year.

Activity. Build a five-year cost table for one EV and one petrol car.

Standard to meet. Add up the totals, list the assumptions, and state the break-even year.

Module 5 Real-world range 3 hEvaluate

Label range is measured in set conditions. Real range moves with speed, load, weather and use of air conditioning.

  • Highway speed and cold weather cut range most.
  • Heavy loads and hills use more energy; downhill braking gives some back.
  • Plan with a margin: use about 80 per cent of label range.
  • Check route chargers before long trips.
Worked example. A car with 400 km label range gives about 320 km usable. In winter on a highway it may give 250 km. A 280 km trip needs a charging stop.

Activity. Plan one trip and decide if a stop is needed.

Standard to meet. Apply the margin, consider the weather, and name in the stop plan where and how long.

Module 6 Safety 4 hApply

High voltage needs respect, from driver to emergency crew.

  • Orange cables usually mark high voltage; only trained people work on them.
  • Cars have isolation systems that disconnect the battery in a crash.
  • Never charge with damaged cables or in standing water.
  • Fire services need clear information on the car type and battery position.
Worked example. A flooded car is not touched or charged. It is reported, isolated outdoors and checked by a trained technician before use.

Activity. Write a ten-line safety card for a new owner.

Standard to meet. Cover charging, damage, flooding and what to tell responders on the card, in plain words.

Module 7 Battery end of life 3 hEvaluate

A battery is still valuable when it can no longer power a car.

  • Below about 70 to 80 per cent health a car battery may serve as home or shop storage.
  • Recycling recovers metals; collect through approved centres only.
  • Ask the maker or seller about take-back.
  • Never dismantle at home.
Worked example. A battery at 72 per cent health still stores 43 kWh from a 60 kWh pack. It can back up a small shop for years before recycling.

Activity. Write a three-step plan for a battery at the end of its car life.

Standard to meet. Plan names reuse, recycling route and who is responsible.

Module 8 Write the advice sheet 3 hCreate

Bring the work together as one honest sheet for a named user.

  • Start with the user: distance, budget, parking, charging access.
  • Show the cost table, range plan and charging plan.
  • State one risk and one limit in plain words.
  • End with a clear recommendation and what would change it.
Worked example. For a user with home parking, 30 km daily trips and one 300 km trip a month, the sheet recommends the EV and a hire car or a charging stop for the long trip.

Activity. Produce the full one-page advice sheet.

Standard to meet. Include all parts, keep numbers consistent, and check that a reader can act on it without help.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: comparison sheet, charging time sheet, safety notes, advice sheet. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

CalculationsErrorsCorrectCorrect with sensitivity
SafetyMissingListedListed and approved
Honest adviceOne-sidedBalancedBalanced and tailored

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What does regen braking do?

Model answer: Returns some energy to the battery. (Covered in module 1: How an electric vehicle works. To pass that module: Complete the chain in order, show losses as percentages, and explain regenerative braking in one sentence.)

Q2. Charging time formula?

Model answer: Energy / power plus losses. (Covered in module 3: Charging levels and time. To pass that module: Show both times with the formula and the 10 per cent loss; choose the right place and give a reason.)

Q3. Name a battery ageing factor.

Model answer: Heat or frequent fast charging. (Covered in module 2: Batteries. To pass that module: Extract all four facts correctly and write one question for the seller.)

Q4. Who works on orange cables?

Model answer: Trained technicians. (Covered in module 6: Safety. To pass that module: Cover charging, damage, flooding and what to tell responders on the card, in plain words.)

Q5. What affects range?

Model answer: Speed, temperature, load, hills. (Covered in module 5: Real-world range. To pass that module: Apply the margin, consider the weather, and name in the stop plan where and how long.)

Q6. EV running cost steps?

Model answer: kWh per 100 km x price per kWh. (Covered in module 4: Running cost comparison. To pass that module: Add up the totals, list the assumptions, and state the break-even year.)

Q7. Name an end-of-life option.

Model answer: Second life or recycling. (Covered in module 7: Battery end of life. To pass that module: Plan names reuse, recycling route and who is responsible.)

Q8. Why keep a reserve?

Model answer: Range varies. (Covered in module 5: Real-world range. To pass that module: Apply the margin, consider the weather, and name in the stop plan where and how long.)

Q9. What costs besides fuel?

Model answer: Service, insurance, tax, depreciation. (Covered in module 4: Running cost comparison. To pass that module: Add up the totals, list the assumptions, and state the break-even year.)

Q10. What is usable capacity?

Model answer: Less than the label capacity. (Covered in module 2: Batteries. To pass that module: Extract all four facts correctly and write one question for the seller.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Standards, subsidies and tax differ by place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Global

The global version uses plain English, metric units, and your own currency. Machine translation is available in every language; the English text is the official text. Before you act for real, check your local rules with an adviser. Currency: USD (or your own currency, written next to every number).
