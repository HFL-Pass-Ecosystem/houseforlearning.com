Accommodation And Food Services

Run a real accommodation And Food Services pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small accommodation And Food Services offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: food, farming and hospitality. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know the places and words of food and lodging 6 hUnderstand

This field covers places where people eat, drink and stay: restaurants, cafes, caterers, hotels and short stays. Learn the roles and words.

  • Terms: front of house, back of house, covers, occupancy, menu, shift, allergen.
  • Front of house serves guests. Back of house cooks and cleans.
  • Hotels measure how many of their rooms are used each night; this is the occupancy percent.
  • Spend a shift observing and write down who does what.
  • Learn the shift pattern: opening, lunch, dinner and closing, and what each role does in each part.
  • Count covers (guests served) for one hour and write the busiest and quietest times.
  • Learn the basic room words for lodging: single, double, suite, check-in, check-out, housekeeping.
Worked example. A 20-room inn with 15 rooms used has 15 / 20 = 75 percent occupancy. If 18 rooms are used the next night, occupancy is 18 / 20 = 90 percent. The change is 15 points, which shows how much one night can vary.

Activity. Observe a busy hour at a cafe or hotel desk and note each role, task and waiting time.

Standard to meet. Task: write an observation note. Condition: one visit with permission. Standard: at least six roles listed, with tasks and one place guests waited.

Module 2 Explain the guest journey from first contact to goodbye 6 hApply

Every guest passes through steps. If one step is poor, the whole visit feels poor.

  • A restaurant guest moves through these steps: find, book, arrive, order, eat, pay, leave and return.
  • A lodging guest moves through these steps: find, book, arrive, stay, depart and review.
  • Write down what the guest sees and what staff must do at each step.
  • Pick one step to improve first, the one with the most complaints.
  • Time each step of one guest visit with a clock and write the longest wait.
  • Ask three guests what they liked and what they would change, and record the answers.
  • Write the words staff say at greeting and goodbye so every guest hears the same welcome.
Worked example. Guests wait 20 minutes for the bill. You test a card machine at the table and the wait falls to 8 minutes. That saves 20 - 8 = 12 minutes per table, so 10 tables in an evening gain 120 minutes of table time.

Activity. Draw the guest journey for one place and mark three trouble spots.

Standard to meet. Task: draw a guest journey chart. Condition: from real observation or ten guest comments. Standard: every step has guest view and staff task; three trouble spots have a proposed fix.

Module 3 Keep food and guests safe 10 hApply

Safety is the first duty. Learn the basic food and guest safety routines.

  • Wash hands often. Keep raw and cooked foods apart. Cook, cool and store at safe temperatures.
  • Many provinces require trained food handlers and public health inspection. Check the current rule with your local health authority.
  • Know the main allergens and keep a clear allergen list for every dish.
  • Keep fire exits clear, floors dry and first aid supplies ready.
  • Check fridge and freezer temperatures with a thermometer at set times and record them; follow the local health rule for safe limits.
  • Label and date all prepared food and use the oldest first.
  • Train every new worker on the allergen list and the cleaning steps before their first service.
Worked example. A guest says they cannot eat peanuts. The server checks the written allergen list, tells the cook, and the cook uses clean tools and surfaces. The server confirms the dish with the guest on delivery and writes the request on the order slip.

Activity. Write a one-page safety checklist for opening, service and closing, and use it for a week.

Standard to meet. Task: complete a daily safety checklist for a week. Condition: in a real or training kitchen or front desk. Standard: every item signed daily; any problem recorded with a fix.

Module 4 Know permits, licences and staff rules 8 hEvaluate

Rules differ across provinces and cities. Find what you need before you open or hire.

  • Typical needs: business registration, food premises approval, signage and zoning approval, fire inspection.
  • Selling alcohol needs a provincial licence and trained staff; rules differ by province. Check with the provincial liquor regulator.
  • Short stays may need a municipal licence and have limits on where they can run. Check the city rules.
  • Employment standards cover minimum wage, breaks, overtime, tips and holiday pay. Check the current provincial rules.
  • Ask the city office what is needed to open a dining room or a short stay, and write the steps in order.
  • Check the rule on posted hours, signage, accessibility and washrooms for your type of venue.
  • Write which permit takes the longest and start that one first.
Worked example. Rules table row: 'Seating over a set number | municipal fire office | occupancy limit posted | before opening | inspection report kept'. If the inspection takes 4 weeks and a hiring plan takes 3 weeks, both can run together, so the earliest opening is week 4, not week 7.

Activity. Build a rules table for one venue type in your city with sources and dates.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table. Condition: from official pages or a call to each office. Standard: each row has enforcer, action, deadline and a dated source; unknowns are marked as to be confirmed.

Module 5 Count food cost, labour and price a menu 8 hApply

Margins are thin. Count every cost before you set a price.

  • Food cost percent = ingredient cost per portion divided by selling price. Many venues aim between 25 and 35 percent.
  • Labour is often the largest cost. Plan staff to match busy and quiet times.
  • Lodging: average daily rate times occupancy gives income per room available.
  • Include fees for payment cards, delivery apps and tax in your price check.
  • Work out the cost of each ingredient per portion from the purchase price and weight.
  • Compute break-even in portions: fixed cost divided by margin per portion.
  • Set the staff plan from the busy hours so that you do not pay for idle shifts.
Worked example. A dish costs 3.10 CAD to make and sells for 12.00. Food cost is 3.10 / 12.00, about 26 percent, and margin is 12.00 - 3.10 = 8.90 per portion. At 900 portions a month the margin is 900 x 8.90 = 8,010 CAD before labour and rent. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Cost five menu items or three room types and compute margins.

Standard to meet. Task: cost a menu or room list. Condition: current supplier prices. Standard: units and currency shown, formulas visible, three what-if cases such as a 10 percent food price rise.

Module 6 Run a service shift well 6 hCreate

A good shift runs from a plan, a clear team and quick problem fixing.

  • Hold a five-minute briefing: bookings, specials, allergens, who is in charge.
  • Set stations and a prep list. Count stock at close.
  • Handle complaints with four steps: listen, say sorry, fix, follow up.
  • Review sales, waste and guest comments after each week.
  • Write a prep list from the bookings: how many portions of each dish to make.
  • Close with a checklist: stock count, cleaning, cash count, locks, fridge temperature.
  • Thank the team and note one thing that went well and one to fix at the end of each shift.
Worked example. Waste log: 4 kg of prepared rice thrown out on Friday. If rice costs 2.50 per kg, that is 4 x 2.50 = 10 CAD lost in one night. If smaller batches cut the waste in half, that saves about 5 CAD a night, or 20 CAD over four Fridays.

Activity. Run or support four shifts using a briefing, a prep list and a waste log.

Standard to meet. Task: run four shifts with logs. Condition: real or training service. Standard: briefing, prep and waste logs complete; one improvement shown by numbers.

Module 7 Draw guests, partner and market the place 6 hEvaluate

Guests need to find you and trust you.

  • Keep your online listing, hours, menu and photos true and current.
  • Partner with nearby shops, events and tour groups; share offers.
  • Answer reviews politely within two days.
  • Provide information in English and French where law or guests need it, and make the venue accessible for people with disabilities.
  • Write a short description of the venue in plain English and French if guests need both.
  • Take clear photos of the menu and the room, and update them when things change.
  • Track where new guests heard about you by asking at the table or at booking.
Worked example. A small cafe offers a lunch deal to the office next door and tracks how many use the card each week. In week one 12 cards are used, in week two 18. At 9 CAD per lunch, week two brings in 18 x 9 = 162 CAD, which is 54 more than week one.

Activity. Run a four-week offer, track the redemptions and respond to every review.

Standard to meet. Task: test one marketing offer. Condition: four weeks with a tracking method. Standard: results recorded, every review answered, a decision on whether to repeat.

Module 8 Pilot a venue or service and plan to grow 8 hCreate

Prove the idea small before you commit more money.

  • Define a pilot: pop-up, weekend menu, one room type or catering for one event.
  • Set measures and a stop rule: covers, average spend, waste, rating, profit.
  • Keep a risk register: staff shortage, supplier failure, health issue, bad weather, theft.
  • After the pilot write a review and a 12-month plan.
  • Pick one location, one menu and one date range for the pilot, and write them on one page.
  • Count guests, average spend and waste every day during the pilot.
  • Ask for guest and staff feedback in writing at the end of the pilot.
Worked example. Stop rule: 'If we serve fewer than 40 covers a day for 30 days, we change the menu or location.' At 40 covers and an average spend of 12 CAD, a day brings in 480 CAD, so 30 days brings in 14,400 CAD. Below 40 covers, income falls short of that plan.

Activity. Run the pilot, write the review and the plan, and present to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run a pilot and review. Condition: real guests and safe, legal operation. Standard: measures tracked, safety rules met, decision supported by numbers.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
