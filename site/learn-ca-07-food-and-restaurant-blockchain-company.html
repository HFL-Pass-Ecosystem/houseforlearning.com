Food and restaurant blockchain company

Run a pilot of food and restaurant blockchain company in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small food and restaurant blockchain company offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Find the traceability problem worth solving 6 hUnderstand

Food traceability means knowing where every ingredient came from and where it went. A shared record can speed recalls and prove claims such as organic or local.

  • List what goes wrong today: slow recalls, paper records, disputed origin claims, waste from over-recalling.
  • Map one product chain from farm to plate: grower, packer, distributor, restaurant. Mark who writes records and who reads them.
  • A shared ledger helps only when several parties must trust one record and no single party should control it. If one firm controls the chain, a normal database is simpler and cheaper.
  • Write the question for a buyer: which problem costs you most each year, and what would you pay to cut it?
  • Interview one person at each step of the chain and write down, in their words, what record they keep today and how long it takes to find one lot.
  • Count the cost of one past incident: lots recalled, product thrown out, sales lost and staff hours. Use the buyer's own figures, not guesses.
  • Rank three candidate problems by yearly cost and by how few parties must change habits; choose the one with high cost and low change.
Worked example. A restaurant group recalled all romaine for 5 days because origin was unclear, losing about 18,000 in sales. If batch records had narrowed the recall to 2 of 10 lots, only 20% of stock would be pulled, so the loss would be about 18,000 x 0.20 = 3,600, a saving of 14,400. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Map one real chain and decide, with reasons, whether a shared ledger beats a normal database.

Standard to meet. The decision names who writes, who reads and who pays.

Module 2 Know food, data and finance rules 6 hApply

Food rules, privacy rules and software rules all apply to a shared traceability record.

  • Many food rules expect traceability one step back and one step forward; check the current federal and provincial rule for your products.
  • Labels and claims such as organic, local or fair-trade need certification and proof.
  • Privacy law applies to any personal data on staff or customers stored in records; do not place personal data on a ledger that cannot be erased.
  • If a token or coin is involved, securities and money-services rules may apply; get advice before you issue anything.
  • List every document a regulator or certifier may ask to see and how long you must keep each one; check the federal and provincial rule for the time.
  • Write a short data-sharing agreement for pilot suppliers that says who can see which fields and what happens to the data when a supplier leaves.
  • Ask the provincial food inspection office which records they accept in digital form, and file their written answer in your rules table.
Worked example. Row: 'Claim organic on a menu | federal labelling rules and certifier | show certificate and lot link | before printing | certificate copy filed'. Row: 'Store supplier contact names on the record | privacy law | keep business contacts only | at sign-up | consent form on file'. A pilot with 6 restaurants and 4 shared suppliers has 6 + 4 = 10 parties, so the data agreement needs 10 signatures.

Activity. Build a rules table for a six-restaurant pilot.

Standard to meet. No row guessed; sources dated.

Module 3 Design the record and the data capture 10 hApply

Good traceability starts with simple capture at each hand-over.

  • Define the minimum record: lot code, product, quantity, date, from, to, temperature if cold chain.
  • Use barcodes or QR codes on cases; test that a phone camera reads them on a wet, cold case.
  • Decide the ledger design: who runs the nodes, who can write, how errors are corrected (a new entry, not an edit).
  • Keep personal data off the shared record; store only business data.
  • Time a real hand-over: count the seconds to scan a case, enter a quantity and confirm. If it takes over 30 seconds, remove a field.
  • Build a test set of 20 entries that includes 5 deliberate mistakes (wrong quantity, missing lot, duplicate scan) and check that your process catches each one.
  • Write a trace query in plain words first ('where did lot R-0612-A go?'), then build it, and check that it also answers the forward question ('which kitchens received it?').
Worked example. Lot 'R-0612-A' moves farm to packer to distributor to kitchen: four entries, each with time and quantity. A trace query returns the full path in under 10 seconds. If one scan takes 12 seconds, the lot costs 4 x 12 = 48 seconds of staff time in all.

Activity. Build a spreadsheet prototype for one chain with at least 20 entries and run two trace queries.

Standard to meet. Both queries return the full path; errors fixed by new entries.

Module 4 Price the service and prove the saving 8 hEvaluate

Customers pay when savings beat the fee.

  • Set the price per supplier per month (or per site), plus a one-time set-up charge.
  • Compute the customer benefit: recall cost avoided, labour saved on paperwork and any premium for proven origin.
  • Compute your own cost: cloud hosting, support, scanners and onboarding time.
  • Break-even = fixed cost divided by monthly margin per supplier.
  • Interview two buyers to learn what they pay now for paper filing, audits and recalls; use their numbers as the ceiling for your price.
  • Offer a starter price for small suppliers and a higher price for large ones, and compute the margin of each tier separately.
  • Run a worst case: if only half the planned suppliers sign, show how large the monthly loss is and how many months your cash lasts.
Worked example. Price 2,400 a year (200 a month); variable cost 900 a year (75 a month); margin 125 a month per supplier. Fixed cost 9,000 a month needs 9,000 / 125 = 72 suppliers to break even; if only 36 sign, margin is 36 x 125 = 4,500 and the loss is 4,500 a month. A supplier that saves 4 hours of paperwork a week at 25 an hour saves 4 x 25 x 52 = 5,200 a year, well above the 2,400 fee. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build the cost sheet and a customer saving case.

Standard to meet. Units shown; saving case uses customer data.

Module 5 Onboard suppliers and keep data clean 8 hApply

The network only works if small suppliers can join easily.

  • Offer phone-first capture with a paper fallback and later entry.
  • Train in under one hour; provide a one-page guide in English and French.
  • Audit sample entries monthly against physical stock.
  • Set a rule for late or missing entries and apply it kindly.
  • Visit one supplier on site and watch a hand-over; note where gloves, wet hands, poor signal or language make capture hard.
  • Make a help sheet of the five most common mistakes, with a photo of each and the fix.
  • Track the time from hand-over to entry; set a target (for example within 4 hours) and report it weekly.
Worked example. Pilot week 1: 62% of cases scanned; week 4: 91% after a quick guide and a helpline. With 400 cases a week, week 1 scanned 400 x 0.62 = 248 and week 4 scanned 400 x 0.91 = 364, so 116 more cases a week could be traced.

Activity. Run a 4-week capture test with three suppliers or a simulation.

Standard to meet. Scan rate tracked weekly.

Module 6 Sell to buyers and partners 6 hCreate

Start where the pain is highest.

  • Approach restaurant groups, grocers, distributors and certifiers.
  • Offer a 90-day pilot with a written success measure.
  • Show a live trace demo in under two minutes.
  • Track the funnel: demos, pilots and paid customers.
  • Prepare a one-page pilot offer: length, price, success measure, who does what, and how either side can stop.
  • Prepare answers to three common objections (cost, extra work, data ownership) with a figure or example for each.
  • Ask each buyer who else signs off; map the decision makers in operations, quality and finance, and speak to all three.
Worked example. Funnel: 30 demos, 8 pilots, 4 paid. Demo-to-pilot is 8 / 30 = 27%; pilot-to-paid is 4 / 8 = 50%; demo-to-paid is 4 / 30 = 13%.

Activity. Deliver a demo to five buyers.

Standard to meet. Funnel recorded.

Module 7 Manage trust, security and misuse risk 6 hEvaluate

A record is only as true as what goes in.

  • Garbage in, garbage out: a ledger proves an entry was made, not that it is true; add checks such as sensors and audits.
  • Secure keys and access, and review accounts monthly.
  • Plan for supplier exit, including how data is returned.
  • Avoid hype: do not claim the ledger makes food safe.
  • List the ways an entry can be false (typo, fraud, scanner on the wrong case) and pick one check for each.
  • Run a recovery drill: a supplier loses a phone with the app; show that access is removed in under one hour and past entries stay intact.
  • Write the public wording of what the system proves and does not prove, and ask a buyer to read it back to you.
Worked example. Risk: 'False origin entry | 3 x 5 | spot audits and sensor data | quality lead | monthly'. Likelihood 3 x impact 5 = 15 of 25, so it ranks near the top. If a spot audit of 10 lots finds 1 mismatch, the error rate is 1 / 10 = 10%, above a 5% limit, so widen the audit to 20 lots and find the cause.

Activity. Write an eight-row risk register.

Standard to meet. Each row has owner and review date.

Module 8 Pilot, review and scale 8 hCreate

Prove it on one chain.

  • Pilot for 90 days on one product with five suppliers.
  • Measure trace time, scan rate and recall scope.
  • Set a stop rule: if trace time is over 1 minute after 60 days, redesign.
  • Plan the next chain only after the review.
  • Agree the success numbers with the buyer before the pilot starts and write them down: trace time, scan rate and recall scope.
  • Hold a review every two weeks with all five suppliers; log each problem with an owner and a date.
  • On day 90, write a one-page decision (scale, change or stop) with the figures behind it.
Worked example. Result: trace time 8 seconds; scan rate 91%; proceed. Recall scope fell from 10 of 10 lots to 2 of 10, so 100% - 20% = 80% less product is pulled. All three measures beat the example targets (under 60 seconds, over 85%, under 50%).

Activity. Run the pilot and present review.

Standard to meet. Review has measures and decision.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
