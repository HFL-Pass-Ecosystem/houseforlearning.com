Portfolio Management Services

Run a pilot of portfolio Management Services in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small portfolio Management Services offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: finance and accounting. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know what a portfolio is and what the words mean 6 hUnderstand

This course is about how portfolio management work is done. It is education, not personal advice. Consult a licensed professional for your own money.

  • Explain that a portfolio is a set of investments held together for a goal.
  • Name the main asset types: cash, bonds, shares and funds.
  • Explain risk as the chance that results differ from what you hoped.
  • Explain return, fees and time frame in plain words.
  • Say clearly that managing money for others is a regulated activity.
  • Describe the difference between saving, investing and borrowing.
  • Identify the roles in the work: client, adviser, portfolio assistant and compliance.
Worked example. A portfolio holds cash for next year's needs, bonds for steadier income and shares for long-term growth. Each part has a different job. For example, 10,000 split 10%, 40% and 50% is 1,000 in cash, 4,000 in bonds and 5,000 in shares.

Activity. Write a one-page glossary of ten terms and explain it aloud to a friend.

Standard to meet. Task: define ten portfolio terms. Condition: own words, one page. Standard: each term is correct and a friend can repeat it back.

Module 2 Know the rules and who may do this work 6 hApply

Managing money for other people is regulated in Canada. Know the line before you cross it.

  • Explain that advising on or managing investments for others usually requires registration with the provincial securities regulator.
  • Check the current rule with your provincial regulator before you offer any service.
  • Explain that a firm must know its client and check that each investment suits the client.
  • Explain the duty to put the client's interest first and to tell about conflicts.
  • Explain anti-money-laundering rules: know your client, keep records, report suspicious activity.
  • Locate the provincial securities regulator's page on registration and write down the link.
  • Describe a complaint process and tell the client how to use it.
Worked example. A person wants to start 'managing friends' money' for a fee. The right step is to ask the regulator what registration is needed first, not to begin. The person writes down the questions and keeps the answer in writing.

Activity. Make a rules table with rule, who enforces it, what it requires and the source with date.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table. Condition: official regulator pages only. Standard: each row has an enforcer, an action and a dated source; no rule is guessed.

Module 3 Know the client: goals, time frame and comfort with risk 10 hApply

A portfolio starts with the person, not the product. This is how professionals gather facts.

  • Ask about goals, time frame, income, debts, savings and needs for cash.
  • Ask how the person feels about losses and write it down in their words.
  • Separate must-have goals from nice-to-have goals.
  • Explain why a short time frame limits how much risk makes sense.
  • Record everything and have the client confirm it.
  • Describe how a client's age, job stability and emergency savings affect the risk that fits.
  • Write a summary in plain words and read it back to the client.
Worked example. A client needs a house down payment in 2 years. Because the time is short, the plan favours safer holdings and the note explains why. If the client needs 40,000 and has 30,000, the gap is 10,000, or 5,000 a year to save.

Activity. Write a fact-finding form with ten questions and practise it with a partner.

Standard to meet. Task: complete a fact-finding. Condition: a partner playing the client. Standard: goals, time frame, cash needs and risk comfort are recorded and confirmed by the partner.

Module 4 Build a simple example portfolio 8 hEvaluate

Build a practice portfolio on paper to learn how parts fit. This is for study only.

  • Explain diversification: spreading across different holdings so one problem hurts less.
  • Match a mix of cash, bonds and shares to a goal and time frame in a worked example.
  • Explain how fees reduce returns over many years.
  • Explain rebalancing: moving back to the planned mix when prices change it.
  • Write the reason for every holding in one sentence.
  • Compare a low-fee fund with a high-fee fund over time.
  • Explain what a cash reserve is for.
Worked example. Plan: 10% cash, 40% bonds, 50% shares. After a rise, shares are 58%, bonds 33% and cash 9%. Rebalancing sells 8 points of shares and adds 7 to bonds and 1 to cash to return to the plan.

Activity. Build a practice portfolio on paper for a made-up client and write the reason for each part.

Standard to meet. Task: build a practice portfolio. Condition: made-up client and numbers. Standard: mix fits the goal and time frame, each holding has a reason and fees are shown.

Module 5 Measure results the honest way 8 hApply

Results must be measured fairly and shown with the risk.

  • Compute simple return = (end value - start value) / start value.
  • Explain that past results do not promise future results.
  • Compare a portfolio to a fair benchmark with a similar mix.
  • Show worst falls as well as gains.
  • Show results after fees.
  • Compute a yearly return from two year-end values and compare years.
  • Explain the difference between a return in money and a return in percent.
Worked example. Start 10,000, end 10,600 after fees: return = 600 / 10,000 = 6%. In the same year the portfolio fell 9% at its worst point, and the report shows both. If the benchmark returned 5%, the portfolio is 1 percentage point ahead.

Activity. Compute and report returns, fees and worst fall for a made-up portfolio over three years.

Standard to meet. Task: write a results report. Condition: a made-up data set. Standard: numbers are correct, a benchmark is stated and risk is shown beside return.

Module 6 Report, communicate and keep records 6 hCreate

Clients need clear reports, honest words and safe records.

  • Write reports in plain language: what you hold, what it did, what it cost, what changes.
  • Never promise returns; show ranges and risks.
  • Keep notes of every instruction and every decision with its date.
  • Protect client data under privacy law; keep records for the time the regulator requires and check it.
  • Explain fee types and tell the client in writing.
  • Put a summary of five lines at the top of each report.
  • Store records securely and limit who can open them.
Worked example. A client calls worried after a fall. The adviser opens the plan, shows the goal and time frame, and notes the call with date and outcome. The note also records that no change was made. As a pricing example, a firm bills a one-hour client review at 150 CAD; the assistant's cost for that hour is 35 CAD, so the margin is 115 CAD, about 77% of the price, before overhead, and the fee is shown to the client in writing.

Activity. Write a one-page client report for the practice portfolio, with fees and risk in plain words.

Standard to meet. Task: write a client report. Condition: one page. Standard: no promises, fees and risk shown and a plain-word summary at the top that a teenager can follow.

Module 7 Manage conflicts, ethics and risk 6 hEvaluate

Trust is the product. Handle conflicts, errors and misuse of data openly.

  • List common conflicts: commissions, own holdings, rewarding sales over fit.
  • Tell the client about any conflict in writing.
  • Have a process for errors: tell the client, fix, record the cause.
  • Spot fraud signs: pressure, guaranteed returns, missing documents.
  • Check suitability again whenever the client's life changes.
  • Describe who the client can complain to if they are not satisfied.
  • Explain why gifts and favours from product sellers must be disclosed or declined; check the firm's rule.
Worked example. An adviser would earn more from one fund. She tells the client and shows a comparable lower-cost option, and records the client's choice. If the funds cost 1.0% and 1.8% a year, the difference on 10,000 is 0.8% of 10,000 = 80 a year.

Activity. Write a one-page conflict and error policy and test it on two made-up cases.

Standard to meet. Task: write a policy. Condition: one page, two cases. Standard: each case follows the policy and the record is complete.

Module 8 Run a portfolio process under supervision 8 hCreate

Run the whole process as a learner in a safe setting: made-up or supervised cases only, under a registered professional.

  • Work with a registered supervisor for any real client matter.
  • Run the cycle: fact-find, plan, build, report, review.
  • Review each practice portfolio every quarter on paper.
  • Record what went wrong and what you changed.
  • Write a plan for your next training step, for example the exams or registration route; check the current requirements with the regulator.
  • Prepare a case file for each practice client: facts, plan, reports and reviews.
  • Present your results to a supervisor and write down their feedback.
Worked example. A student runs three made-up clients for two quarters. The log shows one plan drifted from its mix, so she rebalanced it and noted the reason. With 3 clients and 2 quarters, that is 6 reviews in all.

Activity. Run three practice cases for two quarters and present your review to a panel.

Standard to meet. Task: run practice cases. Condition: made-up data or a registered supervisor. Standard: the cycle is complete, records are dated and the review reasons from evidence.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
