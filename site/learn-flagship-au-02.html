AU-02 · full course

Pharma and Life Sciences Operations

Learn how medicines and health products move from development to patient. The course focuses on accurate quality records, safe storage, tracing batches and explaining risks clearly, all with practice documents.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 140 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Learn how medicines and health products move from development to patient. The course focuses on accurate quality records, safe storage, tracing batches and explaining risks clearly, all with practice documents.

  • Understand Explain the path from development to patient and why each stage has checks.
  • Apply Complete records and cold-chain checks accurately.
  • Analyse Analyse deviations to find causes and prevent repeats.
  • Create Write a plain-language safety summary.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Complete a quality-record exercise with ten practice records, a cold-chain log review, a deviation analysis and a plain-language safety summary using practice documents only.

Condition

About 140 hours over 3 to 6 months. You need practice documents, a free spreadsheet program, a qualified adviser for review, a mentor and a peer circle.

Standard

Ten practice records complete with no gaps; cold-chain log reviewed with a recorded decision; three deviation reports with cause and action; safety summary at a plain reading level; ethics note; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Use practice documents. Never handle, advise on or supply medicines unless qualified and permitted. Check with your local authority about the rules for medicines and health products in your area.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (140 h guided)

Module 1 Development to patient 12 hUnderstand

A medicine takes many years and many checks to reach a patient. Each stage exists to answer a question about safety, quality or benefit, and each has someone responsible for the answer.

  • The main stages are discovery, laboratory testing, human trials, approval, manufacture and supply, and monitoring after release.
  • Human trials usually run in phases, starting with a small group to check safety and growing to larger groups to check benefit.
  • Approval means an independent authority reviews the evidence before the product can be supplied.
  • Manufacture follows written procedures so every batch matches the approved quality.
  • After release, reports of side effects are collected so new risks can be found and acted on.
Worked example. A trial site must have approval before the first person is enrolled. If a plan to enrol 60 people is split into three groups of 20, each group is checked for safety before the next group starts, so a problem is found with 20 people, not 60.

Activity. Draw the path from discovery to monitoring, naming each stage, what question it answers and who is responsible.

Standard to meet. Name all six stages in order, each with one question and one responsible role.

Module 2 Quality records 20 hApply

If it is not written down, it did not happen. Good records are clear, complete and dated, and they let anyone understand what was done and by whom.

  • Record who did the task, what was done, when it was done and the result, using the same format every time.
  • Write in permanent ink or in a system that tracks changes, and never use correction fluid.
  • Correct a mistake by drawing one line through it, writing the right value, then adding your initials and the date.
  • Never leave a blank field; write not applicable or a dash if nothing belongs there.
  • Have a second person check each record against the procedure before it is filed.
Worked example. A record shows a weight entered as 12.5 g but the true value was 15.2 g. The writer draws one line through 12.5, writes 15.2, and adds the initials and the date next to it. The original stays readable, so the change can be checked.

Activity. Complete ten practice records using the correct format and correct at least two mistakes the right way.

Standard to meet. Complete all ten records with no blank fields, line through, initial and date every correction, and have a second person sign each one.

Module 3 Batch and traceability 16 hApply

Traceability means you can say exactly where each batch went. If a problem is found, quick tracing limits harm and makes a recall faster.

  • Every batch has a unique number printed on the label and recorded on each document.
  • Keep a distribution list showing which customers received which batch and how many units.
  • Record every handover so the chain of custody shows who held the product at each step.
  • Run a mock recall to test whether you can find all units of one batch within a set time.
  • Check that the number of units found plus those still in stock equals the number produced.
Worked example. A batch of 2,400 units is traced in a mock recall. The distribution list shows 1,900 units sent to 8 customers and 500 units in stock. Since 1,900 + 500 = 2,400, all units are accounted for, and the team finishes in 1 hour 40 minutes, inside the 2-hour target.

Activity. Complete a timed trace exercise on a practice batch and write a one-page result with the time taken and any units missing.

Standard to meet. Finish the trace inside two hours and check that units found plus units in stock equal units produced.

Module 4 Cold chain and storage 20 hApply

Many health products must stay in a set temperature range. A reading outside the range is an excursion, and the response depends on how far and how long it went.

  • Know the allowed range on the label, for example 2 C to 8 C for refrigerated products.
  • Use calibrated sensors and log readings at set times, such as every 4 hours, plus a continuous recorder where possible.
  • Treat any reading outside the range as an excursion and write down the start time, end time and highest or lowest reading.
  • Isolate the product, tell the responsible person and do not use or ship it until a decision is recorded.
  • Keep calibration certificates and check that each sensor is within its calibration date.
Worked example. A fridge limit is 2 C to 8 C. The log shows 9.5 C for 3 hours overnight. That is an excursion of 1.5 C above the limit for 3 hours, so the stock is quarantined and sent for review, and the decision with a signature is written in the log.

Activity. Review a practice temperature log, mark every excursion and record a decision for each one.

Standard to meet. Find every excursion, with start, end, peak and a signed decision for each.

Module 5 Deviations and improvement 20 hAnalyse

A deviation is anything that did not go as written. Handling deviations well means finding the real cause, fixing the problem and stopping it from returning.

  • Describe the event with facts only: what happened, when, where and who found it.
  • Ask why at least three times to move from the symptom to the root cause.
  • Record an immediate fix and a longer-term action that prevents repeats.
  • Judge the impact on product quality and patient safety, and decide who must be told.
  • Review deviations monthly to spot trends, such as the same fault on the same shift.
Worked example. Three batch records in one month lack a signature. Asking why shows the signing step is not on the checklist. The fix is to add a signature box and a final check; the count of missing signatures is tracked, and falls from 3 in month one to 0 in month two.

Activity. Analyse three practice deviations and write a report for each with description, cause, fix and prevention.

Standard to meet. Write each report with four labelled parts, a cause that goes deeper than the symptom and an action with an owner and a date.

Module 6 Safety communication 20 hCreate

People who take medicines need to understand the risks without a medical degree. Plain language is a safety tool, not a style choice.

  • Write short sentences of about 15 words or fewer and use everyday words, such as stomach pain, not abdominal pain.
  • State the benefit and the main risks side by side and include how often each risk happens, for example 1 in 100.
  • Avoid claims that cannot be shown, such as completely safe or no side effects.
  • Tell the reader what to do if a problem happens and who to contact.
  • Test the text with a reader unfamiliar with the topic and aim for a reading level of about Year 8.
Worked example. Instead of: hypotension may occur in some patients, write: your blood pressure may drop and you may feel dizzy. This happens to about 2 in 100 people. If you feel faint, sit down and call your health service.

Activity. Write a one-page plain-language summary for a practice product and test it with one reader.

Standard to meet. Write the summary in short sentences, give a number for each risk, include what to do, and have the reader explain it back.

Module 7 Ethics and consent 16 hEvaluate

People in trials and people using products trust the system to protect them. Ethics here means consent, privacy and honest reporting, even when the truth is inconvenient.

  • Informed consent means a person is told the purpose, risks and their right to leave, and agrees freely without pressure.
  • Keep personal and health data private, share it only with people who need it and remove names where you can.
  • Report every adverse event honestly and quickly, even if it seems minor or unrelated.
  • Never change or hide a result to make a product look better.
  • Know who to report concerns to, and check with your local authority for the rules in your area.
Worked example. During a practice exercise, a participant reports a headache two hours after a dose. The team writes it as an adverse event within one working day, even though it may be unrelated, so the safety team can look for a pattern across all participants.

Activity. Write a one-page ethics note covering consent, privacy and reporting for a practice scenario.

Standard to meet. Name the three principles in the note, give one example of each and say who to report to.

Module 8 Review 16 hEvaluate

The review is where you turn practice into lasting skill. You compare your work with the standard and decide what to improve first.

  • List three lessons, each linked to a specific record, log or report you produced.
  • Rate your own work against the standard for each exercise and note the biggest gap.
  • Create a short plan with one action, an owner, a date and a measure for each gap.
  • Share your work with a peer and invite one written comment.
  • Update your checklist or template so the lesson is built into the next piece of work.
Worked example. You find three of ten records had a missing date. The plan is to add a date check to the final review, with the aim of zero missing dates in the next ten records by the end of next month, and the new checklist is shared with your peer.

Activity. Present your three lessons, your plan and one updated checklist to your mentor or peer circle.

Standard to meet. Include in the plan at least two actions with a date and a measure, and add at least one new check to the checklist.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why must trials come before general supply?

Model answer: Trials test safety and benefit on small groups first so problems are found before many people are exposed. (Covered in module 1: Development to patient. To pass that module: Name all six stages in order, each with one question and one responsible role.)

Q2. How do you correct a mistake in a quality record?

Model answer: Draw one line through it, write the correct entry, and add your initials and the date so the original is still readable. (Covered in module 2: Quality records. To pass that module: Complete all ten records with no blank fields, line through, initial and date every correction, and have a second person sign each one.)

Q3. Why does every batch have a number?

Model answer: The number lets you trace where each unit went and remove affected units quickly in a recall. (Covered in module 3: Batch and traceability. To pass that module: Finish the trace inside two hours and check that units found plus units in stock equal units produced.)

Q4. What is an excursion?

Model answer: A reading outside the allowed temperature range, which must be recorded and reviewed before the product is used. (Covered in module 4: Cold chain and storage. To pass that module: Find every excursion, with start, end, peak and a signed decision for each.)

Q5. What are the four parts of a deviation report?

Model answer: Describe the event, find the cause, fix it, and prevent it from happening again. (Covered in module 5: Deviations and improvement. To pass that module: Write each report with four labelled parts, a cause that goes deeper than the symptom and an action with an owner and a date.)

Q6. Why is plain language important for safety information?

Model answer: Patients must understand the risks and what to do, or they cannot use the product safely. (Covered in module 6: Safety communication. To pass that module: Write the summary in short sentences, give a number for each risk, include what to do, and have the reader explain it back.)

Q7. Why must adverse events be reported even when they seem unrelated?

Model answer: A single report may look minor but a pattern across reports can reveal a real risk and protect patients. (Covered in module 7: Ethics and consent. To pass that module: Name the three principles in the note, give one example of each and say who to report to.)

Q8. What should you do after finding a gap in your work?

Model answer: Write a specific action with a date and a measure, and add a check to your checklist so the gap is less likely to return. (Covered in module 8: Review. To pass that module: Include in the plan at least two actions with a date and a measure, and add at least one new check to the checklist.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Complete a quality-record exercise with ten practice records, a cold-chain log review, a deviation analysis and a plain-language safety summary using practice documents only. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Ten practice records complete with no gaps; cold-chain log reviewed with a recorded decision; three deviation reports with cause and action; safety summary at a plain reading level; ethics note; at least 70% on the final check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
