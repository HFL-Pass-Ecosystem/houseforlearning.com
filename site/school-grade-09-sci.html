Grade 09: Science

8 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 09 Science, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 8 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

Unit 1 Physics: motion Weeks 1 to 5Understand

In this unit you work on 'Physics: motion', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: describe motion with graphs and equations. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Physics: motion' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe motion with graphs and equations. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Speed = distance / time. A bus covers 120 km in 2 hours: 120 / 2 = 60 km/h. Starting at 0 and reaching 20 m/s in 5 s means acceleration = 20 / 5 = 4 m/s squared. Method: Model run for 'Physics: motion'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Describe motion with graphs and equations: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 3 Physics: gravitation, work and energy Weeks 11 to 15Apply

In this unit you work on 'Physics: gravitation, work and energy', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: use universal gravitation and work and energy. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Physics: gravitation, work and energy' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: use universal gravitation and work and energy. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Work = force x distance. 20 N over 3 m = 60 J. Kinetic energy of 2 kg at 3 m/s = 1/2 x 2 x 9 = 9 J. Method: Model run for 'Physics: gravitation, work and energy'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Use Newton's law of gravitation F = G m1 m2 / r^2 with G = 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2 kg^-2. For two 10 kg balls 0.5 m apart, F = 6.67e-11 x 10 x 10 / 0.25 = 2.67 x 10^-8 N, tiny.
  2. Step 2, Find g on Earth. Using M = 6.0 x 10^24 kg and R = 6.4 x 10^6 m: g = GM/R^2 = 6.67e-11 x 6.0e24 / 4.1e13 = 9.77 m/s2, close to 9.8. Weight = m g, so a 50 kg student weighs 490 N.
  3. Step 3, Compute work. A 20 kg box is lifted 3 m. Force needed equals weight 20 x 9.8 = 196 N. Work W = F x d = 196 x 3 = 588 J.
  4. Step 4, Use energy. Potential energy at height is mgh = 588 J. If the box falls, KE at the ground is 588 J. Then v = sqrt(2KE/m) = sqrt(2 x 588 / 20) = sqrt(58.8) = 7.7 m/s, the same as sqrt(2gh).
  5. Step 5, Power: lifting that 588 J in 4 s gives P = 588 / 4 = 147 W. Conclusion: work transfers energy; energy changes form but its total is conserved.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a 2 kg stone falls from rest through 5 m. Find KE gained and speed (g = 10). Answer: KE = 2 x 10 x 5 = 100 J; v = sqrt(2 x 100 / 2) = 10 m/s.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Use universal gravitation and work and energy: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 4 Physics: sound Weeks 16 to 20Understand

In this unit you work on 'Physics: sound', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: explain sound waves and echo. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Physics: sound' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: explain sound waves and echo. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Echo: sound at 340 m/s returns after 2 s, so it travelled 680 m there and back, and the wall is 340 m away. Method: Model run for 'Physics: sound'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe a sound wave. A tuning fork vibrates and compresses air then leaves a rarefaction, forming longitudinal waves. Mark compressions as high pressure and rarefactions as low pressure on a diagram.
  2. Step 2, Define terms. Frequency f in hertz is vibrations per second, wavelength lambda is the distance between two compressions, and wave speed v = f x lambda. For f = 340 Hz and lambda = 1 m, v = 340 m/s.
  3. Step 3, Solve for wavelength. Audible range is 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. At 340 m/s, a 20 Hz sound has lambda = 340 / 20 = 17 m and a 20,000 Hz sound has lambda = 340 / 20000 = 0.017 m.
  4. Step 4, Do an echo calculation. A girl claps and hears the echo after 0.6 s. Sound speed 340 m/s means the total path is 340 x 0.6 = 204 m, so the wall is 204 / 2 = 102 m away.
  5. Step 5, Link to uses: sonar sends ultrasound (above 20 kHz) and measures echo time, and ultrasound scans use frequencies of a few MHz. Loudness relates to amplitude, pitch to frequency.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a ship's sonar receives an echo after 4 s in water where sound speed is 1500 m/s. Find the depth. Answer: 1500 x 4 / 2 = 3000 m.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Explain sound waves and echo: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 5 Chemistry: matter in our surroundings and is matter around us pure Weeks 21 to 24Understand

In this unit you work on 'Chemistry: matter in our surroundings and is matter around us pure', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: classify matter and calculate concentration. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Chemistry: matter in our surroundings and is matter around us pure' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: classify matter and calculate concentration. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: 10 g of salt in 90 g of water: solution mass 100 g, so concentration = 10 / 100 x 100 = 10 percent by mass. Method: Model run for 'Chemistry: matter in our surroundings and is matter around us pure'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Classify matter. Pure substances are elements like iron and compounds like water. Mixtures are homogeneous like salt solution or heterogeneous like soil and water. Test: a pure substance has a fixed melting point.
  2. Step 2, Compare mixture and compound. Iron and sulphur mixed can be separated by a magnet. Heated together they form iron sulphide, a compound with different properties, not attracted by the magnet.
  3. Step 3, Calculate mass percent. A solution contains 10 g of salt in 90 g of water. Solution mass = 100 g. Mass by mass percentage = 10 / 100 x 100 = 10 per cent.
  4. Step 4, Calculate volume percent. 25 mL of alcohol in 100 mL of solution gives 25 per cent by volume. Mass by volume of 5 g in 200 mL is 5 / 200 x 100 = 2.5 per cent.
  5. Step 5, Describe particles. States differ by spacing and motion. Evaporation occurs below boiling, and rate rises with temperature, surface area and wind. Convert 300 K to Celsius: 300 - 273 = 27 C.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: find the concentration of a solution of 20 g sugar in 180 g water. Answer: 20 / 200 x 100 = 10 per cent by mass.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Classify matter and calculate concentration: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 6 Chemistry: atoms, molecules and the structure of the atom Weeks 25 to 28Apply

In this unit you work on 'Chemistry: atoms, molecules and the structure of the atom', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: use symbols, formulae and the atomic model. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Chemistry: atoms, molecules and the structure of the atom' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: use symbols, formulae and the atomic model. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Water is H2O: 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. Mass = 2 x 1 + 16 = 18 units. Check atoms balance on both sides of an equation. Method: Model run for 'Chemistry: atoms, molecules and the structure of the atom'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Write symbols and valencies. Sodium Na is +1, chlorine Cl is -1, oxygen O is -2, calcium Ca is +2. Criss-cross: Na (1) and Cl (1) give NaCl. Ca (2) and Cl (1) give CaCl2. Al (3) and O (2) give Al2O3.
  2. Step 2, Calculate molecular mass. Using atomic masses H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40: H2O = 2 + 16 = 18 u. CO2 = 12 + 32 = 44 u. CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 u.
  3. Step 3, State the laws. Conservation of mass: in a reaction, 4 g of hydrogen forms water with 32 g of oxygen to give 36 g of water, so mass is unchanged. Constant proportion: in water H to O mass is always 1 to 8.
  4. Step 4, Describe the atom. Sodium has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons, so atomic number 11 and mass number 23. Electron configuration is 2, 8, 1, with one valence electron that is easily lost.
  5. Step 5, Compare isotopes: chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 have the same 17 protons but 18 and 20 neutrons. Average atomic mass about 35.5 u because of the abundance, roughly 3 to 1.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: write the formula for magnesium chloride and its molecular mass (Mg 24, Cl 35.5). Answer: MgCl2; 24 + 71 = 95 u.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Use symbols, formulae and the atomic model: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 8 Biology: diversity, health and natural resources Weeks 33 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Biology: diversity, health and natural resources', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: classify organisms and explain disease and resource cycles. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Biology: diversity, health and natural resources' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: classify organisms and explain disease and resource cycles. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Use a key: has a backbone? yes, vertebrate. Has feathers? yes, bird. Say how disease spreads and how to prevent it. Method: Model run for 'Biology: diversity, health and natural resources'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Classify with the five kingdoms: Monera (bacteria), Protista (amoeba), Fungi (mushroom), Plantae (mango tree) and Animalia (tiger). Take the tiger: Kingdom Animalia, Phylum Chordata, Class Mammalia, Order Carnivora, Family Felidae, Genus Panthera, species tigris.
  2. Step 2, Trace disease. Malaria is caused by Plasmodium protozoa spread by the female Anopheles mosquito. It is infectious. Diabetes is a non-infectious disease. Prevention for malaria: nets, drain standing water, prompt treatment.
  3. Step 3, Explain vaccination. A weakened antigen trains the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells. A later exposure to the real pathogen is then met by a fast response. Herd immunity protects those not vaccinated.
  4. Step 4, Follow the nitrogen cycle: atmospheric N2, nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium in legume root nodules, nitrates in soil, uptake by plants, passage to animals, decomposition to ammonia, and denitrification to N2.
  5. Step 5, Follow the carbon cycle: plants take CO2 by photosynthesis, animals release it by respiration, and burning fossil fuels adds CO2. The greenhouse effect traps heat; rising CO2 warms the climate.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: name the kingdom and one disease prevention for a mushroom and for cholera. Answer: Fungi; cholera is prevented by clean boiled water and sanitation.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Classify organisms and explain disease and resource cycles: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.
