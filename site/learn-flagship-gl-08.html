flagship course · GL-08

Drones: Operations and Applications

Plan and fly safe, legal drone missions for a real purpose such as mapping, inspection or filming, and turn the data into a useful report.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Plan and fly safe, legal drone missions for a real purpose such as mapping, inspection or filming, and turn the data into a useful report.

  • Understand Explain how a drone flies, what the main rules are and who is at risk.
  • Apply Run a pre-flight check and fly a planned mission in a simulator or a permitted site.
  • Analyse Analyse battery use, wind and image overlap to find why a mission failed or succeeded.
  • Create Build a mission plan, risk assessment and finished report for one client need.
  • Evaluate Judge your mission against safety, legal and quality checks.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan, fly (or simulate) and report one drone mission for a real client need, with a written risk assessment.

Condition

A drone you may legally fly or a simulator, a permitted open site, a free planning and mapping tool, a spreadsheet, a mentor with flying experience and a peer circle. About 12 weeks part-time.

Standard

Local rules named and followed; written risk assessment signed by the mentor; pre-flight checklist complete; battery plan leaves a 30% reserve; report answers the client question with evidence.

Who it is for: Surveyors, farmers, estate managers, media learners, safety staff, college learners. Requirements: None; simulator work is accepted if you cannot fly yet. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Fly only where and how your national aviation authority allows. Keep away from people, roads, airports and private property without permission. When unsure, do not fly.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 How a drone flies 4 hUnderstand

Understand the four forces and the parts so faults make sense. You will explain how lift, weight, thrust and drag act on a multirotor, so you can reason about a fault instead of guessing at it.

  • Lift, weight, thrust and drag; four or more rotors give control by changing speed.
  • Parts: frame, motors, battery, flight controller, sensors, radio link, camera.
  • Positioning by satellite signal; what happens when the signal is lost.
  • Return-to-home and failsafe settings must be checked before every flight.
  • Learn the four forces of lift, weight, thrust and drag, and note that a multirotor holds position only because its controller adjusts motor speeds many times each second.
  • Check battery health before every flight. Retire a battery that is swollen or loses charge fast under load. Follow the maker's limits, because power loss is a leading cause of crashes.
  • Learn the sound and feel of each motor; unusual vibration is the earliest warning of a fault.
  • Keep spare propellers and a screwdriver in the kit.
Worked example. A drone drifts in a gust. Cause: wind above the stated limit. Action: land, check the forecast, set a 10 m/s stop limit.

Activity. Label a drone diagram and write what each part does.

Standard to meet. Label all main parts and explain failsafe behaviour in your own words.

Common mistake. Thinking the drone is a toy and skipping the failsafe check.

Second worked example. Before a flight, the return height is 30 m but a tree is 35 m. Raise the return height to 45 m and test the setting.

Check yourself. Name the parts that fail most often and how you check each.

Module 2 Rules, permission and insurance 6 hUnderstand

Rules protect people and decide whether you may fly at all. You will check current rules, airspace limits and your paperwork before each new site, so you know in advance whether a flight is allowed.

  • Find your authority's registration, pilot knowledge and no-fly rules.
  • Keep away from crowds, airports, prisons, power sites and emergencies.
  • Get written permission from land owners and respect privacy.
  • Check insurance: who pays if something goes wrong?
  • Before a new site, read the current national rules and airspace map, note the current height limit for that place, and save a screenshot of what you checked with the date.
  • Keep a folder with your registration details, pilot proof, insurance document and any site permission, so you can show them quickly if someone asks.
  • Write the emergency contact and the nearest landing site on the checklist.
  • Check whether schools, hospitals or prisons nearby have their own no-fly rules.
Worked example. A client asks for a shot over a school. The plan notes children, privacy and a no-fly zone: decline, offer a safe alternative angle.

Activity. Write a one-page rules note for your place and get the mentor to check it.

Standard to meet. Name the authority, limits and permissions required in the rules note.

Common mistake. Assuming rules are the same as in another country.

Second worked example. A visitor flies a drone where a pilot licence is needed. The rules note lists the local height limit and the registration, both checked on the official page.

Check yourself. What are the height and distance limits in your place?

Module 3 Risk assessment 8 hApply

Risk is chance times harm. Write it down and reduce it before launch. You will score hazards for likelihood and harm, write controls and set go or no-go limits, so decisions are made calmly before launch.

  • List hazards: people, wind, trees, wires, signal loss, battery failure.
  • Score chance and harm 1 to 5; multiply for a risk score.
  • Add a control for every score of 10 or more.
  • Name who stops the flight and when.
  • List hazards by category such as people, property, wildlife, weather and radio interference, then give each a likelihood and severity score from 1 to 5 and a control.
  • Set go and no-go limits in advance. For example, do not fly if wind gusts exceed 10 metres per second or visibility is under 1 kilometre. Then the decision is made before pressure builds.
  • Re-score the risks when weather changes; a plan is not fixed for the day.
  • Brief every helper in two minutes: what to watch, who can say stop.
Worked example. Wires near the site: chance 3, harm 5, score 15. Control: keep 30 m away, spotter watches the wire side, stop if wind passes the limit.

Activity. Complete a risk assessment for your mission.

Standard to meet. Give every high risk a control and an owner.

Common mistake. Writing "low risk" without a score.

Second worked example. People near the site: chance 2, harm 5, score 10, control: cordon and spotter.

Check yourself. Which hazard has your highest score, and what is the control?

Module 4 Pre-flight and flight skills 8 hApply

Checks come before skill. Then practise slow, smooth control. You will follow a written pre-flight checklist and fly basic patterns smoothly, building the control habits that keep people and equipment safe.

  • Checklist: weather, battery, propellers, firmware (the drone's built-in software), memory card, radio, return point.
  • Practise take-off, hover, square, figure of eight and landing.
  • Keep line of sight unless your rules allow otherwise.
  • Log every flight: date, place, time, battery, faults.
  • Practise a square, a circle and a slow figure of eight at 10 metres height until each is smooth, then repeat with the nose pointing toward you.
  • Use a printed pre-flight checklist covering props, battery seating, memory card, compass, home point and a short hover at 1 metre before climbing.
  • Practise a manual landing from 10 m in a simulator first. Then practise in an empty field with a safety observer.
  • Never fly with a swollen or damaged battery; store batteries in a fire-safe bag.
Worked example. Battery reads 100% at start; mission needs 18 minutes of a 25-minute flight. Reserve = 7 of 25 minutes = 28%, below 30%; shorten the route.

Activity. Fly or simulate five practice drills and log them.

Standard to meet. Log five drills and use the checklist each time.

Common mistake. Flying until the battery warning and landing in panic.

Second worked example. Rule: land at 30%. With 25 minutes of flight, land at about 17 minutes.

Check yourself. What is your land-by time for the mission?

Module 5 Mission planning and data capture 8 hAnalyse

A good plan sets height, overlap and speed to match the question. You will pick height, overlap and speed to match a client's question, so the pictures you collect can actually answer it.

  • Choose height: lower gives more detail, covers less.
  • Plan overlap of about 75% front and 65% side for mapping.
  • Plan batteries and landing spots; fly at steady light.
  • Take a few ground reference points for accuracy checks.
  • For mapping missions choose about 75 percent forward overlap and 65 percent side overlap, and fly at a steady height so the pixel size stays the same across the site.
  • Time flights for even light, ideally midday for mapping or the golden hours for visuals, and avoid shifting cloud that creates patchy exposure across the images.
  • Fly slower for sharper images; blur is a common cause of failed maps.
  • Plan the mission so the sun is behind you where possible, to reduce glare.
Worked example. A 4-hectare field at 60 m height needs 6 passes and 2 batteries. Each pass takes 4 minutes, so 6 passes are 24 minutes of flying. Split over 2 batteries, that is 12 minutes each, which keeps a reserve above 30%.

Activity. Write the mission plan with a map and battery table.

Standard to meet. Plan has height, overlap, passes, batteries and a stop rule.

Common mistake. Planning one huge flight instead of safe, short ones.

Second worked example. Splitting a 6 ha site into two 12-minute flights keeps the reserve above 30% on both.

Check yourself. How many batteries does your plan need and why?

Module 6 Process and check data 10 hCreate

Raw pictures are not an answer. Process, check, then explain. You will process images, check results against known ground points and judge accuracy, so your numbers can be trusted before anyone sees them.

  • Build a map or model with a free tool; check gaps and blur.
  • Compare a measured distance on the ground with the map to find error.
  • Remove people and plates before sharing.
  • Store files with dates and a backup.
  • After processing, check three ground points of known position and compare; an error over twice the pixel size (the ground width of one image dot) means the control or the settings need another look.
  • Back up the raw images to two places before you start processing, and name folders by date, site and flight number so you can trace any result.
  • Keep a copy of raw pictures separate from processed maps.
  • Mark any area with gaps so the client knows what is missing.
Worked example. A 50 m ground tape reads 49.4 m on the map: error 0.6 m, 1.2%. Report it with the result.

Activity. Process your pictures and calculate one error figure.

Standard to meet. Calculate the error figure and list gaps with causes.

Common mistake. Trusting the map without a ground check.

Second worked example. Map width 52.1 m against 52.0 m measured is 0.2% error; report it.

Check yourself. What is the error figure for your map?

Module 7 The client report 6 hEvaluate

The report answers the client's question in plain words with numbers. You will write a short client report with a direct answer, a map and honest limits, so the reader can act without needing the raw files.

  • Start with the answer, then three supporting findings.
  • Add a map or picture for each finding, with scale and date.
  • State limits: weather, accuracy, areas missed.
  • Give next actions and costs in your own currency.
  • Open with a one-paragraph answer to the client's question, then add a map, two key images, the method in five lines and a note on the accuracy found.
  • State plainly what the data cannot show, such as ground under dense trees, and offer a follow-up flight rather than stretching the claims.
  • Use the same scale bar and north arrow on every map in the report.
  • Add a one-line cost per hectare so the client can compare with other methods.
Worked example. Report: "Roof has 12 loose tiles on the north side (map 3). Repair estimate EUR 540 (an estimate; check your market). Re-inspect in 6 months."

Activity. Write a two-page report for your mission.

Standard to meet. Answer first; every claim has a picture or number.

Common mistake. Burying the answer on page two.

Second worked example. Report line one: "Three loose panels on the east roof (map 2); repair estimate EUR 380."

Check yourself. Does your first line answer the client question?

Module 8 Review and next 90 days 4 hCreate

Reflect on what went wrong and right and plan your growth. You will review your flight log for repeated slips and set measurable goals, turning each flight into practice for the next.

  • Hold a short debrief: what happened, why, what to change.
  • Give two peers written feedback.
  • Share a checklist or template to help the next learner.
  • Plan next 90 days: hours to log, skills to add.
  • After every five flights, review the log for patterns such as the same checklist item missed or a battery that sags, and write one improvement for the next five.
  • Set three goals for 90 days, for example one new mission type, a documented accuracy check and one client report, and review progress monthly.
  • Share a near-miss report even if nothing went wrong; patterns show up early.
  • Set a flight-hours target for the next 90 days and log each hour.
Worked example. Debrief finds the card filled mid-flight. New rule: format card and check space at the start.

Activity. Hold the debrief and write the plan.

Standard to meet. Write a debrief with at least three changes and dates.

Common mistake. Skipping the debrief after a good flight.

Second worked example. A smooth flight still shows the card was nearly full; add a check step.

Check yourself. What one change will you make to your checklist?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The crowd

A client asks for a sunset shot over a street fair.

Question: What is your answer?

Model answer: Decline over the crowd. Offer a shot from a permitted position that keeps people away, after checking rules and getting permission.

Case 2. The wind

Forecast gusts rise from 6 to 11 m/s during your slot. Stop limit is 10 m/s.

Question: What do you do?

Model answer: Postpone or fly earlier within the limit; log the decision and the forecast.

Glossary

Failsafe
What the drone does if the signal or battery is lost.
Overlap
How much one picture repeats the next so a map can be built.
Line of sight
Keeping the drone in view of the pilot.
Ground reference point
A marked spot with a known position used to check accuracy.
Reserve
Battery kept unused for safety.
Spotter
A helper who watches the drone and the area.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: rules note, risk assessment, flight log, mission plan, processed map, error figure, client report and 90-day plan. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Marking rubric (Not yet, Meets, Exceeds)
CriterionNot yetMeetsExceeds
Legal and safeMissing, or claimed without a recordRules note and risk assessment completeRules checked with an adviser and controls tested
Flying disciplineMissing, or claimed without a recordChecklist and log used every timeLog shows improvement and no repeated fault
Mission planMissing, or claimed without a recordHeights, overlap and batteries correctPlan adjusted for wind and re-tested
Data qualityMissing, or claimed without a recordError figure calculatedGaps explained and fixed
ReportMissing, or claimed without a recordAnswer first with evidenceClient confirms it was useful

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the four forces on a drone.

Model answer: Lift, weight, thrust and drag. (Covered in module 1: How a drone flies. To pass that module: Label all main parts and explain failsafe behaviour in your own words.)

Q2. What must you check before every flight?

Model answer: Weather, battery, propellers, firmware, memory, radio and return point. (Covered in module 4: Pre-flight and flight skills. To pass that module: Log five drills and use the checklist each time.)

Q3. How is a risk score calculated?

Model answer: Chance times harm, each scored 1 to 5. (Covered in module 3: Risk assessment. To pass that module: Give every high risk a control and an owner.)

Q4. What battery reserve do we keep?

Model answer: At least 30%. (Covered in module 4: Pre-flight and flight skills. To pass that module: Log five drills and use the checklist each time.)

Q5. Why take ground reference points?

Model answer: To check how accurate the map is. (Covered in module 5: Mission planning and data capture. To pass that module: Plan has height, overlap, passes, batteries and a stop rule.)

Q6. When should you not fly?

Model answer: Near people, airports or banned zones, or when unsure or out of permitted conditions. (Covered in module 2: Rules, permission and insurance. To pass that module: Name the authority, limits and permissions required in the rules note.)

Q7. What is overlap for?

Model answer: So pictures can be joined into a map. (Covered in module 5: Mission planning and data capture. To pass that module: Plan has height, overlap, passes, batteries and a stop rule.)

Q8. Why blur people and plates?

Model answer: To protect privacy. (Covered in module 6: Process and check data. To pass that module: Calculate the error figure and list gaps with causes.)

Q9. What goes first in a report?

Model answer: The answer to the client question. (Covered in module 7: The client report. To pass that module: Answer first; every claim has a picture or number.)

Q10. Why log every flight?

Model answer: It shows hours, faults and patterns, and supports insurance and safety. (Covered in module 4: Pre-flight and flight skills. To pass that module: Log five drills and use the checklist each time.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Aviation rules differ widely by country and by zone: always read your authority's current rules before any flight. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

ladder L1 to L7

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Basics of flight

On the slide: L1 Basic · Basics of flight

Notes: Explain how a drone flies: lift, thrust, weight and drag, and the parts that control them. Notes: watch a flight and name the parts and forces. Standard: a labelled sketch with four forces and four parts.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Awareness of rules

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Awareness of rules

Notes: Describe registration, no-fly zones, height limits and privacy rules for your place. Notes: read your authority's public page together and note the rule numbers. Standard: five rules listed with their source and date.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Safe operator basics

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Safe operator basics

Notes: Run a pre-flight check, practise take-off, hover and landing drills, and plan a simple mission. Notes: a simulator is acceptable. Standard: checklist completed before every flight and three clean landings in a row.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Practitioner

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Practitioner

Notes: Fly or simulate a short mission with a map, a battery plan and a written risk assessment. Notes: record battery use and wind at start and end. Standard: mission flown inside the plan, landing with at least 25% battery, risks and controls listed.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Specialist mapping or inspection

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Specialist mapping or inspection

Notes: Plan, fly (or simulate) and report one drone mission for a real client need, with a written risk assessment. Notes: choose mapping, inspection or film; the client report states what was done, what was found and the limits. Standard: permission obtained, risk assessment signed, report delivered with images and limits.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Advanced operations lead

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Advanced operations lead

Notes: Plan multi-drone work with a safety system: roles, a flight log, an incident process and a maintenance schedule. Notes: add a briefing script and a check for each pilot's currency. Standard: every flight logged, incidents reviewed within a week and maintenance on schedule.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Super expert

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Super expert

Notes: Set safety method and teach teachers. Notes: review incidents yearly and publish lessons.
