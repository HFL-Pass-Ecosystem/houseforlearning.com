Information Technology Support Services

Run a pilot of information Technology Support Services in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small information Technology Support Services offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know the parts of a computer and a network 6 hUnderstand

IT support starts with knowing what is inside a computer and how devices join a network.

  • Name the main parts: processor, memory, storage, screen, battery and ports.
  • Explain what the operating system does and how it differs from an app.
  • Explain what a router, Wi-Fi and an internet provider each do.
  • Name common devices: laptop, phone, printer and tablet.
  • Say the difference between hardware, software and settings.
  • Compare Wi-Fi and a cable connection for speed and reliability.
  • Find the model, serial number and operating system version of a device.
Worked example. A user says 'the internet is down'. You ask: is it one device or all? If only one device fails, the problem is likely in that device, not the router. If all 5 devices fail, the router or the internet provider is the more likely cause.

Activity. Draw a home network with five devices and label each part's job.

Standard to meet. Task: draw and label a network. Condition: one real or practice network. Standard: every device and link is named and its job is stated in one sentence.

Module 2 Listen, ask and write the problem down 6 hApply

Most fixes start with a clear description. Good support asks short questions and writes the answers.

  • Ask: what did you try to do, what happened, what did you expect, when did it start?
  • Ask if anything changed recently, such as an update or a new device.
  • Write a ticket with date, user, device, problem and steps tried.
  • Use plain words and avoid blaming the user.
  • Tell the user what you will do next and how long it may take.
  • Paraphrase the problem back to the user to confirm you understood.
  • Rank tickets by urgency and impact: one user blocked, or a whole office blocked.
Worked example. Ticket: 'Printer not printing. Started this morning after an update. Other computers print fine. Tried restarting the printer.' The note already points to a computer setting. If three tickets arrive, the office-wide outage is handled first, then the single-user printer, then the how-to question.

Activity. Role-play three support calls and write a ticket for each.

Standard to meet. Task: write three tickets. Condition: practice calls with a partner. Standard: each ticket has the five facts, plain words and a next step.

Module 3 Fix common problems step by step 10 hApply

Use a fixed order: check the simple things, change one thing at a time, and test.

  • Check power, cables, Wi-Fi and storage space before deeper steps.
  • Restart the device and the app; many faults clear.
  • Change one thing at a time and write it down.
  • Check for pending updates and look at free disk space, because a nearly full disk makes a device slow.
  • Decide when to stop and escalate to a person with more skill.
  • Recreate the fault to confirm it, then confirm the fix afterwards.
  • Write the cause and fix so the next person can repeat it.
Worked example. A laptop is slow. The disk is 98% full: 490 GB used of 500 GB. You clear 60 GB of old downloads with the user's consent, leaving 430 GB used, or 86%. Speed returns, and you note the cause.

Activity. Fix five practice faults on a spare device and record each step.

Standard to meet. Task: fix five faults. Condition: a spare or practice device. Standard: each fix has the steps, the test that proves it and the cause.

Module 4 Keep data and accounts safe 8 hEvaluate

Support staff see private data. Keep accounts safe and handle data with care.

  • Explain strong passwords, a password manager and two-step sign-in.
  • Spot a phishing message: odd sender, urgent tone, strange link, request for a password.
  • Never ask users to tell you their password; reset it with them instead.
  • Explain that federal and provincial privacy laws apply to personal information, and that some provinces, such as Quebec, have their own private-sector laws; check which law applies.
  • Report a suspected data leak to the responsible person quickly; check the current rule on breach reporting with the privacy regulator.
  • Explain why shared passwords are risky and how to give each person their own login.
  • Apply the least-access rule: give each person only the access the job needs.
Worked example. A user gets an email saying 'your account will close in one hour'. You check the link without opening it and see the address is not the real one. You advise deleting it and reporting it, and you help the user turn on two-step sign-in.

Activity. Build a one-page safe-use guide for non-experts and a five-line phishing checklist.

Standard to meet. Task: write a safety guide. Condition: plain words, one page. Standard: covers passwords, two-step sign-in, phishing and who to tell when something goes wrong.

Module 5 Back up, update and set up devices 8 hApply

Setup and care prevent most problems. Teach users the habits too.

  • Set up a backup that runs on a schedule, and test a restore at least once.
  • Turn on automatic updates where safe and plan restarts at quiet times.
  • Set up a new user: account, access level, email, printer and Wi-Fi.
  • Remove access when a person leaves.
  • Keep a list of devices, who uses them and when they were bought.
  • Apply the 3-2-1 idea: three copies, two kinds of storage, one copy kept away from the building.
  • Label cables, chargers and devices so setup is quick.
Worked example. A small office has no backup. You set a nightly backup to external storage and a copy to a second place. You then restore one file to prove it works. That makes 3 copies: the working file, the external copy and the off-site copy.

Activity. Set up a backup and restore one file on a practice device.

Standard to meet. Task: back up and restore. Condition: a practice device. Standard: the backup runs on schedule, one restore works and a written note says where copies are.

Module 6 Price support and write simple terms 6 hCreate

A support business sells time and trust. Price covers your time, travel and tools.

  • Choose a price model: per hour, per visit or a monthly plan.
  • Count unpaid time: travel, tickets, learning and admin.
  • Write terms: what is covered, response time, payment date and what is out of scope.
  • Ask a licensed adviser about insurance and sales tax, and check the current rule before you invoice.
  • Offer a plan for a small group with a fixed monthly fee.
  • Calculate the margin percentage: margin divided by price.
  • Explain why a fixed fee needs a limit on hours or a clear scope.
Worked example. A monthly plan for a five-person office is 250 CAD. You expect 3 hours a month at 40 CAD cost per hour = 120. Margin is 130 CAD, about 52%, before overhead. At 5 hours the cost is 200 and the margin falls to 50 CAD, or 20%, so the terms must say what happens above 3 hours (teaching numbers only).

Activity. Build a price sheet and a one-page terms document.

Standard to meet. Task: price and write terms. Condition: a spreadsheet. Standard: formulas show cost and margin and the terms list scope, response time and exclusions.

Module 7 Work with small businesses and community 6 hEvaluate

Many clients are households, seniors and small shops. Help them stay safe and keep things simple.

  • Explain things in short steps and let users try it themselves.
  • Write a one-page how-to with screenshots for common jobs.
  • Respect accessibility needs such as larger text or a screen reader.
  • Offer help in English, French or another language you can use well.
  • Keep a log of all visits for later review.
  • Make a large-print version of a how-to page for users who find small text hard to read.
  • Slow down, leave pauses and let the user type and click on their own.
Worked example. A senior wants video calls. You set up one icon on the screen, write a three-step card and practise twice with her until she can start the call alone. The log notes that she needed two tries, and the card now has a bigger picture.

Activity. Create a one-page how-to for a common task and test it with a real user.

Standard to meet. Task: write a how-to. Condition: one real user. Standard: the user completes the task alone using only the page.

Module 8 Run an IT support service for real 8 hCreate

Now run it: track tickets, measure quality and improve.

  • Track tickets: opened, closed, time to first reply and time to fix.
  • Spot repeat problems and fix the cause once.
  • Keep a runbook for the three most likely faults.
  • Set a review each month with the client.
  • Write a 12-month plan with milestones and a budget.
  • Calculate the share of tickets by cause to find the biggest one.
  • Check the time to first reply against a target, such as one working day.
Worked example. Over 30 days a service closes 40 tickets. Eleven are 'password reset', which is 11 / 40 = 27.5%. A short self-help page cuts these to four the next month, a drop of 7 tickets.

Activity. Run a 60-day pilot with real or practice clients and write the review.

Standard to meet. Task: run a support pilot. Condition: 60 days. Standard: tickets are logged, repeat causes are found and the review gives decisions with evidence.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
