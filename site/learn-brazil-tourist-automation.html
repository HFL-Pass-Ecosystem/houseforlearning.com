Tourist Automation

Eight modules take you from the first customer to a running, measured service in tourist automation.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan, start and run tourist automation in Sao Paulo: serve a visitor and a local operator, deliver a booking, guide or visit tool, keep records, and show a working month of numbers.

Condition

About 8 weeks part-time. Own phone or computer, real customers or a real practice site, a peer and a mentor for the final review. Local rules and licences checked against official sources.

Standard

Every module standard is met; all claims are true and backed by records; peers and a mentor mark the evidence 'Meets' or better.

Who it is for: students, graduates, career changers, owners, workers and returners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, tracker, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know your customer and market 4 hAnalyse

Every sound business in tourist automation begins with one clear customer and one clear problem. In Sao Paulo and the wider state, demand is real but crowded, so a small, well-described customer group wins before a broad one.

  • In tourist automation: Visitors want times, prices and directions at the moment of need; local operators want fuller schedules.
  • Write the customer in one sentence: a visitor and a local operator. Add the place, the moment of need and what they use today.
  • List five rivals or substitutes within reach. For each, record price, strength, weakness and how people reach it.
  • Interview at least eight real customers with open questions. Record exact words, not your summary.
  • Count the market in units: how many bookings could one area buy in a month? Use two public sources and your own count, then take the lower figure.
  • Choose one first segment and say why the others wait.
Worked example. Weak: 'Everyone needs a booking, guide or visit tool.' Strong: 'Our first customer is a visitor and a local operator within 8 km of the centre, who today pays R$ 66 per booking and complains about delay. We target 20 bookings a month by month three.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: ten customers are interviewed for tourist automation. Seven say price, two say speed, one says trust. Question: which first message do you write, and which interview record backs it? Model answer: lead with the booking price and speed promise, keep trust proof as the second line, and cite the seven price answers by date.

Activity. Write the one-sentence customer, a five-row rival table and eight interview records. Share them with a peer for a challenge round.

Standard to meet. Customer is specific; rival table has prices; every claim traces to an interview or a source.

Module 2 Rules, registration and data duties in Brazil 4 hUnderstand

Brazil asks every trader to be visible and accountable. Know which register, tax regime and sector rules apply before the first sale, and check each against the current official text.

  • In tourist automation: Operators need correct registration; listings must show true prices and cancellation rules.
  • Choose a legal form: micro-entrepreneur (MEI) for the smallest traders, or a company registered with a CNPJ. Check the current limits and activity lists on the official portal.
  • Learn the tax regime that fits your size and write down which taxes you will collect and pay, and when.
  • Find the sector rules for tourist automation: licences, inspections, professional registers. List each with the office that issues it and the renewal date.
  • Personal data of customers falls under Brazil's data protection law (LGPD). Collect only what you need, say why, keep it safe, and name who answers questions.
  • Consumer law gives buyers clear rights on information, returns and complaints. Put your terms in plain Portuguese.
Worked example. Checklist for a new tourist automation trader: 1) legal form and CNPJ or MEI; 2) city operating licence; 3) sector licence or register; 4) tax set-up; 5) privacy notice; 6) written terms. Each has an owner and a date.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a neighbour says registration can wait until the first R$ 300 of sales. Question: what do you do? Model answer: do not delay; write the compliance table first, confirm the register, tax and sector licence with the official office, and start only where the table shows no open blocker.

Activity. Build your own six-line compliance table for your first location. Mark what is done, what is pending and who confirms it.

Standard to meet. Every line cites an official source; nothing is assumed; pending items have dates. Local law always wins over this course.

Module 3 Design the offer 4 hCreate

An offer states what the customer gets, how well, by when and at what price. For a booking, guide or visit tool, the offer must make the quality visible and the next step easy.

  • In tourist automation: Design the tool around one task: find, book and pay in three screens.
  • Describe the core offer in three lines: what is delivered (a booking, guide or visit tool), the standard it meets, and the time it takes.
  • Add one proof for each promise: a sample, a test result, a guarantee or a customer record.
  • Price from cost upward and from customer value downward, then pick a price inside both.
  • Write a simple one-page terms sheet: what is included, what is extra, how to cancel.
  • Create two options at most, so choice stays easy.
Worked example. Offer: 'A booking, guide or visit tool, delivered within agreed time, checked against a written standard, price R$ 60 per booking. Proof: photo record and sign-off by the customer. Cancel any time before work begins.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a customer asks for a discount from R$ 60 to R$ 54 per booking. Question: decide with numbers. Model answer: contribution falls from R$ 38 to R$ 32 per booking; offer an extra of value (for example faster service) before cutting price, and accept only if the lower contribution still clears break-even.

Longer notes. Visit a small guesthouse or tour guide and list every message sent twice: booking confirmation, directions to the door, check-in code, Wi-Fi password, late checkout rule, review request. Count each per week and automate the three that eat the most time, using a form plus scheduled messages before any costly platform. Compare two tools by monthly price, language support and how easily the owner edits a text alone. Mark what stays with a person: complaints, allergies, special celebrations. Show the owner hours saved and bookings no longer missed overnight. Protect guest data and check the current official text of data protection rules.

Extra worked example. A small guesthouse spends 10 hours a week answering the same booking questions. Staff time costs R$ 25 per hour, so that is R$ 250 per week. Automated replies cut the work by 60 percent, saving 6 hours, or R$ 150 per week. Over 4 weeks you save R$ 600. The tool costs R$ 120 per month, so the net gain is R$ 480 per month.

Activity. Write the offer card, the proof list and the terms sheet. Test them with three customers and note every point where they hesitated.

Standard to meet. Promise, proof and price are consistent; terms fit one page; three customer reactions are recorded.

Module 4 Run the work step by step 5 hApply

Good operations make results repeatable. For tourist automation, write each step so a new person could follow it on their first day, and measure each step.

  • In tourist automation: Keep listings live: availability, price and opening hours checked daily.
  • Map the process from first contact to paid and closed. Typical stages: enquiry, quote, preparation, delivery of the booking, check, payment, follow-up.
  • Name the key resource at each stage: an accurate live listing. Note what happens if it is missing.
  • Write a one-page standard procedure per stage: who, what, tool, time, record.
  • Choose three measures: speed, quality (errors per 100) and customer rating. Review them every week.
  • Fix the cause of a defect, not the symptom. Ask 'why' five times and record the answer.
Worked example. Stage record: 'Prepare. Owner: lead. Time: 30 min. Tool: checklist. Record: signed sheet. Defect found on 3 of 40 bookings last week; cause: missing check on an accurate live listing; fix: add check at start, review next Friday.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: of 500 bookings last month, 30 had a defect traced to an accurate live listing. Question: what is the defect rate and what is your first action? Model answer: about 6 defects per 100; stop the cause (add a check at the start), correct affected customers, log the cause, and re-measure next week.

Activity. Draw the process map and write procedures for the two riskiest stages. Run each once and time it.

Standard to meet. Procedures are followable by a stranger; measures have weekly numbers; one defect is traced to a root cause.

Module 5 Safety, quality and risk 4 hEvaluate

The most serious risk in this field is a wrong time, price or place shown. A calm, written risk routine protects people first and the business second.

  • In tourist automation: Avoid sending a visitor to a closed or unsafe place; verify listings and report errors fast.
  • List ten things that could go wrong. Score each for how likely (1 to 5) and how serious (1 to 5). Multiply and sort.
  • For the top five, choose a control: remove the hazard, replace it, guard it, train people, or use protective gear, in that order of preference.
  • Write who does what in an emergency, and rehearse it once a quarter.
  • Keep an incident and near-miss log. Near-misses are free lessons.
  • Check that insurance and legal duties match the risk you actually carry.
Worked example. Risk row: 'Hazard: a wrong time, price or place shown. Likelihood 3, severity 5, score 15. Control: written check before each booking, trained staff, log sheet. Owner: site lead. Review monthly.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a near-miss occurs: a wrong time, price or place shown nearly happened but no one was hurt. Question: what do you do in the next 24 hours? Model answer: make safe, record the event, ask the five whys, add or tighten one control, brief the team, and set a review date; near-misses count as lessons, not blame.

Activity. Build a ten-row risk register and a one-page emergency plan. Run a ten-minute drill with a peer and note two improvements.

Standard to meet. Top risks have controls ranked by preference; drill done; log started. Where a regulation applies, follow the official rule.

Module 6 People, skills and service 4 hApply

People deliver the promise. Define roles, train to a standard and give feedback in the open.

  • In tourist automation: Train local partners to update listings and answer messages the same day.
  • Write a role card for each person: purpose, five tasks, the standard for each, and who to ask for help.
  • Train by showing, doing with support, then doing alone with a check. Record sign-off per task.
  • Set service rules for answering customers: reply times, tone, how to apologise and fix.
  • Hold a ten-minute team check each day: yesterday's problem, today's plan, one risk.
  • Build a growth path: what a new person can do in week 1, month 3 and month 12.
Worked example. Training record: 'Task: handle a complaint. Shown 2 May; done with support 4 May; done alone 8 May, checked: apology, fix, follow-up call. Signed by lead.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a new hire completes training but a customer complaint shows a skipped step. Question: whose gap is it? Model answer: first the system: check the role card, the training sign-off and the checklist; then coach the person and fix the document so the step cannot be skipped.

Longer notes. Give every automated message a named owner and a daily inbox check at nine in the morning. Ask each staff member to play the guest on a phone, booking a room in Portuguese and then in English, and to write down every confusing step. Rehearse the hand-over line: a calm sentence that tells the guest a person will reply within the hour. Read ten real conversations every Friday and mark where the guest asked again. Rewrite any reply with a clear local touch, like naming the bakery on the corner. Nominate a backup for holidays and write the steps so a newcomer can follow them.

Extra worked example. You read 40 guest conversations in a week and find 6 where the automatic reply missed the question, so the miss rate is 6 / 40 = 15 percent. Each miss costs about 20 minutes of staff time, worth R$ 8, so the weekly cost is 6 x R$ 8 = R$ 48. After rewriting four replies, misses fall to 2, costing R$ 16. You save R$ 32 per week, about R$ 128 per month.

Activity. Write two role cards and a training plan for one new hire. Role-play a hard customer conversation twice and review.

Standard to meet. Role cards have measurable standards; training shows sign-off steps; service rules are written in plain words.

Module 7 Money: cost, price and break-even 5 hAnalyse

A business lives or dies on a few numbers. Know your contribution per unit, your fixed costs and the volume you need.

  • In tourist automation: Earn per booking; track commission after payment fees and refunds.
  • Contribution per booking = price minus variable cost. Fixed costs are paid whether you sell or not.
  • Break-even units = fixed cost divided by contribution. Plan to pass it by a safe margin.
  • Make a 12-month cash plan. Cash timing matters as much as profit: customers may pay late, suppliers may want money first.
  • Keep money for business and money for the owner apart.
  • Test the plan: what if sales fall by a quarter, or cost rises by a tenth?
Worked example. Worked example, all figures in reais: price R$ 60, variable cost R$ 22, contribution R$ 38 per booking. Fixed cost R$ 12.000 a month. Break-even = R$ 12.000 / R$ 38 = 316 bookings a month. At 500 bookings a month, profit = R$ 38 x 500 - R$ 12.000 = R$ 7.000. These are teaching figures; use your own.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: sales fall to 375 bookings a month. Question: what is the monthly result? Model answer: R$ 38 x 375 - R$ 12.000 = R$ 2.250; compare with break-even of 316 bookings and decide whether to cut fixed cost or raise volume first. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build your own table: price, variable cost, contribution, fixed cost, break-even and a downside case at 75% of sales. Show it to a peer or mentor.

Standard to meet. Arithmetic is correct; every figure has a source or is marked as an assumption; a downside case is shown.

Module 8 Grow, measure and renew 4 hEvaluate

Growth should follow proof. Measure, learn, then add; and keep returning to the customer.

  • In tourist automation: Grow with local content and honest reviews; remove poor performers.
  • Pick one growth lever at a time: new customers, a higher repeat rate, a better price, or a new offer.
  • Set one measurable target for 90 days, such as 'raise repeat customers from 20% to 30%'.
  • Ask each customer how they found you, and record it. Spend more where the record shows results.
  • Review monthly: numbers, defects, complaints, cash. Write three decisions each time.
  • Plan the renewal of licences, equipment and skills a year ahead.
Worked example. 90-day plan: 'Lever: repeat customers. Action: follow-up call after every booking. Target: repeat rate from 20% to 30%. Check: weekly count. Review date: day 45 and day 90.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: you can add 100 bookings a month by one of two levers: a follow-up call to past customers or a 6-reais price rise to R$ 66. Question: how do you choose? Model answer: test the cheaper reversible lever first (the call), measure for 30 days, and only then try the price change, so you can tell which one worked.

Longer notes. Follow the guest from first question to review and count five points: replies sent within an hour, bookings per hundred inquiries, repeat guests, review score and the month with the emptiest rooms. Change one thing at a time, such as a faster first reply or a price list with photos, and keep it for a full month before judging. Send a thank-you note after checkout with a small perk that costs you little, such as a late coffee. Prepare a rainy-season offer for low months. Keep only the automations that raise bookings. Never promise full occupancy to the owner.

Extra worked example. You receive 200 inquiries per month and 30 book, so conversion is 30 / 200 = 15 percent. A faster automatic reply lifts it to 18 percent, which is 36 bookings, or 6 more. An average stay brings R$ 450, so the gain is 6 x R$ 450 = R$ 2.700 per month. After the tool cost of R$ 120, the net gain is R$ 2.580.

Activity. Write a 90-day plan with one target, three actions and a review date. Present it in five minutes and take questions.

Standard to meet. Target is measurable and dated; actions are small enough to start this week; review date is in the calendar.

case study

A case to work through

Situation. A small walking-tour company in the historic center of São Paulo takes bookings by messages, and the owner answers them by hand after work. Last month she received 140 requests, answered 95 within a day, and lost the rest. A tour costs the customer R$ 120, and each guide earns R$ 60 per tour. She is thinking of an automatic reply and booking form, but she worries it will confuse visitors who ask about accessibility, language, or rain. Two bookings were also doubled on the same Saturday morning because she tracked them on paper. She has a simple budget of R$ 400 for tools and wants a plan that keeps a human in charge of hard questions.

Task. Say what you would do first, next and last, and what record you would keep.

Model answer. First, list the 10 questions asked most often, such as price, meeting point, duration, language, and rain. Next, write a clear answer for each and set up an automatic reply that gives them and a booking link. Then build a single shared calendar so one slot cannot be sold twice, and test it with five friends before opening it. After that, add a rule that sends any question about accessibility, health, or a group over 12 people to the owner. Last, review weekly how many requests were answered in an hour and how many needed a person. Records: the question list, the booking log, the reply times, and the complaints. Re-compute: 45 lost requests at an assumed one in three booking is 15 tours, worth R$ 1.800 in sales and R$ 900 after paying guides. Standard: every request receives an answer within one hour, and no slot is booked twice. Respect visitor privacy and check the current official text on personal data.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why write the customer in one sentence?

Model answer: It makes the offer, the price and the message specific; you can test it with real people.

Q2. Name the two Brazilian registers a small trader may choose between.

Model answer: MEI for micro-entrepreneurs, or a company with a CNPJ (check current limits).

Q3. Which Brazilian law governs customer personal data?

Model answer: LGPD, the general data protection law.

Q4. What three parts does an offer state?

Model answer: What is delivered, to what standard and by when, and at what price, with proof.

Q5. Why write a procedure per stage?

Model answer: So any trained person can repeat it and so each stage can be measured.

Q6. What is the main risk named for tourist automation in this course, and what is the first control to try?

Model answer: A wrong time, price or place shown. Try to remove or replace the hazard before guarding, training or protective gear.

Q7. Order the controls from best to last.

Model answer: Remove, replace, guard, train, protect with gear.

Q8. Define contribution per unit.

Model answer: Price minus variable cost per unit.

Q9. Using the worked example, what is break-even in bookings?

Model answer: 316 bookings a month (fixed R$ 12.000 / contribution R$ 38, rounded up).

Q10. Why change only one growth lever at a time?

Model answer: So you can tell which change caused the result.

levels L1 to L7

Where this sits on the ladder

  1. L1 Basic. Name the main parts of tourist automation and the words used. Notes: for a first-time learner, no experience needed.
  2. L2 Awareness. Describe how tourist automation works for a customer and the rules that apply. Notes: suits school leavers and career changers.
  3. L3 Foundation. Perform the core tasks under supervision to a written standard. Notes: needs L2 knowledge.
  4. L4 Practitioner. Run the whole service or process alone to standard. Notes: this course is pitched here.
  5. L5 Specialist. Diagnose faults and improve a process with data. Notes: needs a year of practice.
  6. L6 Advanced. Design systems, lead a team and manage risk and budgets. Notes: for managers and owners.
  7. L7 Super expert. Set standards, advise others and shape the sector. Notes: for recognised leaders and examiners.
