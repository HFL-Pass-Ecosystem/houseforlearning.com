CPD · Business professionals

Writing a one-page business case

A short, honest case that a decision-maker can act on.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 PractitionerAbout 3 hours (180 minutes) of CPDSelf-study plus role-playBusiness professionals

A short, honest case that a decision-maker can act on.

  • State the problem, given a scenario, in two sentences with a number.
  • Compare three options, given data, with cost, benefit and risk for each.
  • Recommend one option, given the comparison, with a reason and a first step.
  • Edit the page, given a checklist, to one page with no jargon.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Write a one-page business case that states the problem in two lines, compares three options including do nothing, cites sources, recommends one option with its main risk and gives a dated next step.

Condition

Given a scenario, a data sheet, a one-page layout and a checklist, working in a three-hour supervised session.

Standard

Produce one page with every part present, each number sourced or marked as an estimate, a clear reason for the recommendation, the main risk with a plan, and no jargon.

These courses teach process, communication and recording. They give no clinical, legal, tax, financial or engineering sign-off. A named subject expert must review every line, and you must check your own professional body's current CPD rules, before you rely on a certificate.

sessions

8 modules

Module 1 The one-page shape 15 min
  • Six parts on one page
  • A decision-maker reads it in minutes
  • Cut what does not help the decision
Worked example. One-page shape: Problem (2 lines); options A, B and do nothing; costs and benefits with sources; choice and why; main risk and plan; next step with date. If it runs to two pages, cut.

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "the one-page shape": state the problem, given a scenario, in two sentences with a number.

Standard to meet. Describe the one-page shape of a business case, listing the parts it must contain and keeping it to a single page.

Module 2 Problem and options 25 min
  • Two lines with a number
  • Always include do nothing
  • Name each option clearly
Worked example. Problem: "One delivery van runs full and about 10 orders a week are late or lost. This costs an estimated 15,000 a year." Options: A buy a second van, B hire a van by the day, C do nothing (baseline).

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "problem and options": compare three options, given data, with cost, benefit and risk for each.

Standard to meet. State a problem in two lines and set out the options, including a do-nothing option as the baseline for comparison.

Module 3 Costs and benefits with sources 25 min
  • List cost and benefit per option
  • Cite the source of each number
  • Mark guesses as estimates
Worked example. Option A (amounts in the local currency): buy 24,000 (Quote Q1); running 6,000 a year (estimate from current van); extra income 10 orders a week x 30 each x 50 weeks = 15,000 (estimate). Net 9,000 a year. Payback 24,000 / 9,000 = 2.7 years.

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "costs and benefits with sources": recommend one option, given the comparison, with a reason and a first step.

Standard to meet. List costs and benefits for each option and cite where each number came from, marking any guess as an estimate.

Module 4 Recommendation and main risk 20 min
  • Reason in one line
  • Most likely or most harmful risk
  • Dated next step
Worked example. Recommend A: net 9,000 a year, payback 2.7 years. Main risk: demand halves to 5 orders a week: income 7,500, net 1,500, payback 16 years. Plan: review after 6 months. Next step: manager decides by 30 June.

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "recommendation and main risk": edit the page, given a checklist, to one page with no jargon.

Standard to meet. Recommend a course of action with the reason for it, and state the main risk with a plan to reduce it, plus a dated next step.

Module 5 Practice and feedback 10 min
  • Write from a case
  • Read the feedback list
  • Correct the gap
Worked example. Draft had one source missing: the 6,000 running cost had none. Feedback: add "estimate from last year's running costs". Redraft has 6 of 6 parts and a source for every number.

Practise: Run one full practice round for "writing a one-page business case" on a case card or template: work through each outcome in turn (state the problem; compare three options; recommend one option; edit the page).

Standard to meet. Write a practice page on a case such as a second delivery van, then use the feedback to correct any missing part.

Module 6 Check 5 min
  • Tick each point
  • Fix before passing on
  • One page only
Worked example. Check list: parts present, sources shown, risk named with plan, one page, no jargon. Pass is 5 of 5 before the page goes on.

Practise: Take the end check for "writing a one-page business case": show that you can edit the page, given a checklist, to one page with no jargon, and meet every outcome standard above.

Standard to meet. Check a finished page against the list of parts, sources, risk and length, then correct any gap before it is passed on.

Module 7 Second case: replace 12 laptops now or later 45 min
  • Work case card 2
  • Compare now, later and do nothing
  • Write the page
Worked example. Case card 2: 12 old laptops. Now: 12 x 800 = 9,600 (Quote L1). In a year: repairs 12 x 120 = 1,440 this year (estimate), then 9,600 next year (estimate), total 11,040 over two years. Waiting costs 1,440 more but keeps 9,600 in cash for a year. Do nothing: repairs continue and failures cost staff time (estimate).

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "second case: replace 12 laptops now or later": edit the page, given a checklist, to one page with no jargon. Use a new case this time (round 2).

Standard to meet. Write the one-page case for the 12 laptops, comparing replacing now, replacing in a year and doing nothing, with sources, a recommendation and the main risk.

Module 8 Teach-back and peer check 35 min
  • Present your page in two minutes
  • Peer checks the six parts
  • Answer one challenge question
Worked example. Teach-back: "The problem is 12 old laptops. Three options. I recommend replacing now because repairs would cost 1,440 more over the year. Main risk: cash is tight; plan: pay in two stages. Decision needed by the end of the month."

Practise: Practise with a case card or template for "teach-back and peer check": edit the page, given a checklist, to one page with no jargon. Use a new case this time (round 2).

Standard to meet. Present your finished page in two minutes and answer one challenge question so that a peer can confirm all six parts, the sources and the risk plan.

checks

10 check questions with model answers

Why include "do nothing"?

Model answer: It is the baseline option. (Covered in module 2: Problem and options. To pass that module: State a problem in two lines and set out the options, including a do-nothing option as the baseline for comparison.)

Cite what?

Model answer: Where each number came from. (Covered in module 3: Costs and benefits with sources. To pass that module: List costs and benefits for each option and cite where each number came from, marking any guess as an estimate.)

How long?

Model answer: One page. (Covered in module 1: The one-page shape. To pass that module: Describe the one-page shape of a business case, listing the parts it must contain and keeping it to a single page.)

Biggest risk to state?

Model answer: The one most likely or most harmful, with a plan. (Covered in module 4: Recommendation and main risk. To pass that module: Recommend a course of action with the reason for it, and state the main risk with a plan to reduce it, plus a dated next step.)

Why name sources?

Model answer: So the reader can check them. (Covered in module 3: Costs and benefits with sources. To pass that module: List costs and benefits for each option and cite where each number came from, marking any guess as an estimate.)

How many options?

Model answer: Usually three, including do nothing. (Covered in module 2: Problem and options. To pass that module: State a problem in two lines and set out the options, including a do-nothing option as the baseline for comparison.)

What belongs under risk?

Model answer: The biggest threat and how to reduce it. (Covered in module 4: Recommendation and main risk. To pass that module: Recommend a course of action with the reason for it, and state the main risk with a plan to reduce it, plus a dated next step.)

How long is the page?

Model answer: One page. (Covered in module 1: The one-page shape. To pass that module: Describe the one-page shape of a business case, listing the parts it must contain and keeping it to a single page.)

What if numbers are guesses?

Model answer: Mark them as estimates. (Covered in module 3: Costs and benefits with sources. To pass that module: List costs and benefits for each option and cite where each number came from, marking any guess as an estimate.)

Cost is 24,000 and the yearly net benefit is 9,000. What is the payback in years?

Model answer: 24,000 / 9,000 = about 2.7 years. (Covered in module 3: Costs and benefits with sources. To pass that module: List costs and benefits for each option and cite where each number came from, marking any guess as an estimate.)

where next

Ladder L1 to L7, slide by slide with notes

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: What is a business case. Speaker note: A business case is a short page that helps someone decide. Learners at this level can say that it states the problem, the choices and a recommendation, and that it should be short.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Awareness: The decision-maker. Speaker note: Show who reads a business case and what they need: the problem, the numbers, the choice and the risk. Learners know that a reader who cannot find the cost will say no.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Foundation: Fill a template. Speaker note: With a mentor, learners fill a one-page template from a simple case such as a second delivery van, writing the problem in two lines and listing three options with do nothing.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Practitioner: Write the page. Speaker note: This course sits here. The learner states the problem, compares three options with sourced numbers, recommends one with its main risk and a dated next step, and edits the page to one page.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Specialist: Sharper numbers. Speaker note: The specialist works with estimates and ranges, checks sources, tests what happens if numbers are wrong, such as demand halving, and builds comparison tables that others can reuse.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Advanced: Cases that win decisions. Speaker note: The advanced writer handles larger cases with several stakeholders, aligns the page with strategy, answers board challenges and tracks whether the decision delivered the benefit it promised.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Super expert: A decision system. Speaker note: The super expert designs how the organisation makes decisions: case templates, review levels, evidence rules and follow-up. They teach writers to make every page easy to check.

Your place

Use your own body's forms, units and currency. Local professional bodies set the rules for CPD hours; check them first.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Personal data: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Credit and levels: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Personal data: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Credit and levels: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Credit and levels: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

United States

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Credit and levels: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia
