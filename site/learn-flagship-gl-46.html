DNA Analysis (Awareness Level)

Understand what DNA tests can and cannot tell you, how samples are handled, and how to protect privacy and dignity. Awareness only.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L2 Awareness16:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain DNA, genes and the main kinds of tests in plain words.
  • Apply Read a sample report and say what it does and does not mean.
  • Analyse Spot overclaims in test marketing.
  • Evaluate Weigh benefits and risks of sharing genetic data.
  • Create Draft a plain-language consent explainer.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Produce a two-page plain-language explainer on DNA testing with a risk list and a consent checklist.

Condition

A computer, public information, two peers and a mentor. You will not handle real samples or interpret anyone's results.

Standard

Facts are correct and sourced; limits are stated; no diagnosis is offered; privacy risks are named; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Lab assistants, health communicators, teachers, curious adults. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 DNA in plain words 2 hUnderstand

DNA is the code inside almost every cell that carries instructions for how a body is built and runs. You do not need chemistry to understand the main idea.

  • DNA is made of four letters arranged in a very long order. Genes are sections that carry instructions.
  • You inherit about half your DNA from each parent, with small changes.
  • Most traits depend on many genes and on life, such as food, health and environment.
  • A test reads some parts of DNA, never the whole story of a person.
Worked example. Height is influenced by hundreds of genes and also by food and health in childhood. Two brothers with the same parents can differ by several centimetres.

Activity. Draw a one-page picture: DNA to genes to traits to environment, with plain labels.

Standard to meet. Picture is accurate and a non-expert can explain it back.

Module 2 Kinds of DNA tests 2 hUnderstand

"A DNA test" can mean very different things, with different uses and limits.

  • Ancestry tests estimate where your ancestors may have lived, using comparison groups.
  • Health-risk tests report chances of some conditions; carrier tests show if you could pass a condition on.
  • Paternity and relationship tests compare DNA between people; forensic tests compare crime-scene samples.
  • Medical diagnosis belongs with a clinician and an accredited lab.
Worked example. A carrier test reports that a couple each carry a change linked to a blood condition. This does not mean any child will be affected; it means that, for a typical recessive condition, the chance is about one in four per pregnancy, and a genetic counsellor explains the options.

Activity. Make a table of five test kinds with purpose, what it cannot tell you, and who should explain results.

Standard to meet. Each row states a limit and a right person to explain.

Module 3 From sample to result 2 hAnalyse

Mistakes can enter at every step. Quality systems exist to catch them.

  • Steps: consent, collect, label, store, extract, read, check, report.
  • Chain of custody records who held the sample and when.
  • Errors: mislabelling, contamination, poor storage, misreading.
  • Good labs use barcodes, two-person checks and repeat tests.
Worked example. Two tubes are swapped at the front desk. A barcode scan at the lab finds a mismatch with the form and stops the run, avoiding a wrong report.

Activity. Map the sample steps and write one quality check for each step.

Standard to meet. Every step has a check and an owner.

Module 4 Reading a result safely 2 hAnalyse

Numbers like "twice the risk" sound alarming but need context.

  • Relative risk compares groups; absolute risk is the real chance for a person.
  • Gene changes whose meaning is unclear are common and are not proof of disease.
  • Results can be wrong or incomplete; confirm important findings in an accredited lab.
  • Only a genetic counsellor or clinician explains health results.
Worked example. A report says a 50% higher chance of a condition. The absolute chance changes from 2 in 100 to 3 in 100. A counsellor explains that lifestyle and screening matter more than fear.

Activity. Explain a sample result (given by your mentor) to a peer in under two minutes using absolute numbers.

Standard to meet. Peer repeats the meaning correctly and does not panic.

Module 5 Marketing and overclaims 2 hEvaluate

Some adverts promise a perfect diet, talent or career from your DNA. Most go beyond evidence.

  • Look for independent studies, regulator approval and clear limits.
  • Be wary of "personalised diet" or "talent" claims and of high prices.
  • Check whether samples are analysed in an accredited lab.
  • Read the privacy terms before sending a sample.
Worked example. An advert promises a diet plan from a saliva test with no study cited. The learner marks it "unproven" and notes the missing evidence.

Activity. Review two real adverts with a five-point checklist.

Standard to meet. Each advert has a scored list with reasons.

Module 6 Privacy and family impact 2 hEvaluate

Your DNA also describes your relatives. Sharing it can affect them.

  • Data may be sold, hacked, or requested by authorities or insurers in some places.
  • Findings can reveal unexpected relatives or risks for the family.
  • Consent must be informed and free, with the right to withdraw.
  • Never test a child or another adult without proper consent and guidance.
Worked example. A family agrees before testing that surprises will be shared only with a counsellor present. Two siblings opt out of learning some results.

Activity. Write a consent checklist with at least eight questions.

Standard to meet. Each question is clear and answerable yes or no.

Module 7 Ethics, fairness and access 2 hEvaluate

Genetic science can help or harm depending on how it is used.

  • No test should be forced on employees, students or insurance applicants.
  • Some groups have less data in reference sets, so results can be less accurate for them.
  • Respect cultural and faith views.
  • Ask who benefits, who pays and who is left out.
Worked example. A research project adds samples from under-represented communities with community agreement, improving accuracy for them.

Activity. Discuss two cases with peers and write a reasoned view on each.

Standard to meet. Peers reach a view with at least two reasons and one risk.

Module 8 Write a plain-language explainer 2 hCreate

Bring everything into a two-page guide that a curious non-expert can use.

  • Use short sentences and a glossary.
  • Include the limits, privacy risks and who to ask for help.
  • Test with one person aged under 25 and one over 55.
  • Date the guide and plan a yearly review.
Worked example. A reader says, "Now I know to ask a counsellor, not a website, about my result."

Activity. Finish and test the explainer.

Standard to meet. Both readers can list three limits of DNA tests.

Model answer: The instruction code in cells. (Covered in module 1: DNA in plain words. To pass that module: Picture is accurate and a non-expert can explain it back.)

Q2. Is a result destiny?

Model answer: No; it is a chance. (Covered in module 4: Reading a result safely. To pass that module: Peer repeats the meaning correctly and does not panic.)

Q3. Name two test kinds.

Model answer: Ancestry and carrier (or health risk, paternity). (Covered in module 2: Kinds of DNA tests. To pass that module: Each row states a limit and a right person to explain.)

Q4. Why chain of custody?

Model answer: To show who held the sample. (Covered in module 3: From sample to result. To pass that module: Every step has a check and an owner.)

Q5. Who explains health results?

Model answer: A genetic counsellor or clinician. (Covered in module 4: Reading a result safely. To pass that module: Peer repeats the meaning correctly and does not panic.)

Q6. Name one marketing red flag.

Model answer: A big promise with no evidence. (Covered in module 5: Marketing and overclaims. To pass that module: Each advert has a scored list with reasons.)

Q7. Why does DNA affect relatives?

Model answer: Relatives share genes. (Covered in module 6: Privacy and family impact. To pass that module: Each question is clear and answerable yes or no.)

Q8. Name a data risk.

Model answer: Sale, hacking or misuse by insurers. (Covered in module 6: Privacy and family impact. To pass that module: Each question is clear and answerable yes or no.)

Q9. Can employers force tests?

Model answer: They should not. (Covered in module 7: Ethics, fairness and access. To pass that module: Peers reach a view with at least two reasons and one risk.)

Q10. Why is diverse data needed?

ten locations and global

Your place

Genetic testing and data laws differ by place; some tests need a clinician.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.
