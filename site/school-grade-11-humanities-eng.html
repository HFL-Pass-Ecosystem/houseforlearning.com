Grade 11 Humanities: English

5 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 11 Humanities English, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 5 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

5 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Prose and poetry from world writers Weeks 1 to 9Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Prose and poetry from world writers', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: analyse theme and voice in two texts. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Prose and poetry from world writers' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: analyse theme and voice in two texts, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Simile: 'The moon is like a silver coin.' Metaphor: 'The classroom was a zoo.' Imagery: 'the dew-wet grass' appeals to sight and touch. Say what feeling each creates. Method: Model run for 'Prose and poetry from world writers'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Analyse theme and voice in two texts: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Reading: unseen passages and poems Weeks 28 to 32Apply

In this unit you work on 'Reading: unseen passages and poems', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: answer inference and evaluation questions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Reading: unseen passages and poems' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: answer inference and evaluation questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: cat, hat and mat rhyme. Make a couplet: 'The sun is high, a bird can fly.' High and fly rhyme. Method: Model run for 'Reading: unseen passages and poems'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read my own passage. "In the village archive, the clerk guarded the old registers as if they were relatives. Nobody asked why she never let anyone borrow them; the dust on the shelves said enough." Gist: she values the past and keeps it secure.
  2. Step 2, Locate evidence. "Guarded...as if they were relatives" is a simile that suggests deep attachment. "The dust on the shelves said enough" implies the registers are rarely touched. Reason: quote briefly, then comment.
  3. Step 3, Answer an inference question. "What does the clerk's behaviour suggest?" Model: "It suggests she feels responsible for the community's memory, since she treats the registers like family." Reason: an inference goes beyond what is stated.
  4. Step 4, Evaluate language effects. "How does personification in 'the dust said enough' affect the reader?" Model: "It gives silent objects a voice, which creates a quiet, sad mood and implies neglect."
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Weak: "The writer uses a simile to make it better." Right: "The simile compares registers to relatives, which suggests affection and emotional value." Always explain effect, not just a label.
  6. Step 6, Read a poem, my own: "The river keeps the town's old names, / and carries them to sea." Inference: time carries memories away. Evidence: "carries them to sea" suggests loss. Tone: wistful, reflective.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Passage: "Though the festival had ended, the lanterns still hung in the empty street." What mood is created and how? Model answer: "A lonely, nostalgic mood; 'still hung' and 'empty' contrast with the festival's past liveliness."

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Answer inference and evaluation questions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 5 Speaking and listening Weeks 33 to 36Apply

In this unit you work on 'Speaking and listening', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: deliver a structured speech and respond to questions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Listen first: your teacher or a recording says the new words or sentences from 'Speaking and listening' twice. You repeat them aloud.
  • Notice the pattern: find where the new sound, word form or sentence rule appears in a short text and mark it.
  • Use it: deliver a structured speech and respond to questions, first with help, then alone.
  • Check and fix: read your work aloud once more; fix one thing you hear is wrong.
Worked example. Solved: Retell in order with first, next, then, last. Ask: 'What happened next?' Reply politely: 'Thank you, I agree because...' Method: Model run for 'Speaking and listening'. Teacher text (3 lines about a daily activity). Step 1, read it aloud together. Step 2, mark every word or sentence that uses the new pattern. Step 3, a learner writes or says two new lines of their own that use the same pattern. Step 4, the learner reads them aloud and corrects one slip. What a good answer looks like: the pattern is used correctly in both lines, the lines make sense, and the learner can say which part is the pattern.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Plan with a clear thesis. Topic: "Local history should be taught in schools." Thesis: "Studying local history gives students identity and critical skills." Outline: hook, thesis, three points, counter-argument, conclusion.
  2. Step 2, Write a persuasive hook. "Do you know who built the bridge you cross every morning? Most of us don't, and that silence says something about what we teach." Reason: a question draws listeners in.
  3. Step 3, Develop a point with an example and a reason. "First, local history builds identity. For example, learning how our market began shows students that ordinary people shape history." Reason: example plus reason convinces.
  4. Step 4, Use rhetorical devices with purpose. Triad: "to remember, to question and to rebuild." Contrast: "not only the kings, but also the carpenters." Reason: devices create rhythm and emphasis, but they must support the argument.
  5. Step 5, Fix an error. Weak: "History is very important and everyone says so." Better: "History trains students to weigh evidence, and that skill applies to every subject." Remove vague claims; give a specific benefit.
  6. Step 6, Answer a question with a structure. Q: "Isn't world history more useful?" A: "World history matters, but local history makes it real; students connect national events to places they know." Reason: concede, then argue.
  7. Step 7, Your turn. Write a thesis and one counter-argument response for "Public libraries remain essential". Model answer: "Thesis: Libraries give equal access to knowledge." "Response: Online resources help, but not everyone has devices or connection."

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Deliver a structured speech and respond to questions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Letters, sounds and first reading

On the slide: L1 Basic · Letters, sounds and first reading

Notes: Match letters to sounds and read short words and simple sentences aloud. Notes: use the sounds s, a, t, p, i, n; learners blend them into sat, pin and tap, then read a three-word sentence and clap the syllables.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Sentences and simple writing

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Sentences and simple writing

Notes: Write a clear sentence with a capital letter and full stop, and read a short story with understanding. Notes: give a picture prompt; learners say the sentence first, write it, and check capital, spacing and full stop with a partner before reading it aloud.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Paragraphs, grammar and reading for meaning

Notes: Write a paragraph of five connected sentences and identify nouns, verbs and adjectives in a text. Notes: colour nouns blue and verbs red in a short passage; learners then write about their street, using at least two adjectives and one linking word.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Essays, speaking and responding to texts

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Essays, speaking and responding to texts

Notes: Write a 300-word structured essay and give a short talk, quoting evidence from a text to support a view. Notes: model Point, Evidence, Explain; learners write one paragraph about a character, quote one line, and explain what it shows, then speak it aloud.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Analysis, argument and style (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Analysis, argument and style

Notes: Analyse a writer's choices of language and structure and write a sustained argument of 600 words. Notes: take two openings of different short stories; learners compare tone, sentence length and imagery in a table, then argue which opening works better and why.

This course: Grade 11 Humanities English is at this level. Its units include Prose and poetry from world writers; Grammar and editing; Writing: speeches, articles and reports; Reading: unseen passages and poems and more.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Literary and linguistic study

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Literary and linguistic study

Notes: Evaluate texts through a critical lens, tracing how language changes across time and context in a researched essay. Notes: compare an older and a modern passage; learners list five differences in vocabulary and syntax and argue in 1,500 words what each reveals about its period.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research, editing and authorship

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research, editing and authorship

Notes: Produce an original piece of research or a polished long-form text, with a literature review and defended method. Notes: pick a narrow question about language in one genre; build a corpus of fifty samples, analyse patterns, write 5,000 words, and revise after external review.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico (Central America)

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
