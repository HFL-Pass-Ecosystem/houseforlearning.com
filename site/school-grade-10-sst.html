Grade 10: Social science

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4Secondary4 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Understand, Analyse
  • Understand Explain how nationalism shaped regions.
  • Understand Explain resource use and industry location.
  • Analyse Compare power sharing arrangements.
  • Understand Explain development, credit and trade.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 10 Social science, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 History: nationalism and the making of the modern world Weeks 1 to 9Understand

In this unit you work on 'History: nationalism and the making of the modern world', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: explain how nationalism shaped regions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'History: nationalism and the making of the modern world' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain how nationalism shaped regions. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Nationalism is a feeling of belonging to one nation, shared by people with a common history, language or land. Example: Germany was proclaimed a single empire on 18 January 1871 after many separate states joined. Write two things that make people feel part of a nation and one risk if nationalism turns into hostility to others. Method: Model run for 'History: nationalism and the making of the modern world'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Define the idea. Nationalism links people who share a sense of belonging to one nation. After the French Revolution of 1789, ideas of a nation of citizens, a flag and a common law spread; the Napoleonic Code of 1804 gave equal legal rules in lands France controlled.
  2. Step 2, Case one, Italy. Italy was split into states in the early 1800s. Cavour's diplomacy, Garibaldi's volunteers and Victor Emmanuel II of Sardinia-Piedmont brought most of it together in 1861, and Rome joined in 1870. From 1859 to 1870 is about 11 years of unification.
  3. Step 3, Case two, Germany. Prussia under Bismarck won wars with Denmark in 1864, Austria in 1866 and France in 1870 to 1871. The German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January 1871. Here unity came through war and a strong army, not mainly through popular uprisings.
  4. Step 4, Compare in a small table. Italy: patriotic volunteers, a kingdom, central role of one state. Germany: military victories, an empire, central role of Prussia. Both: a shared language and culture, growing middle-class support, and leaders who used nationalist feeling to build one state.
  5. Step 5, Write a model answer: 'Nationalism shaped Europe because it created new states, but it also produced conflict in the Balkans where many groups claimed the same land. This tension helped cause the First World War in 1914.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: how did nationalism grow in India after 1919? Model answer: 'Anger at Jallianwala Bagh and the Rowlatt Act led to the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920 to 1922, which united many groups against colonial rule.'

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Explain how nationalism shaped regions: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 2 Geography: resources, agriculture, minerals and industries Weeks 10 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'Geography: resources, agriculture, minerals and industries', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: explain resource use and industry location. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Geography: resources, agriculture, minerals and industries' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain resource use and industry location. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: A district has 50,000 hectares in all and 30,000 of them are sown, so the net sown share is 30,000 / 50,000 x 100 = 60 percent. Match soil to crop: alluvial soil suits wheat and rice, and black soil suits cotton. Give two ways a farmer can raise yield without harming the soil. Method: Model run for 'Geography: resources, agriculture, minerals and industries'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read land use data. A state has 20 million hectares in all, with a net sown area of 12 million. Net sown share = 12 / 20 x 100 = 60 percent. If forest is 3 million hectares, it is 15 percent, so the rest, 25 percent, is other uses.
  2. Step 2, Match soil to crop. Alluvial soil of the Ganga plain suits wheat and rice. Black soil of the Deccan holds moisture and suits cotton. Red soil needs fertiliser for millets. Tea grows on well-drained hill slopes in Assam and the Nilgiris.
  3. Step 3, Locate minerals. Iron ore is mined in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Karnataka. Coal is found mainly in Jharkhand, Odisha and Chhattisgarh. Bauxite, used for aluminium, is found in Odisha. These resources are unevenly spread, so transport matters.
  4. Step 4, Explain industry location. Jamshedpur grew as an iron and steel centre because iron ore, coal and water lie close by. Suppose a plant needs about 1.5 tonnes of iron ore and 0.8 tonne of coal for 1 tonne of steel (a rough figure that varies by plant); it will locate near the heavy raw materials to cut transport cost.
  5. Step 5, Write a model answer: 'Cotton textile mills grew in Mumbai because cotton from black soil lay nearby, a port allowed exports and the damp climate helped spinning. Industry can harm air and water, so rules for waste treatment are needed.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: explain why steel plants are placed near coal and iron ore. Model answer: 'These raw materials are heavy and lose weight in processing, so placing the plant near them cuts transport costs.'

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Explain resource use and industry location: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 3 Civics: power sharing, federalism and political parties Weeks 19 to 27Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Civics: power sharing, federalism and political parties', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: compare power sharing arrangements. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Civics: power sharing, federalism and political parties' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: compare power sharing arrangements. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Federalism shares power between the central government and state governments, with local government as a third tier. Sort three tasks (defence, local roads, schools) into the tier that decides them. Method: Model run for 'Civics: power sharing, federalism and political parties'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Compare Belgium's case. Roughly 59 per cent of people speak Dutch, 40 per cent French and 1 per cent German, which adds to 100. In the capital Brussels, about 80 per cent speak French and 20 per cent Dutch (check the current figure). The Dutch majority could have ruled, but that would anger French speakers, so Belgium shared power.
  2. Step 2, Describe Belgium's solution. The central government must have equal numbers of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers. Many powers go to regional governments, and Brussels has its own government with equal representation. There is also a community government for each language group, chosen by that group. No single group can dominate.
  3. Step 3, Describe Sri Lanka's case. About 74 per cent of people are Sinhala-speaking and about 18 per cent Tamil-speaking (check the current figure). In 1956 a law made Sinhala the only official language, and the majority's policy ignored Tamil needs in jobs and education. Distrust grew into a civil war that lasted until 2009, which is 26 years after 1983.
  4. Step 4, Compare reasons. Belgium chose accommodation, and Sri Lanka chose majority rule. Power sharing has a prudential reason: it reduces conflict and keeps the country united. It also has a moral reason: citizens have a right to be consulted on how they are governed, so power sharing is part of democracy itself.
  5. Step 5, Describe four forms of power sharing. Horizontal: legislature, executive and judiciary share power. Vertical: federalism among central, state and local governments, as in India, where the Constitution has a Union list, a State list and a Concurrent list (check the current number of subjects in each). Among social groups: reserved seats. Among parties and groups: coalitions.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: name the form of power sharing in each: (a) central, state and local governments, (b) courts checking the legislature, (c) a coalition of parties. Answer: (a) vertical, (b) horizontal, (c) among parties.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Compare power sharing arrangements: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 4 Economics: development, money, credit and globalisation Weeks 28 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Economics: development, money, credit and globalisation', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: explain development, credit and trade. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Economics: development, money, credit and globalisation' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain development, credit and trade. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Money is a medium of exchange; barter needs a double coincidence of wants. Credit: borrow 10,000 at 10 percent a year, interest is 1,000 a year. Method: Model run for 'Economics: development, money, credit and globalisation'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Explain development. Development means people live longer, healthier, better-educated lives, not only that a country earns more. Income per person is one measure: a country with a GDP of 3,000 billion and 1,500 million people has 3,000 billion divided by 1,500 million, which is 2,000 per person.
  2. Step 2, Explain why averages can hide gaps. If 2,000 is the average but a few people earn most of the income, many families live far below it. So economists also look at life expectancy, years of schooling and the share of people in poverty, not income alone.
  3. Step 3, Explain money and credit. Money works as a medium of exchange and a store of value. Credit is a loan repaid later with interest. Borrow 10,000 at 8 percent a year for 3 years: simple interest gives 12,400 back, but yearly compounding gives 10,000 times 1.08 cubed, which is 12,597.12.
  4. Step 4, Explain who gains and loses from credit. Formal credit from a bank at 8 percent can help a farmer buy seed and raise output. A moneylender charging far higher interest can trap a borrower in debt. Cheap, fair credit supports development; costly credit can undo it.
  5. Step 5, Explain globalisation and trade. Globalisation links markets through trade, investment and technology. If 1 unit of a foreign currency costs 80 of ours, a 25-unit foreign import costs 2,000 of ours. Trade gives choice and larger markets, but can hurt small producers who cannot match imported prices.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a loan of 5,000 at 10 percent compounded yearly for 2 years is taken by a small shop. How much is owed, and name one gain and one risk of imports. Answer: 5,000 times 1.1 squared is 6,050; gain is cheaper choice, risk is competition for local makers.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Explain development, credit and trade: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Community helpers

On the slide: L1 Basic · Community helpers

Notes: Name three community helpers and say what each does. Notes: use pictures of a nurse, a bus driver and a shopkeeper; learners match each helper to a tool and say one way they help the neighbourhood.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Needs, wants and rules

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Needs, wants and rules

Notes: Tell needs from wants and say why rules help a group. Notes: sort ten picture cards into needs and wants, then vote on one class rule and count the hands: 14 for and 6 against, so 20 voters took part.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Government and markets

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Government and markets

Notes: Describe local, regional and national government and how a simple market works. Notes: follow one pencil from maker to shop; if it costs 4 to make and sells for 6, the margin is 2, which is 50 per cent of cost. Learners name one service each level provides.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Rights, duties and prices (this course)

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Rights, duties and prices

Notes: Explain how supply and demand affect price and why rights come with duties. Notes: if a bad season cuts mangoes from 100 to 60 crates while buyers stay the same, price tends to rise; learners draw the two lines and predict direction before stating a duty tied to a right.

This course: Grade 10 Social science is at this level. Its units include History: nationalism and the making of the modern world; Geography: resources, agriculture, minerals and industries; Civics: power sharing, federalism and political parties; Economics: development, money, credit and globalisation.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Reading social data

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Reading social data

Notes: Interpret a table of census or survey data and compare how two institutions work. Notes: a population rising from 2.4 to 2.7 million in ten years grew by 12.5 per cent; learners give two possible reasons and one limit of the data.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Theory and research methods

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Theory and research methods

Notes: Apply core social science theories and methods to a social problem. Notes: compare a survey with interviews for one question. Practice: an economy of 600 billion with 50 million people is 12,000 per person, and learners explain what an average hides, such as unequal shares.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Causal research design

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Causal research design

Notes: Design and critique a causal study with ethics, consent and clear limits. Notes: in a before-and-after comparison, the treated group rises 8 points and the comparison group 3, so the estimated effect is 5 points if trends would have run parallel. Learners test that assumption.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
