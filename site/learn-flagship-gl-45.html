flagship course

Virtual Medical Appointment Systems

Set up and run an online appointment system for a clinic: booking, identity, consent, records, reminders and fall-back plans.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner28:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Describe the full patient journey from booking to follow-up.
  • Apply Configure booking slots, reminders and consent steps.
  • Analyse Find failure points such as no-shows and poor connections.
  • Evaluate Judge privacy and security of a tool.
  • Create Launch a pilot with a service level and review.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design and pilot a virtual appointment flow for one small clinic, with booking, identity check, consent, reminders, records and a fall-back for failed calls.

Condition

A practice clinic or case, a free booking tool, a spreadsheet, two peers and a clinic mentor. Real patient data is not used.

Standard

Flow works end to end in 10 mock appointments; ID and consent are recorded; failed-call fall-back works within 10 minutes; privacy checklist passes; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Clinic managers, receptionists, nurses, health-IT beginners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Map the patient journey 3 hUnderstand

A virtual appointment is more than a video call. It is a chain of steps, and any weak link loses the patient. Map every step before choosing a tool.

  • Steps: request, book, remind, join, check identity, consent, consult, record, follow up.
  • Mark who owns each step: patient, front desk, clinician, system.
  • Add a route for patients without a smartphone, such as a phone call at the same time.
  • Mark where things go wrong most often: booking confusion, late joining, poor sound, missing notes.
Worked example. A clinic maps the journey and finds that older patients drop out at "join". It adds a phone-in option and a helper call the day before. Attendance of patients over 65 rises from 61% to 79% in a month.

Activity. Draw a journey map for a small clinic with all nine steps, owners and one risk per step.

Standard to meet. All nine steps have an owner; at least one phone-only route is shown.

Module 2 Booking, slots and reminders 4 hApply

Good booking design cuts no-shows and protects clinician time.

  • Create slot types by clinician, length and visit type (new, follow-up, urgent) with buffer time between visits.
  • Send reminders the day before and one hour before, with a join link and a cancel or reschedule link.
  • Collect only the data needed to book.
  • Review no-show and late-cancel rates weekly.
Worked example. A clinic with 18% no-shows adds two reminders and a two-tap reschedule. No-shows fall to 9% in six weeks, giving back about 14 appointment slots per week.

Activity. Configure slots, reminders and a reschedule flow in a free booking tool and test with five mock patients.

Standard to meet. All five mock bookings complete; reminders arrive at the right times.

Module 3 Identity and consent 4 hApply

The clinician must know who is on the call and that the patient agrees to a virtual visit.

  • Check name and date of birth against the record; for children, check the guardian.
  • Ask consent for the video visit, for notes, and for any recording (recording only if the rules allow it).
  • Confirm the patient's location in case emergency help is needed.
  • Write the consent and location in the visit record.
Worked example. The clinician says: "Please confirm your name, birth date and where you are right now. Are you happy to have this visit by video? I will take notes. I will not record." The patient agrees and the note is saved.

Activity. Write a one-minute consent script and practise it twice.

Standard to meet. Script covers identity, location, consent to video, notes and recording rule.

Module 4 Technology, connection and fall-backs 4 hAnalyse

Connections fail. A plan turns a lost visit into a 2-minute delay.

  • Test link, camera, microphone and speed before the visit; offer a test call.
  • Fall-back order: reconnect, switch to audio, switch to phone, rebook within 48 hours.
  • Keep a help number for technical problems.
  • Log every failure with cause and time to fix.
Worked example. A test call shows the patient's microphone was blocked by the browser. A staff member guides the setting in two minutes, saving a missed visit.

Activity. Write a three-step fall-back and run it twice with a peer by cutting the connection.

Standard to meet. Contact is restored within 10 minutes in both runs and both are logged.

Module 5 Privacy, security and records 4 hEvaluate

Health records are among the most sensitive data. Weak security harms patients and the clinic.

  • Use unique passwords, two-step sign-in, encrypted storage and access logs; never share logins.
  • Give staff only the access their role needs.
  • Do not use personal chat apps for clinical messages unless the rules allow it.
  • Know your health-data rules and who to report a breach to.
Worked example. A receptionist shares a password to cover a shift. An access log shows two people using one account. The clinic ends sharing and issues a separate account.

Activity. Complete a privacy and security checklist for a tool and mark each item pass or fix.

Standard to meet. No item left without a pass or a dated fix plan.

Module 6 What virtual care cannot do 3 hEvaluate

Some problems need an in-person visit or emergency care.

  • Emergencies such as chest pain, stroke signs, severe bleeding or breathing difficulty go to emergency services, not video.
  • Physical examination, tests and procedures need a visit.
  • Write red-flag rules with a clinician: when to refer in person, when to call emergency.
  • Give patients a clear "if it gets worse" instruction.
Worked example. A patient describes chest tightness during a follow-up call. The clinician tells them to call the emergency number now, stays on the line, and records the time and advice.

Activity. Write five red-flag rules agreed with a clinician and a patient information line.

Standard to meet. Clinician mentor signs the rules.

Module 7 Access and fairness 3 hAnalyse

Virtual care can widen access or leave people behind.

  • Offer phone, large-text, language and interpreter options.
  • Support low-bandwidth modes and community access points.
  • Track who misses visits and ask why.
  • Do not make virtual-only the default for people who cannot use it.
Worked example. A rural clinic uses a village centre with a tablet and a trained helper twice a week, bringing 30 new patients into care.

Activity. List three barriers in your area and a fix and owner for each.

Standard to meet. Each barrier has a fix, an owner and a date.

Module 8 Pilot, measure and improve 3 hCreate

Test with real steps and real numbers before scaling.

  • Run 10 mock visits; measure wait, success and satisfaction.
  • Set targets, for example 90% of calls start within 5 minutes of schedule.
  • Fix the top three problems and run again.
  • Plan monthly reviews with a clinician and a patient voice.
Worked example. First pilot: 7 of 10 start on time. After fixing the link emails and adding a pre-test, 9 of 10 start on time.

Activity. Present your pilot review with numbers and a dated improvement list.

Standard to meet. Numbers are real and totals match; at least three dated fixes.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: flow diagram, tool settings, 10 mock appointment log, privacy checklist, pilot review. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

Marking rubric (Not yet, Meets, Exceeds)
CriterionNot yetMeetsExceeds
Journey designMissing stepsAll steps ownedSteps owned with access options
Consent and IDNoneScriptedScripted and logged in tests
Fall-backNoneWrittenTested twice within 10 minutes

Ten-question check, with model answers

Model answer: Booking, reminder, ID check, consent, consult, record, follow-up. (Covered in module 1: Map the patient journey. To pass that module: All nine steps have an owner; at least one phone-only route is shown.)

Q2. Why two reminders?

Model answer: They cut no-shows. (Covered in module 2: Booking, slots and reminders. To pass that module: All five mock bookings complete; reminders arrive at the right times.)

Q3. What do you check before video visits?

Model answer: Identity and consent. (Covered in module 3: Identity and consent. To pass that module: Script covers identity, location, consent to video, notes and recording rule.)

Q4. What is the fall-back order?

Model answer: Voice, phone, then rebook. (Covered in module 4: Technology, connection and fall-backs. To pass that module: Contact is restored within 10 minutes in both runs and both are logged.)

Q5. Name two security measures.

Model answer: Two-step sign-in and encrypted storage. (Covered in module 5: Privacy, security and records. To pass that module: No item left without a pass or a dated fix plan.)

Q6. Name a case not for video.

Model answer: Emergency such as chest pain. (Covered in module 6: What virtual care cannot do. To pass that module: Clinician mentor signs the rules.)

Q7. Why keep access logs?

Model answer: To show who saw a record. (Covered in module 5: Privacy, security and records. To pass that module: No item left without a pass or a dated fix plan.)

Q8. Name one access fix.

Model answer: Phone option or community point. (Covered in module 7: Access and fairness. To pass that module: Each barrier has a fix, an owner and a date.)

Q9. What is a pilot target example?

Model answer: 90% of calls start on time. (Covered in module 8: Pilot, measure and improve. To pass that module: Numbers are real and totals match; at least three dated fixes.)

Q10. Who approves red-flag rules?

Model answer: A clinician. (Covered in module 6: What virtual care cannot do. To pass that module: Clinician mentor signs the rules.)

  • Telehealth technician. Fixes connection issues.
  • Clinic digital lead. Selects and reviews tools.
  • Privacy assistant. Checks access logs.

ten locations and global

Your place

Health-data and telehealth rules differ by place; check licensing and data rules with an adviser.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC). Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
