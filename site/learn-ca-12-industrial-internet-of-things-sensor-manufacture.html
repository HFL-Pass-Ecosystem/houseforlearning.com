Industrial internet of things sensor manufacturer

Run a real industrial internet of things sensor manufacturer pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small industrial internet of things sensor manufacturer offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Identify what sensors must measure 6 hUnderstand

Industrial sensors turn physical conditions into numbers used for control and safety.

  • Common signals: temperature, pressure, flow, level, vibration, gas.
  • Match accuracy, range and environment to the job: heat, wash-down, dust, explosive atmosphere.
  • Ask users what a wrong reading costs and what accuracy they need.
  • Pick one sensor type for your first product.
  • Walk the plant with the user and list each measurement point with its current method: gauge, manual check or none.
  • Write the output you need, such as a current signal, a voltage or a digital bus, and which controller will read it.
  • Record the highest and lowest temperature, moisture, vibration and chemicals the sensor will meet.
Worked example. A cold-room temperature sensor needs plus or minus 0.5 degrees C from -30 to +10; wrong readings spoil stock worth 40,000. The range spans 10 - (-30) = 40 degrees C, so plus or minus 0.5 is 0.5 / 40 = 1.25% of span. One spoiled batch costs 40,000, which equals the price of about 276 sensors at 145 (40,000 / 145 = 275.9).

Activity. Interview three plant users and write a requirement sheet.

Standard to meet. Requirements have units and limits.

Module 2 Know product safety and approval rules 6 hApply

Industrial products must be safe and legal.

  • Electrical safety approvals from an accredited body are normally required; check the current federal and provincial rule for your product.
  • Equipment for explosive atmospheres needs special certification.
  • Radio modules need radio approval.
  • Calibration claims need traceable standards.
  • Find which approval marks Canadian buyers accept and which test labs are accredited; ask a lab for a quote and lead time.
  • Keep a technical file with drawings, parts list, test reports and approval certificates, each dated.
  • Check what bilingual labels and manuals your buyers require, and check the provincial rule where it applies.
Worked example. Row: 'Sell a sensor into a gas plant | hazardous-location certification | certify | before sale | certificate'. Second row: 'Fit a wireless module | national radio equipment rules | use a pre-approved module or test | before sale | approval certificate'. Check the provincial rule on inspection of installed electrical equipment.

Activity. Build a rules table for one sensor.

Standard to meet. Rows sourced and dated.

Module 3 Design, build and calibrate 10 hApply

Accuracy comes from design and calibration.

  • Choose a sensing element and a stable circuit.
  • Calibrate at several points against a reference.
  • Test for temperature drift and noise.
  • Write the data sheet from measured results.
  • Use a reference instrument with a current calibration certificate that is more accurate than your sensor, often about four times better.
  • Let the sensor warm up and settle at each point before you read it, and write down the wait time.
  • Repeat each reading five times and record the spread, to separate random noise from fixed offset.
Worked example. Reference points 0, 20 and 40 degrees C; the sensor reads 0.4, 20.4 and 40.4, so the offset is 0.4 at each point. After subtracting it, the remaining error is at most 0.1 degrees C, which is 0.1 / 40 = 0.25% of span.

Activity. Calibrate a sensor at three points and record error.

Standard to meet. Error computed.

Module 4 Cost and price the product 8 hEvaluate

Cost per sensor falls as volume rises, so price with a volume in mind.

  • Add up parts, assembly, test and calibration time to get the cost of one sensor.
  • Spread the one-time certification cost over the number of units you expect to sell.
  • Set aside money for warranty repairs and returned units.
  • Set the price so the margin per sensor covers fixed costs at your expected volume.
  • Add a returns allowance: if 2% of units come back and each costs 70 to replace, add 0.02 x 70 = 1.40 per unit.
  • Count test and calibration minutes per unit, divide by 60 and multiply by the hourly labour cost.
  • Write a volume discount table and check the margin at each step so no tier sells at a loss.
Worked example. Price 145; cost 70; margin 75 (52%); fixed cost 150,000 → break-even 2,000 sensors. Teaching figures in CAD. Check: 2,000 x 75 = 150,000. If only 800 sell in year one, margin earned is 800 x 75 = 60,000, leaving 150,000 - 60,000 = 90,000 uncovered.

Activity. Build cost sheet with low-volume case.

Standard to meet. Both cases shown.

Module 5 Control production quality 8 hApply

Every unit must be tested.

  • Inspect incoming parts against the drawing before they enter production.
  • Test every finished unit at the end of the line against written pass limits.
  • Give each unit a serial number so you can trace its parts and test results.
  • Track first-pass yield each week to see where faults come from.
  • Write pass limits as numbers on the test sheet, such as 'error not above 0.3 at all three points'.
  • Tag failed units with a red label and a fault code, and never ship a reworked unit without a retest.
  • Check a sample from each supplier lot and link the lot number to the serial numbers.
Worked example. Yield 94%; main fail: solder joint; fix: reflow profile. Of 500 units built, 94% pass first time, which is 470 passes and 30 fails. If solder joints cause 18 of the 30, fixing them lifts yield to (470 + 18) / 500 = 97.6%.

Activity. Design a test sheet.

Standard to meet. Limits and sign-off.

Module 6 Sell and support 6 hCreate

Industrial buyers want proof.

  • Offer evaluation units so buyers can try the sensor in their own plant.
  • Provide integration notes for common controllers.
  • Answer support calls quickly, because plant downtime is costly for buyers.
  • Ask early customers to act as references for new buyers.
  • Write a one-page integration note per controller type showing wiring, signal range and a test step.
  • Prepare a pitch with the buyer's own numbers: cost of a wrong reading, sensors needed, price and payback.
  • Set a service promise, such as a reply within one working day, and track how often you keep it.
Worked example. Funnel: 15 plants, 6 evaluations, 3 orders. Plant to evaluation is 6 / 15 = 40%, evaluation to order is 3 / 6 = 50%, and overall 3 / 15 = 20%.

Activity. Pitch three plants.

Standard to meet. Pitch has data.

Module 7 Manage cyber and safety risk 6 hEvaluate

Connected sensors can be attacked.

  • Make firmware updates secure so only your signed code can run.
  • Give every device its own credentials instead of a shared default.
  • Design the sensor to go to a safe state when it detects a fault.
  • Document the known limits of the sensor in the data sheet and manual.
  • Sign firmware, check the signature before installing an update, and keep the previous version for rollback.
  • Keep the sensor network apart from the office network where you can, and list every open port.
  • Define the safe state on a fault, such as an output that goes to a known value, and test it by pulling power and cable.
Worked example. Risk: 'Default password found | 3 x 5 | unique keys | firmware lead | each release'. Likelihood 3 times impact 5 gives a score of 15 out of 25, so it is fixed first. Once each device has a unique key, likelihood falls to 1 and the score is 1 x 5 = 5.

Activity. Write a risk register.

Standard to meet. Owners named.

Module 8 Pilot with a customer 8 hCreate

Prove it in real conditions.

  • Install 20 sensors for 90 days.
  • Compare with reference readings.
  • Stop rule: drift above spec means redesign.
  • Plan the first production batch only after the pilot review passes.
  • Agree the pilot plan in writing with the customer: sensors, locations, dates, who reads reference values and how often.
  • Read reference values weekly and plot sensor minus reference on a graph over the 90 days.
  • Collect operator feedback on mounting, wiring and display, and list changes for the next version.
Worked example. The spec allows 0.2 degrees C of drift over 90 days. The worst of the 20 sensors drifted 0.18 and the average was 0.15, so all pass, with 0.2 - 0.18 = 0.02 of margin on the worst; proceed to a first batch.

Activity. Run pilot review.

Standard to meet. Review has decision.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name three industrial signals.

Model answer: Temperature, pressure, flow (also level, vibration, gas).

Q2. Why calibrate at several points?

Model answer: To find and correct errors across the range.

Q3. What does a data sheet state?

Model answer: Measured accuracy, range and conditions.

Q4. Why unique device credentials?

Model answer: Default passwords are an attack path.

Q5. What is first-pass yield?

Model answer: Units passing test the first time.

Q6. When is special certification needed?

Model answer: In explosive atmospheres.

Q7. Why offer evaluation units?

Model answer: Buyers want proof in their plant.

Q8. What is drift?

Model answer: Slow change in reading over time or temperature.

Q9. What triggers redesign?

Model answer: Drift above specification.

Q10. What is break-even?

Model answer: Fixed cost divided by margin per sensor.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
