flagship course · GL-07

360-Degree Photography and Virtual Tours

Capture, stitch and publish a safe, easy-to-use virtual tour of one real space, and prove that visitors can find their way around it.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L3 Foundation54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Capture, stitch and publish a safe, easy-to-use virtual tour of one real space, and prove that visitors can find their way around it.

  • Understand Explain how a 360 image works and who gains from a virtual tour.
  • Apply Capture a space on a steady stand with even light and overlap.
  • Analyse Analyse seams, load time and visitor scores to find what to fix.
  • Create Build a linked tour with captions, alt text and a floor map.
  • Evaluate Judge the finished tour against rights, privacy and ease-of-use checks.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Capture, build and publish a virtual tour of one real space with 6 to 10 linked spots.

Condition

A phone or low-cost 360 camera, a steady stand, a free stitching and tour tool, a free image editor, written permission from the space owner, a mentor and a peer circle. About 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Written permission held; faces, number plates and private papers hidden; every spot has a caption and alt text; the first spot loads in under 5 seconds on slow data; ten visitors give an average ease score of 4 out of 5 or better.

Who it is for: Small-business owners, estate and hospitality staff, school and college learners, freelancers. Requirements: A phone and a space you may photograph. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Get written permission from the owner of the space and from anyone who appears. Blur faces, number plates and private papers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Why a tour, and for whom 4 hUnderstand

A tour is useful only when a real person uses it to decide something. Start with the user, not the camera. After this module you can write a one-line brief for a 360-degree tour and name its real user, so every later shooting choice has a clear test.

  • A 360 image is a full sphere of view; the viewer looks around on a screen.
  • List five places where a tour helps a buyer decide: classroom, shop, guest room, workshop, hall.
  • Ask the owner what a visitor should see first and what must stay hidden.
  • Write the plan: 6 to 10 spots, a hide list, and how the link will be shared.
  • Write a one-line brief before shooting, such as 'help a first-time guest find the breakfast room in under two minutes', and check every later decision against that line.
  • Walk the space once with no camera and mark 8 to 15 candidate spots on a sketch; shooting from a plan saves hours of re-visits and stops you capturing dull corners.
  • Ask the owner for a short list of the three questions visitors ask most; make sure the tour answers each in the first four spots.
  • Decide in advance how long the tour should take: under three minutes for a quick look, ten for a full walk.
Worked example. A guest house owner wants fewer phone questions. Plan: 8 spots (entrance, 3 rooms, kitchen, garden, dining, road view); hide list: guest belongings and the office desk.

Activity. Choose one space, get written permission and write the one-page plan.

Standard to meet. Permission on file; 6 to 10 spots listed; hide list written.

Common mistake. Choosing a space that nobody has agreed to show, then finding at the end that the owner objects.

Second worked example. A school hall: the head teacher asks that the staff room door stays closed in every spot. Add it to the hide list before capture and re-plan the route around it.

Check yourself. Who is the tour for, and what is the first thing they must see?

Module 2 Rights, privacy and access 6 hUnderstand

A tour can harm people if it shows what it should not. Decide the rules before you press the shutter. You will be able to spot privacy and access problems in a space and agree written rules with its owner before any photograph is taken.

  • Permission from the owner and from any person who can be recognised.
  • Blur faces, number plates, screens and papers; remove street-door codes.
  • Write alt text for each spot so blind and low-vision visitors can follow the tour.
  • Keep a short consent note and a takedown contact on the tour page.
  • Before each session sweep the room for papers, screens, whiteboards, name badges and family photos, and cover or move anything that reveals private details inside the sphere.
  • Agree in writing what may be shown, how long the tour stays online, and who can ask for a spot to be removed. Then repeat those terms in an email the owner can answer yes to.
  • Keep a signed copy of the permission note with the project files and put its date in the tour page footer.
  • For children, schools and care homes, ask for written consent from a responsible adult and avoid showing anyone under 18.
Worked example. A hall has a notice board with phone numbers. The hide list says blur the board; the checker confirms it in spot 4.

Activity. Write the hide list and the alt-text style (one sentence: where, what, what to do next).

Standard to meet. Hide list covers every spot; alt-text style agreed with the mentor.

Common mistake. Treating blur as optional. A single readable number plate or paper can break trust.

Second worked example. A shop tour shows a delivery note with a customer name. Blur it, record the fix in the log, and re-check every spot before publishing.

Check yourself. Name three things on your hide list and the spot where each appears.

Module 3 Capture technique 8 hApply

Good capture saves hours of repair later. Keep height, level and light steady. You will capture overlapping spheres at a steady height, level and exposure, which cuts the repair time spent in the stitching stage.

  • Camera at eye level on a stand, the same height at every spot.
  • Spots about 3 metres apart in rooms, and up to 5 metres apart in halls.
  • Step out of view or use a timer; check mirrors and glass for your reflection.
  • In bright rooms take two or three exposures so light can be balanced later.
  • Place the camera at about 1.5 metres, keep spots 3 to 5 metres apart in rooms and halls, and number each position on your sketch so the capture order matches the tour order.
  • Check the exposure on the brightest window and the darkest corner before leaving a spot; if the range is too wide, take three shots: one normal, one darker and one lighter than normal.
  • Use a bubble level on the stand; a tilt of even two degrees shows as a curved floor.
  • Capture at the same time of day for all spots so shadows and colour match.
Worked example. A room with a bright window: one shot exposed for the room, one for the window. Merged later, both the sofa and the garden are visible.

Activity. Walk the space with a floor plan, mark spots and capture them all.

Standard to meet. All spots captured at one height with overlap; no person visible in the raw files.

Common mistake. Changing camera height between spots, so the tour jumps up and down.

Second worked example. A tour captured at 1.5 m except spot 4 at 1.2 m feels like sinking. Re-shoot spot 4 at 1.5 m, using a mark on the stand.

Check yourself. What height did you use, and how did you keep it the same?

Module 4 Stitching and fixing 8 hApply

Stitching joins overlapping pictures into one sphere. Seams and a tilted horizon are the usual faults. You will stitch overlapping frames into one sphere and correct seams and tilted horizons, so viewers never notice where pictures were joined.

  • Check seams on straight lines such as door frames and tables.
  • Level the horizon; fix colour so adjacent spots match.
  • Retouch the stand out of the floor.
  • Log each fault and the fix: a short table beats memory.
  • Zoom to 100 percent along each seam and inspect doorframes, tiles and table edges, since straight lines that kink are the quickest way to spot a stitch error.
  • Keep a short log of each fix, such as 'spot 04, added matching points on stair rail', so the next job reuses the cure instead of rediscovering it.
  • Save the raw files untouched and work on copies, so you can always start again.
  • Compare each stitched spot with a normal photograph to check proportions look true.
Worked example. Of 8 spots, 2 show a bent door frame at the seam: 2 of 8 is 25% needing a fix. After re-stitching with more overlap, 0 of 8 fail.

Activity. Stitch all spots and keep a fault log.

Standard to meet. Fault log complete; no visible seam error in the final images.

Common mistake. Fixing seams by guesswork and never logging what worked.

Second worked example. Fault log row: "Spot 6, bent door frame, cause: too little overlap, fix: re-shoot with 30% overlap, result: pass".

Check yourself. Which fault happened most often, and what stops it next time?

Module 5 Building the tour 8 hAnalyse

A tour is a path, not a pile of pictures. Links, captions and a map guide the viewer. You will link spheres, captions and a floor plan into a guided route, so a first-time visitor reaches the right room without getting lost.

  • Choose the start spot: the one that answers the visitor's first question.
  • Add arrows between spots; keep each spot to two or three links.
  • One caption and one alt text per spot; add a simple floor map.
  • Compress images so the tour opens on a phone.
  • Limit each sphere to three or four hotspots with short verbs, such as 'Go to kitchen', and test that every arrow leads somewhere the viewer expects.
  • Add a small floor plan with a moving position marker and a start-here button; guided orientation raises the share of visitors who reach the last room.
  • Name every file with the spot number and room, for example 03-kitchen, so links stay tidy.
  • Add a "You are here" dot on the floor map that moves as the viewer moves.
Worked example. 8 images at 6 MB are 48 MB. Compressed to 1.5 MB each they are 12 MB, about 48 seconds on a 2 Mbit/s link if loaded all at once. Loading only the first spot takes 6 seconds; cut it to 1 MB and it takes 4 seconds.

Activity. Build the draft tour and test it on a phone with slow data and on a computer.

Standard to meet. First spot loads in under 5 seconds; every spot has a caption and alt text.

Common mistake. Linking every spot to every other spot, so visitors get lost.

Second worked example. A hall tour with 8 spots uses two links per spot (next and back) plus a map, and testers find the kitchen in 20 seconds.

Check yourself. How many links does each of your spots have, and why?

Module 6 Viewing circle and testing 10 hCreate

Fresh eyes find problems you cannot see. Watch people use it without helping them. You will run a silent test with fresh viewers on several devices and turn their hesitations into specific, fixable changes to the tour.

  • Ask three peers to find the kitchen without any help.
  • Record where they hesitate, get lost or leave.
  • Count load time, number of spots and confusing links.
  • Ask a mentor with photo or property experience to check light, seams and rights.
  • Test on a phone over a mobile connection, a laptop, and one headset if available, and write down load time and any control that is hard to find for each device.
  • Ask three testers to find a named object without help and watch silently; the places they hesitate are the places the tour needs a clearer label or arrow.
  • Time how long each tester takes to reach the target room; under 30 seconds is a good aim.
  • Ask one tester who uses a screen reader to try the alt text and note what is unclear.
Worked example. Three of four peers cannot find the garden: the link is hidden behind a plant. Move the arrow, retest: all four find it in under 20 seconds.

Activity. Run the viewing circle and write down five fixes.

Standard to meet. Five fixes listed, ranked and dated.

Common mistake. Helping testers during the viewing circle, which hides the real problems.

Second worked example. A tester stalls at spot 3; you stay silent and note the exact spot and second. The fix is a larger arrow.

Check yourself. Where did two or more testers hesitate?

Module 7 Measure and decide 6 hEvaluate

A tour earns its keep only if people use it. Measure and then decide: publish, improve, or keep private. You will track a few usage figures and choose whether to publish, improve or keep a tour private, backed by evidence instead of opinion.

  • Ask the owner and ten visitors to score ease and truth to the real place from 1 to 5.
  • Average the scores: sum divided by number of people.
  • Count views and enquiries for two weeks.
  • Write three lessons learned and one clear decision.
  • Track four figures for two weeks: views, average time spent, completion of the target room, and clicks on the contact button, and note the starting values.
  • Compare changes one at a time, such as a new first sphere, and wait a full week before judging; changing three things at once tells you nothing about the cause.
  • Show the owner the numbers in a one-page summary with one picture; owners decide faster with less to read.
  • If views are low, check where the link is shared before changing the tour.
Worked example. Ten visitors give ease scores totalling 42, an average of 4.2; truth-to-place totals 45, average 4.5. Decision: publish, and fix the one slow spot.

Activity. Collect scores, calculate averages and write the decision note.

Standard to meet. Scores calculated correctly; decision backed by numbers.

Common mistake. Quoting only the best score and forgetting the lowest.

Second worked example. Scores 5,5,5,4,4,4,4,4,3,2 give average 4.0; the two low scores point to a slow-loading spot.

Check yourself. What is your lowest score and its reason?

Module 8 Review room and next 90 days 4 hCreate

Show your work, learn from others, and plan the next step. You will present your tour for critique, record what others notice, and set concrete targets for your next ninety days of practice.

  • Present the space, plan, tour, scores and decision in five minutes.
  • Give two peers written feedback: one strength, one fix.
  • Get a short written note from one real user.
  • Share a checklist or template so the next learner starts higher.
  • Hold a 30-minute review room session with the owner and one tester, bring the data page, and leave with three written changes and an owner for each.
  • Set three targets for the next 90 days, such as one new space, a 20 percent faster capture routine, and one published case study, and tick them off monthly.
  • Keep a "lessons" page for every project; the third tour is always faster than the first.
  • Offer a short price list for similar spaces only after the owner and reviewers have seen your finished tour.
Worked example. A learner shares a hide-list template; two later learners reuse it and credits are recorded.

Activity. Present, give feedback and write your 90-day plan.

Standard to meet. Two peer reviews given; 90-day plan has dates and a measure.

Common mistake. Giving only praise in peer feedback.

Second worked example. Feedback: "Strong: clear captions. Fix: spot 5 is dark; add a second exposure."

Check yourself. What one template or checklist will you share back?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The private office

A property owner wants a tour of a shared office. One desk has a visible bank letter and a screen with a customer list.

Question: What do you do before publishing?

Model answer: Stop, remove or blur the letter and screen in every spot where they appear, confirm with the owner, log the fix and re-check the whole tour.

Case 2. The slow tour

Visitors on mobile data leave a 14-spot tour before it opens. Files total 90 MB.

Question: How do you fix it?

Model answer: Compress images to about 1.5 MB, load the first spot only at start, cut spots to what matters, then retest on slow data until the first spot opens in under 5 seconds.

Glossary

Stitching
Joining overlapping pictures into one sphere.
Equirectangular image
A flat picture that maps a full sphere, like a world map.
Hotspot
A clickable point that jumps to another spot or shows a caption.
Alt text
A short written description of an image for people who cannot see it.
Overlap
The shared part of two shots that lets software join them.
Hide list
The agreed list of things that must not appear in the tour.

Your evidence: permission note, hide list, fault log, published tour link, ten visitor scores, decision note and 90-day plan. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a 360 image?

Model answer: A full-sphere picture the viewer can look around on a screen. (Covered in module 1: Why a tour, and for whom. To pass that module: Permission on file; 6 to 10 spots listed; hide list written.)

Q2. Why keep the camera at one height?

Model answer: So spots match and the tour feels steady. (Covered in module 3: Capture technique. To pass that module: All spots captured at one height with overlap; no person visible in the raw files.)

Q3. What must you hold before capturing a private space?

Model answer: Written permission from the owner. (Covered in module 2: Rights, privacy and access. To pass that module: Hide list covers every spot; alt-text style agreed with the mentor.)

Q4. What is stitching?

Model answer: Joining overlapping pictures into one sphere. (Covered in module 4: Stitching and fixing. To pass that module: Fault log complete; no visible seam error in the final images.)

Q5. Why write alt text?

Model answer: So blind and low-vision visitors can follow the tour. (Covered in module 2: Rights, privacy and access. To pass that module: Hide list covers every spot; alt-text style agreed with the mentor.)

Q6. Why compress images?

Model answer: So the tour loads on a phone with slow data. (Covered in module 5: Building the tour. To pass that module: First spot loads in under 5 seconds; every spot has a caption and alt text.)

Q7. What must be blurred or hidden?

Model answer: Faces, number plates, screens, papers and codes. (Covered in module 2: Rights, privacy and access. To pass that module: Hide list covers every spot; alt-text style agreed with the mentor.)

Q8. Why test on slow data?

Model answer: Many visitors have slow links; waiting makes them leave. (Covered in module 5: Building the tour. To pass that module: First spot loads in under 5 seconds; every spot has a caption and alt text.)

Q9. How do you calculate an average score?

Model answer: Add all scores and divide by the number of people. (Covered in module 7: Measure and decide. To pass that module: Scores calculated correctly; decision backed by numbers.)

Q10. Name three decisions after testing.

Model answer: Publish, improve, or keep private. (Covered in module 7: Measure and decide. To pass that module: Scores calculated correctly; decision backed by numbers.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Image and privacy rules differ by place: ask whether recognisable people, children or private homes need extra consent. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.
