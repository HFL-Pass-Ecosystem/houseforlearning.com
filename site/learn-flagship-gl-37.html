flagship course · GL-37

Crowdfunding Platform Practice

Prepare an honest crowdfunding campaign on a reputable platform or design a platform concept: story, goal, rewards, risk disclosure, backer care and delivery.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Prepare an honest crowdfunding campaign on a reputable platform or design a platform concept: story, goal, rewards, risk disclosure, backer care and delivery.

  • Understand Explain crowdfunding models and who bears which risks.
  • Apply Build a clear, honest campaign page and budget.
  • Analyse Analyse goal, fees, rewards and delivery costs.
  • Create Create a communication plan and delivery plan.
  • Evaluate Evaluate a campaign's fairness, compliance and results.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Prepare a complete campaign (page, budget, rewards, risk statement, update plan) for a real or practice project, without raising real money in the course.

Condition

A project idea, a reputable platform's public rules, a spreadsheet, a mentor with funding experience and a peer circle. About 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Goal justified by a budget; platform fees included; rewards costed with delivery; risks stated plainly; no promises you cannot keep; compliance questions raised with an adviser.

Who it is for: Founders, creators, community groups, fundraisers, students. Requirements: None. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Be truthful about risks and progress. Raising money from the public is regulated in many places; check the current rules from the official source for your place. Use only reputable platforms and follow their rules. This is not financial or legal advice.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Models 4 hUnderstand

Know the types. You will compare funding models by testing the 60 percent scenario, letting you choose a campaign structure you can honestly fulfil if the target is only partly reached.

  • Donation, reward, equity, loan models.
  • Who gets what in return.
  • Risk to backers.
  • Regulation differs by model.
  • Compare all-or-nothing and keep-what-you-raise models by asking what happens if you reach only 60 percent of your goal, and choose the one you can honestly fulfil.
  • Check which platform types suit your project, such as creative, community or product launches, and read the rules about allowed projects before building a page.
  • Read the platform's rules on prohibited projects and fees.
  • Define who the backers are and how they will hear of you.
Worked example. A reward campaign gives a product; an equity campaign gives shares and needs extra rules.

Activity. Compare four models in a table.

Standard to meet. Table lists risks and rules.

Common mistake. Treating all models as the same.

Second worked example. A community garden compares a donation page, a rewards page, a loan from neighbours and a small grant. Only the rewards page matches its 3,000 target and gives backers something real, so the team picks it.

Check yourself. Which model fits?

Module 2 The idea and audience 6 hUnderstand

Start with a clear need. You will test your idea with likely backers and build an early supporter list, so the first two days of the campaign begin with real momentum.

  • What, why, who benefits.
  • Existing supporters.
  • Evidence of demand.
  • Fit with a platform.
  • Test the idea with ten possible backers before launching, asking whether they would back it and at what amount, and record their answers honestly.
  • Build a list of at least 100 people who already know about your project, as early support in the first 48 hours strongly influences how later visitors respond.
  • Write the story in 150 words, then cut it by a third.
  • Prepare three pictures that show progress, not ideas.
Worked example. 100 people on a list; aim to convert 20% = 20 backers.

Activity. Write a project summary.

Standard to meet. One paragraph, specific.

Common mistake. No audience before launch.

Second worked example. A list of 100 supporters converts at 20% in the first week, giving 20 backers; the plan targets 40 so reaches beyond the list are needed.

Check yourself. How big is your list?

Module 3 Budget and goal 8 hApply

Raise what you need. You will calculate a funding goal from real production, delivery, fee and tax costs plus a buffer, preventing a successful campaign from losing money.

  • Costs: making, rewards, delivery, fees.
  • Platform fee plus payment fee.
  • Goal = total costs / (1 - fees).
  • Contingency.
  • Calculate your goal from real costs: production, materials, delivery, platform and payment charges and tax, plus a 10 to 15 percent buffer for delays and price changes.
  • Write a simple table of costs per reward and delivery to each region so that no reward loses money once postage is counted.
  • Calculate the break-even number of backers at each reward level.
  • Discuss tax with an adviser before you launch.
Worked example. Costs 8,000; fees 8%: goal = 8,000 / 0.92 = 8,696.

Activity. Compute the goal.

Standard to meet. Calculation shown.

Common mistake. Forgetting fees.

Second worked example. A school band needs 5,000 for instruments with fees of 10%. The goal is 5,000 / 0.90 = 5,556, so the page asks for 5,600 to stay safe.

Check yourself. What is your goal?

Module 4 Rewards 8 hApply

Fair and deliverable. You will design three to five reward levels with realistic delivery dates, giving backers clear choices and giving yourself promises you can keep.

  • Tiers by price.
  • Cost of each reward and delivery.
  • Timeline.
  • Limits (stock).
  • Offer three to five reward levels at clear prices, such as a thank-you at a low amount, a main product, and a limited extra, and state delivery dates for each.
  • Keep rewards simple enough that you can deliver them on time; a smaller list delivered well builds more trust than an ambitious one that slips.
  • Plan who answers questions during the campaign.
  • Set a rule for what you will do if you pass the goal.
Worked example. Reward at 25 costs 11 with postage 4: margin 10.

Activity. Cost three rewards.

Standard to meet. Margins positive.

Common mistake. Under-costing rewards.

Second worked example. A 25 reward costs 11 to make and 4 to post, leaving 10 towards other costs; a 15 reward with 3 postage leaves little.

Check yourself. What are reward margins?

Module 5 Story and page 8 hAnalyse

Clear, short, honest. You will write a short, honest campaign page with a plain statement of risks, which helps visitors understand what you are making and trust you to finish it.

  • Headline, video or picture, plan, team.
  • Show progress to date.
  • Risks and challenges.
  • FAQ.
  • Open your page with a short video or image and one sentence on what you are making, who it is for and when backers will receive it.
  • Show your team, your progress so far and your risks plainly; a section on challenges makes the rest of the page more believable.
  • Check shipping costs to the three biggest backer places.
  • Never promise profit or returns in a reward campaign.
Worked example. Page says "Prototype works; main risk is supplier delay of up to 6 weeks".

Activity. Draft the page.

Standard to meet. Contains risk section.

Common mistake. Hiding risk.

Second worked example. A repair-cafe page says 'Tools are bought; we need space rent for 6 months; if we reach half the goal we run one day a week'. Visitors know exactly what each tier buys.

Check yourself. What risks do you state?

Module 6 Rules and compliance 10 hCreate

Know your duties. You will read platform terms and local advertising and payment rules and keep clean financial records, so you avoid promises you cannot lawfully make.

  • Platform rules.
  • Local rules on public fundraising.
  • Tax on funds.
  • Refund policy.
  • Read the platform's terms and the rules for your country about advertising and payments, and do not promise results, returns or ownership that you cannot lawfully provide.
  • Keep records of all money received and spent, including platform payouts, refunds and supplier invoices, and ask a qualified adviser about tax obligations rather than guessing.
  • Keep a risk register with owner and date for each risk.
  • Schedule updates in a calendar from day one.
Worked example. The platform holds funds until the goal is met.

Activity. List the rules and ask an adviser.

Standard to meet. Adviser comments recorded.

Common mistake. Skipping rules.

Second worked example. A film project checks the platform's all-or-nothing rule: if the goal is missed, no money moves. The team writes that sentence under the donate button so no backer is surprised.

Check yourself. What rules apply?

Module 7 Backer care 6 hEvaluate

Keep trust. You will plan weekly updates, fast replies and a ready delay message, keeping backers informed and confident even when the schedule changes.

  • Thank and update often.
  • Share bad news early.
  • Handle questions quickly.
  • Deliver as promised.
  • Post an update at least every week during the campaign and answer every question within 24 hours, since quick replies show backers you are reliable.
  • Prepare a message template for delays that states the reason, the new date and what you are doing, and send it as soon as you know.
  • Keep campaign money in a clearly separate account when allowed.
  • Write a thank-you note ready to send at the end.
Worked example. A delay is announced in week 2 with a new date and a thank-you.

Activity. Write an update plan.

Standard to meet. Plan has dates.

Common mistake. Silence after launch.

Second worked example. A game maker sends updates every two weeks and answers each question within a day. When a part arrives late, backers get the news early with a new date, and only 2 of 300 ask to cancel.

Check yourself. When are updates?

Module 8 Delivery and review 4 hCreate

Finish well. You will compare planned and actual costs and timelines after delivery and thank backers publicly, leaving you with lessons and goodwill for your next project.

  • Production and shipping plan.
  • Track costs against budget.
  • Final report.
  • Lessons.
  • After delivery, compare planned and actual costs, timelines and backer numbers in a table, and write what you would change in the goal, rewards and schedule.
  • Thank backers publicly, share photos of the finished product, and ask for honest feedback that you can use in your next project.
  • Review what you would do differently.
  • Share your page for feedback before it is public.
Worked example. Final report: raised 9,100; costs 8,300; 3 delays; all rewards delivered.

Activity. Write a mock final report.

Standard to meet. Report honest.

Common mistake. Overpromising.

Second worked example. Final report: raised 4,200; costs 3,700; two delays; 118 of 120 rewards delivered, two reshipped. The team lists what they would change: order materials a month earlier.

Check yourself. What can you promise?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The shortfall

The campaign reaches 70% of the goal.

Question: What now?

Model answer: Follow platform rules; consider a smaller relaunch with honest communication.

Case 2. The delay

Production slips by two months.

Question: Response?

Model answer: Tell backers early, give a new date and offer refund options if rules require.

Glossary

Backer
A person who supports a campaign.
All-or-nothing
Funds released only if the goal is met.
Reward
Something given in return.
Platform fee
Charge by the platform.
Stretch goal
An extra target.
Risk statement
A plain list of what could go wrong.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name four models.

Model answer: Donation, reward, equity, loan. (Covered in module 1: Models. To pass that module: Table lists risks and rules.)

Q2. Goal formula?

Model answer: Costs divided by (1 minus fee rate). (Covered in module 3: Budget and goal. To pass that module: Calculation shown.)

Q3. Why cost rewards?

Model answer: Rewards and delivery can eat the margin. (Covered in module 4: Rewards. To pass that module: Margins positive.)

Q4. Why show risks?

Model answer: Honesty and trust. (Covered in module 5: Story and page. To pass that module: Contains risk section.)

Q5. Who regulates?

Model answer: Local financial or fundraising authorities. (Covered in module 6: Rules and compliance. To pass that module: Adviser comments recorded.)

Q6. What does all-or-nothing mean?

Model answer: Funds are released only if the goal is met. (Covered in module 1: Models. To pass that module: Table lists risks and rules.)

Q7. Why update backers?

Model answer: Trust. (Covered in module 7: Backer care. To pass that module: Plan has dates.)

Q8. What if delayed?

Model answer: Tell early with a new date. (Covered in module 7: Backer care. To pass that module: Plan has dates.)

Q9. Is this course for raising real money?

Model answer: No, preparation only. (Covered in module 6: Rules and compliance. To pass that module: Adviser comments recorded.)

Q10. Why a contingency?

Model answer: Costs rise. (Covered in module 3: Budget and goal. To pass that module: Calculation shown.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Public fundraising and securities rules differ strongly by place; check before anything is published. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a Brainport-area small firm.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC). Partner to practise with: SEBRAE small-business support, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or a SkillsFuture-linked training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a TAFE or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico (Central America)

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
