Mobile Services

Run a pilot of mobile Services in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small mobile Services offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know mobile services 6 hUnderstand

Mobile services use phones and tablets to reach people and do work. This can mean repair, set-up, apps for small firms, or services that travel to the customer.

  • Name the parts of a phone: screen, battery, storage, camera, SIM or eSIM, operating system.
  • Describe two kinds of mobile service: fixing and setting up devices, and offering a service through a phone.
  • Explain what an app, an update and a data plan are.
  • List words: backup, sync, permission, signal, handset.
  • Explain the difference between storage and memory on a phone.
  • Describe what a SIM and an eSIM do to connect a phone to a network.
  • Identify which uses of a phone take the most data.
Worked example. A customer's phone has a cracked screen and a full storage. The tech writes both on the job sheet and fixes the cheaper problem first. Two problems are listed, so the job sheet has two lines with a cost beside each.

Activity. Interview one person about their three biggest phone problems and write them in a table.

Standard to meet. Task: name phone parts and service types. Condition: after one interview. Standard: six parts and two service types correctly named.

Module 2 Find and fix common phone problems 6 hApply

Most phone problems are simple. Use a clear method and protect the owner's data.

  • Apply a fault method: ask, restart, check update and storage, test one fix at a time.
  • Check battery health, charger and cable before blaming the phone.
  • Explain why you back up data and ask for consent before any repair.
  • Compare repair and replace using cost and age of the device.
  • Test the charger and cable on a second phone to find which part is faulty.
  • Check for signs of water damage and tell the owner before opening the phone.
  • Calculate the repair quote as a share of the price of a similar used phone.
Worked example. Phone drains fast. A check shows one app uses most of the battery in the background. Closing its permission fixes it; no parts needed. If a repair costs 120 and a similar used phone costs 300, the repair is 120 / 300 = 40% of replacing it.

Activity. Fix three practice problems on test phones and write a job sheet for each.

Standard to meet. Task: fix a phone problem. Condition: test phone, owner consent. Standard: one change at a time and a signed job sheet.

Module 3 Follow the rules in Canada 10 hApply

Mobile work touches privacy, telecom rules, e-waste and consumer law. Check with the local authority.

  • Explain that personal data on a phone is private; get consent and use it only for the job.
  • Describe that telecom and radio equipment are federally regulated; check the federal rule before selling or modifying equipment.
  • Check provincial consumer rules for repairs, estimates and warranties.
  • Apply local e-waste rules for batteries and broken devices.
  • Ask the provincial consumer office what an estimate must state before a repair and write the answer.
  • Tell the customer that a repair may change warranty status; check the maker's terms.
  • Find the drop-off for lithium batteries in your area.
Worked example. Rules table row: Estimate | provincial consumer rules | give a written estimate before work | before every repair | copy kept in job file. If the estimate was 90 and the bill would be 140, the 50 extra needs the customer's approval before work continues.

Activity. Build a rules table covering privacy, equipment, repair estimates and e-waste for your province.

Standard to meet. Task: map the rules. Condition: official pages only. Standard: each row has an enforcer, an action and a checked date.

Module 4 Set up devices and accounts 8 hEvaluate

Many customers need help with set-up more than repair. A clear checklist saves time.

  • Build a set-up checklist: update, screen lock, accounts, backup, key apps, find-my-device.
  • Apply a data transfer method and verify that contacts and photos arrived.
  • Explain two-step sign-in and help the customer turn it on.
  • Teach one skill the customer asked for, in short steps.
  • Compare backup options by cost, space and ease and recommend one.
  • Write a list of the accounts the customer must remember, without writing passwords.
  • Wipe the old phone only after the customer confirms the new one has everything.
Worked example. Older customer moves to a new phone. Tech transfers contacts and photos, sets a larger text size, turns on backup, and writes three simple steps on a card. Planned time: 25 + 10 + 15 + 10 = 60 minutes for transfer, backup, accounts and a short lesson.

Activity. Set up a test phone from the checklist and write a one-page guide for a beginner.

Standard to meet. Task: set up a phone. Condition: written checklist. Standard: transfer verified and customer can repeat three tasks.

Module 5 Make a mobile service for others 8 hApply

Many small firms need simple mobile tools such as a booking page or a message list. Start small and test.

  • Describe the customer's task in one sentence before you build.
  • Build a simple mobile-friendly page or message flow with no-code tools.
  • Check it on two different phones and with slow data.
  • Evaluate what happens if the tool fails: a plain back-up such as a phone number.
  • Define who uses the page, on what phone and where.
  • Write the page text in plain words at a size readable without zoom.
  • Check the page with large text so people with weak sight can use it.
Worked example. A cleaner wants bookings by phone. A simple page shows hours, price and a call button. A test on two phones finds the button too small; it is made bigger. On slow data the page took 9 seconds to load; after shrinking pictures it takes 3, saving 6 seconds.

Activity. Build and test one simple mobile page for a practice client.

Standard to meet. Task: build a mobile page. Condition: no-code tool. Standard: works on two phones and one tester completes a booking in under two minutes.

Module 6 Price and plan a mobile business 6 hCreate

Mobile work saves the customer a trip, so travel and time must be priced.

  • Compute cost per job: parts, travel, tools, insurance, time.
  • Compare prices of five local repair or set-up services.
  • Build a route plan to group jobs by area.
  • Check parts suppliers for quality and return terms.
  • Calculate break-even visits as fixed cost divided by margin per visit.
  • Compare pay per hour for a long drive and a short one.
  • Calculate the cost of a spare-parts stock and the repairs needed to cover it.
Worked example. Set-up visit 70, travel and tools 8. Margin 62 per visit. Fixed cost 900 a month gives break-even at 900 / 62 = 14.5, so 15 visits. At 25 visits a month, margin is 25 x 62 = 1,550, minus 900 leaves 650. Teaching numbers only.

Activity. Build a cost sheet and a price list for five services.

Standard to meet. Task: price a mobile service. Condition: all costs counted. Standard: break-even correct and shown with units.

Module 7 Safety, trust and good service 6 hEvaluate

You may hold a person's whole life on one phone. Handle it with care.

  • Apply a privacy rule: never open personal photos or messages unless the job needs it.
  • Check parts and chargers for safety; do not use damaged batteries.
  • Explain scams: fake support calls and fake links; teach customers to spot them.
  • Handle a complaint with a written fix or refund as agreed.
  • Hand back or wipe the old device only as the customer decides in writing.
  • Store customer contact details in a locked place and delete them when not needed.
  • Practice a calm script for a customer upset about a delayed repair.
Worked example. A customer receives a call from a fake bank asking for a code. The tech teaches the rule: no one real ever asks for a one-time code. The customer practices hanging up and calling the number on the bank card.

Activity. Write a privacy note, a safety checklist and a customer scam-awareness card.

Standard to meet. Task: protect a customer. Condition: practice job. Standard: privacy note signed and safety checklist completed.

Module 8 Run the service for real 8 hCreate

Now run your mobile service with real customers and a growth plan.

  • Run a 60-day pilot with a stop rule agreed in advance.
  • Review weekly: jobs, income, time, complaints, repeats.
  • Create a 12-month plan with training and equipment budget.
  • Lead others: write the checklist so a helper can do the same job.
  • Calculate visits per week and income per hour at the end of the pilot.
  • Compare results with the stop rule on the planned date.
  • Identify the service customers asked for most and decide to add, change or skip it.
Worked example. Stop rule: if fewer than 30 visits in 60 days, stop and write lessons. Result: 41 visits x 62 = 2,542 in margin, minus 1,800 fixed costs for two months = 742; add a small group class for older users.

Activity. Run the pilot, write the review and present your plan to three people.

Standard to meet. Task: run a mobile service pilot. Condition: 60 days, real customers. Standard: results compared with the stop rule and rules re-checked.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
