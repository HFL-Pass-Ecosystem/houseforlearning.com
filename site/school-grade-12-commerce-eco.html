Grade 12 Commerce: Economics

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

  • Understand Describe the budget and exchange rates.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Commerce Economics, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 National income and related aggregates Weeks 1 to 9Apply

In this unit you work on 'National income and related aggregates', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: measure national income. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'National income and related aggregates' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: measure national income. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: GDP = C + I + G + (X - M) = 600 + 200 + 150 + (80 - 60) = 970. Method: Model run for 'National income and related aggregates'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Use the expenditure method. Private consumption 600, investment 200, government spending 150, exports 100, imports 80. GDP at market price = 600 + 200 + 150 + (100 - 80) = 970. Net exports are exports minus imports.
  2. Step 2, Move to national income. Depreciation 50, so NDP at market price = 970 - 50 = 920. Net factor income from abroad is -10, so NNP at market price = 910. Indirect taxes 70 minus subsidies 20 gives net indirect tax 50.
  3. Step 3, Finish the chain. NNP at factor cost = 910 - 50 = 860, which is national income. Check with the income method: NDP at factor cost = 500 compensation + 280 operating surplus + 90 mixed income = 870. Subtracting 10 net factor income from abroad gives 860.
  4. Step 4, Avoid double counting with value added. A farmer sells wheat for 40 to a miller; flour sells for 70 to a baker; bread sells for 120. Value added = 40 + 30 + 50 = 120, equal to the final product value, not 230.
  5. Step 5, Real GDP. If nominal GDP is 970 and the price index is 110 with base 100, real GDP = 970 / 110 x 100 = 881.8 approximately. Model answer: 'Real GDP removes price changes, so it shows true output growth.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: GDP at market price 500, depreciation 30, net indirect tax 20, net factor income from abroad 5. Model answer: 'NDP at market price 470, NNP at market price 475, NNP at factor cost 455 (national income).'

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Measure national income: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Money, banking and income determination Weeks 10 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'Money, banking and income determination', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: explain money creation and income determination. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Money, banking and income determination' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: explain money creation and income determination. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Money multiplier = 1 / reserve ratio = 1 / 0.2 = 5. A deposit of 100 can support up to 500 in total deposits. Method: Model run for 'Money, banking and income determination'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Show how banks create money. A deposit of 1,000 is made and the required reserve ratio is 10 percent. The bank keeps 100 and lends 900, which returns as deposits. The money multiplier is 1 / 0.10 = 10, so total deposits can reach 10,000.
  2. Step 2, Name central bank tools. To reduce credit, it raises the repo rate, cash reserve ratio or sells securities in open market operations. To raise credit, it does the opposite. A higher reserve ratio of 20 percent cuts the multiplier to 5.
  3. Step 3, Find equilibrium income. Consumption is C = 100 + 0.75Y and investment is 50. Y = (100 + 50) / (1 - 0.75) = 150 / 0.25 = 600. Check: consumption 550 and saving 50 equals investment 50, so planned saving equals investment.
  4. Step 4, Use the multiplier. Multiplier = 1 / (1 - MPC) = 1 / 0.25 = 4. If investment rises by 20, income rises by 20 x 4 = 80, to 680. New consumption 610 plus investment 70 equals 680.
  5. Step 5, Analyse a gap. Full-employment income is 700. At 700, aggregate demand is 625 + 50 = 675, so there is a deficient demand gap of 25. Raising investment by 25 brings income up by 100 to 700.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: MPC is 0.8; investment increases by 50. Model answer: 'The multiplier is 1 / 0.2 = 5, so income rises by 50 x 5 = 250.'

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Explain money creation and income determination: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 Government budget and the balance of payments Weeks 19 to 27Understand

In this unit you work on 'Government budget and the balance of payments', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe the budget and exchange rates. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Government budget and the balance of payments' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe the budget and exchange rates. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Revenue receipts 100, revenue expenditure 120: revenue deficit 20. Fiscal deficit = total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowing. Method: Model run for 'Government budget and the balance of payments'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, A government budget lists revenue and spending. Revenue is 800 and spending is 920, so the budget deficit is 920 - 800 = 120. If national income is 4,000, the deficit is 120 / 4,000 = 3 percent of national income. If revenue were bigger than spending it would be a surplus.
  2. Step 2, Taxes raise revenue. Direct taxes, such as income tax, are paid on income. Indirect taxes, such as sales tax, are paid on goods. A progressive tax takes a larger share as income rises. If the first 10,000 is untaxed and the rest pays 20 percent, income of 30,000 pays 20 percent of 20,000, which is 4,000, an average rate of 13.3 percent.
  3. Step 3, Fiscal policy uses spending and taxes to steer the economy. In a recession the government spends more or cuts taxes. If households spend 0.8 of extra income, the multiplier is 1 / (1 - 0.8) = 5, so extra spending of 50 raises national income by up to 250. In high inflation it does the reverse.
  4. Step 4, The balance of payments records a country's dealings with the world. In the current account, goods exports 500 and imports 560 give a trade balance of -60. Add net services +30, net income -20 and transfers +15: -60 + 30 - 20 + 15 = -35, a current account deficit. Financial account inflows pay for it.
  5. Step 5, Exchange rates. Say 1 foreign currency unit (FC) = 80 local units. A local good priced at 400 costs foreigners 400 / 80 = 5 FC. If the local currency depreciates to 100 per FC, the good costs 400 / 100 = 4 FC, so exports become cheaper, while an imported good priced at 5 FC now costs 500 local units.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: revenue is 650 and spending is 600. Find the budget balance. If 1 FC = 50 local units, what is a 2,000 local-unit item worth in FC? Answer: a surplus of 650 - 600 = 50; and 2,000 / 50 = 40 FC.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe the budget and exchange rates: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Indian economy and development Weeks 28 to 36Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Indian economy and development', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: analyse development, poverty and reforms. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Indian economy and development' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: analyse development, poverty and reforms. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Compare two years of one indicator, such as literacy or per capita income, in a table; say the change in percent and one cause. Method: Model run for 'Indian economy and development'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Date the policy phases. Planning began with the first Five Year Plan in 1951, which stressed agriculture, and from 1956 the plans gave priority to public sector heavy industry. By 1991 a balance of payments crisis, with very low foreign exchange reserves, led to reforms.
  2. Step 2, Explain the 1991 reforms. They are summarised as liberalisation (fewer licences), privatisation (smaller state role in some sectors) and globalisation (lower import duties, foreign investment). Model answer should state both gains and costs in a balanced way.
  3. Step 3, Compute growth. If GDP rises from 1,000 to 1,060 units, growth is (1,060 - 1,000) / 1,000 x 100 = 6 percent. If population grows 1.5 percent, per capita income grows roughly 4.5 percent, which affects living standards.
  4. Step 4, Read sector shares. Agriculture employs roughly 45 percent of workers but produces under 20 percent of output, so productivity per worker is low there. This gap explains why rural incomes lag and why shifting work to industry and services is a goal.
  5. Step 5, Measure poverty. Head count ratio = poor / population x 100. If 220 million of 1,000 million live below the poverty line, the ratio is 22 percent. Compare using HDI as well, which includes health and education, not just income.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: assess one effect of the 1991 reforms. Model answer: 'Services and exports grew faster because of openness, but inequality between regions and sectors persisted, so growth needs jobs and skills too.'

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Analyse development, poverty and reforms: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Scarcity and choice

On the slide: L1 Basic · Scarcity and choice

Notes: Name what scarcity means and give one opportunity cost from daily life. Notes: a farmer can grow 40 tonnes of wheat or 20 of maize on one field; learners say what giving up one tonne of wheat costs in maize.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Demand and supply

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Demand and supply

Notes: Explain why price changes move buyers and sellers in opposite directions. Notes: draw a demand line and a supply line for a market stall, then shift one at a time and say what happens to price and quantity.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Equilibrium and elasticity

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Equilibrium and elasticity

Notes: Calculate equilibrium and price elasticity of demand from simple equations. Notes: demand Q = 100 minus 2P and supply Q = 20 plus 2P give price 20 and quantity 60; raising price to 22 cuts quantity by about 6.7 per cent, elasticity about minus 0.67.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Growth, inflation and the multiplier

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Growth, inflation and the multiplier

Notes: Apply the multiplier and measure inflation from index numbers. Notes: an index rising from 200 to 210 is 5 per cent inflation; a marginal propensity to consume of 0.8 gives a multiplier of 5, so an injection of 10 raises income by 50.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Market failure and policy (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Market failure and policy

Notes: Analyse an externality and compare a tax, a subsidy and regulation. Notes: with a marginal external cost of 4 per unit and 1,000 units sold, a tax of 4 raises 4,000; learners list who gains, who loses and one unintended effect.

This course: Grade 12 Commerce Economics is at this level. Its units include National income and related aggregates; Money, banking and income determination; Government budget and the balance of payments; Indian economy and development.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Market power and welfare

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Market power and welfare

Notes: Derive monopoly output and deadweight loss and compare with competition. Notes: demand P = 100 minus Q with marginal cost 20 gives monopoly output 40 at price 60 and profit 1,600; competition gives 80, so the welfare loss is 800.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Growth models and causal evidence

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Growth models and causal evidence

Notes: Evaluate a growth model and the identification strategy behind a policy claim. Notes: with saving rate 0.2, depreciation plus population growth 0.05 and capital share 0.5, steady-state capital per worker is 16; discuss what instrument would justify a causal reading.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
