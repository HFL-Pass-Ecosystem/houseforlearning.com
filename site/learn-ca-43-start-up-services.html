Start-Up Services

Run a pilot of start-Up Services in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small start-Up Services offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: software, data and digital services. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know what a start-up is and the words used 6 hUnderstand

A start-up is a new business that tests an idea and aims to grow. Learn the words you will hear.

  • Terms: customer, problem, solution, prototype, pilot, revenue, cost, runway, founder.
  • Runway is how many months you can run before money ends.
  • Start-up services help new firms with setup, product tests, bookkeeping, planning and finding customers.
  • Talk to one founder about the first six months and write what surprised them.
  • Learn what a founder does in a normal week: sell, build, count money, fix problems.
  • Write the problem you want to solve in one sentence and name who has it.
  • List what you can do yourself and what you need a partner or adviser for.
Worked example. Cash 12,000 and monthly spending 3,000 gives runway of 12,000 / 3,000 = 4 months. If you cut spending to 2,000 a month, runway grows to 6 months. The same cash lasts longer with lower costs.

Activity. Interview one founder and write the problem they solve, who pays and what they would do differently.

Standard to meet. Task: write a founder interview note. Condition: one 20-minute talk. Standard: problem, customer, money source and one lesson, in plain words.

Module 2 Explain how an idea becomes a tested offer 6 hApply

Test the idea with real people before you spend much.

  • Steps: find the problem, talk to ten people, sketch the offer, build the smallest test, measure, decide.
  • Ask what they do now, what it costs them and what they have tried.
  • A prototype is a rough version used to learn, not to impress.
  • Write down what would prove you wrong.
  • Ask open questions and write the answers word for word.
  • Count how many of the ten people say the problem is serious enough to pay for.
  • Change the offer after each round of talks and note what you changed.
Worked example. Test: a one-page sign-up for a tutoring service. Goal: five paid sign-ups from fifty visitors, which is 5 / 50 = 10 percent. If fewer than two sign up (under 4 percent), change the offer.

Activity. Interview ten possible customers and write the one-line value statement.

Standard to meet. Task: run ten customer interviews. Condition: two weeks. Standard: notes dated; the value statement names who, need, result and how it differs from what they do now.

Module 3 Build the smallest version and test it 10 hApply

Apply what you learned by making a simple test and measuring it.

  • Choose one feature or service that solves the main problem.
  • Set one measure of success before you start.
  • Run the test for a fixed time with real users.
  • Write down what you learned and what you will change.
  • Make the test by hand before you build any software or buy equipment.
  • Write the cost of the test, in money and in hours.
  • Ask each user why they did or did not pay, and record the answer.
Worked example. Smallest test: a hand-made weekly meal list sent to ten homes. Measure: how many pay for week two. If 4 of 10 pay, the rate is 40 percent. At 12 CAD per list, 4 payers bring in 48 CAD for the week.

Activity. Build and run a small test for two weeks and record the measure.

Standard to meet. Task: run a small test. Condition: real users, fixed time. Standard: measure set in advance, results recorded and one decision made.

Module 4 Choose a business form and meet Canadian rules 8 hEvaluate

How you set up affects tax, risk and paperwork. Check the options.

  • Common forms: sole proprietor, partnership, corporation. They differ in liability, tax and cost to set up. Ask an accountant or lawyer for your case.
  • Register your business name and get any needed local licences. Check with the provincial and city offices.
  • Register for a business number and sales tax once required. If you hire, payroll deductions must be remitted. Check the current rules with the tax agency.
  • If there are several founders, put in writing who owns what and what happens if someone leaves.
  • Ask an accountant about the tax effects of each business form before you choose.
  • Check whether your name, domain and logo are free to use.
  • Write the date each registration or filing is due, and set a reminder.
Worked example. Rules table row: 'Business name | provincial registry | register before using the name | before launch | registration kept on file'. If registration takes 2 weeks and launch is in week 6, apply by week 4 at the latest, and sooner for a safety margin.

Activity. Build a rules table and a one-page founder agreement outline.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table and outline. Condition: official pages and one adviser conversation. Standard: each row has a dated source; the outline covers roles, ownership and exit.

Module 5 Plan money, price and runway 8 hApply

Count costs and cash. Many start-ups fail from running out of cash, not from a bad idea.

  • List start-up costs and monthly costs. Separate fixed and variable.
  • Margin per unit = price minus variable cost. Break-even = fixed cost divided by margin.
  • Plan three cases: low, expected and high sales.
  • Learn about public grants, loans and programs for new business, and read the terms. Check current programs with business support offices.
  • Add a cash buffer for the first three months of costs.
  • Compare your monthly cash in and cash out, and note the lowest balance.
  • Check what is due and when, such as tax, rent and supplier payments.
Worked example. Price 150 per job, variable cost 35, margin 115. Fixed cost 1,380 a month: break-even is 1,380 / 115 = 12 jobs. At 15 jobs, margin is 15 x 115 = 1,725, so profit is 1,725 - 1,380 = 345 CAD. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build a 12-month cash plan with three cases and a runway number.

Standard to meet. Task: build a cash plan. Condition: spreadsheet with visible formulas. Standard: units shown, three cases, runway computed, mentor checks arithmetic.

Module 6 Run a lean week and keep records 6 hCreate

A start-up runs on a few clear numbers and weekly habits.

  • Pick three weekly measures, such as new leads, paying customers and cash.
  • Hold a short weekly review and choose one action.
  • Keep invoices, receipts and bank records in order from day one.
  • Review with an adviser each quarter.
  • Write the one action you will take for each of the three measures.
  • Pay yourself on a schedule and record it as a cost.
  • Spend 30 minutes each week entering receipts and bank lines so the books never fall behind.
Worked example. Week 5: 12 leads, 3 paying customers, cash 4,100. Conversion is 3 / 12 = 25 percent. Action: call back the leads that did not reply. If two of the nine become customers, next week's total is 5.

Activity. Run a four-week log with the weekly one-page check.

Standard to meet. Task: run and log four weeks. Condition: real or practice venture. Standard: four weekly pages complete and each action is dated with a result.

Module 7 Sell and win early customers 6 hEvaluate

Early customers teach you the most.

  • Start with direct contact and personal introductions.
  • Write a one-page offer: who, what, result, proof, price, how to start.
  • Use consent for emails and texts; follow the current anti-spam rules.
  • Track a funnel and fix the weakest step first.
  • Ask each early customer for a short written comment, with permission to share it.
  • Offer a small discount for the first few customers in return for feedback.
  • Keep a list of every person you contact, the date and the reply.
Worked example. Funnel: 40 contacts, 12 conversations, 5 offers, 2 customers. Contact to conversation is 12 / 40 = 30 percent, conversation to offer is 5 / 12, about 42 percent, and offer to customer is 2 / 5 = 40 percent. Weakest step: contact to conversation.

Activity. Send twenty consent-based messages and book five conversations.

Standard to meet. Task: run an outreach test. Condition: two weeks. Standard: messages logged, replies recorded, one test and its result written.

Module 8 Manage risk, pilot and decide 8 hCreate

A pilot tells you whether to stop, change or grow.

  • Risk register: cash, key person, copy, data breach, supplier, rule changes.
  • Ask an adviser about intellectual property, such as trademarks, copyright and patents, before you share your idea widely.
  • Run a 60 to 90 day pilot with a stop rule.
  • Write the review and a 12-month plan with three milestones.
  • Decide which parts of your idea to keep secret, such as a recipe or a client list, and which to protect formally.
  • Choose the numbers that will show if the pilot works: customers, cash and repeat buyers.
  • List three people to ask for advice, and the question to ask each.
Worked example. Stop rule: 'If we have fewer than five paying customers by day 60, we stop or change the offer.' With 12 jobs at 150 CAD, income would be 1,800 CAD. If only 4 customers have paid by day 60, the rule is triggered.

Activity. Run the pilot and present the review and plan to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run a start-up pilot. Condition: legal and ethical practice. Standard: measures tracked, decision supported by numbers, plan clear.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
