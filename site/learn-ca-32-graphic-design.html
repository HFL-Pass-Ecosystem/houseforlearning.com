Graphic Design

Run a pilot of graphic Design in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small graphic Design offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: content, creative and marketing work. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know the words of graphic design 6 hUnderstand

Graphic design uses shapes, type, colour and images to send a clear message.

  • Learn the terms: layout, grid, typeface, hierarchy, contrast, margin, vector, raster, resolution, RGB, CMYK.
  • Raster images are made of pixels; vector images are made of lines and can be enlarged without blur.
  • RGB is for screens; CMYK is for print.
  • Look at five posters and name the headline, image, text and call to action.
  • Check contrast by squinting: the headline should still stand out.
  • Match each term to an example on a poster you collected.
  • Describe when you would choose vector over raster.
Worked example. A poster has a big headline, one image and a small date line. The eye goes headline, image, date. A 10 cm wide print image at 300 pixels per inch needs about 10 / 2.54 x 300 = 1,181 pixels across.

Activity. Mark hierarchy, contrast and alignment on five designs.

Standard to meet. Task: analyse five designs. Condition: with printed or saved samples. Standard: all parts labelled and one improvement named for each.

Module 2 Explain a design job from brief to files 6 hApply

A job has steps. Name them to plan your time.

  • The steps: brief, research, sketches, first design, feedback, changes, final files.
  • A brief states purpose, audience, size, where it will be used, deadline and the one action wanted.
  • Ask for text, logos and images before you start.
  • Agree how many rounds of changes are included.
  • Write the one action wanted at the top of the brief.
  • Check what the client has supplied against your list and ask in writing for anything that is missing.
  • Draft a schedule with dates for sketches, first design, feedback and final files.
Worked example. A client wants a flyer. You ask: Who reads it? Where is it handed out? What size? What must it say? A schedule of sketches 1 day, design 2 days, feedback 1 day, changes 1 day and files 1 day is 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6 days.

Activity. Write a brief form and test it on a friend.

Standard to meet. Task: complete a brief. Condition: 30-minute conversation. Standard: purpose, audience, size, use, deadline and change rounds are stated.

Module 3 Design a clear piece to a standard 10 hApply

Now you make. Use layout, type and colour to guide the reader.

  • Put the main point first and make it the biggest.
  • Use two typefaces at most and one clear colour scheme.
  • Leave white space; do not fill every corner.
  • Check contrast so text can be read, including by people with low vision.
  • Sketch three layouts in ten minutes before you open any software.
  • Align elements to a grid and check the margins are equal.
  • Test the design at thumbnail size and at arm's length.
Worked example. Before: five fonts, crowded text. After: one headline font, one body font, 20 mm margins, strong contrast. On a letter-size page about 216 mm wide, 20 mm margins on both sides leave 216 - 40 = 176 mm for content.

Activity. Design a flyer and a social post from one brief, with three sketches first.

Standard to meet. Task: design a flyer and a post. Condition: from a written brief in four hours. Standard: clear hierarchy, readable contrast, correct size and no spelling errors.

Module 4 Follow Canadian rules for design work 8 hEvaluate

Design touches copyright, advertising and accessibility rules.

  • Copyright protects images, fonts, music and logos. Use your own work or licensed work and keep the licence; check the federal copyright rules when unsure.
  • Typefaces have licences. Check what your licence allows before using one in a client job.
  • Advertising must be truthful. Some products, such as alcohol, food and health, have extra rules; check the current rule with the local authority.
  • In Quebec, commercial signage and advertising generally need French; check the current rule with the local authority. Accessibility rules can apply to public documents.
  • Keep licence receipts in a folder for each client.
  • Check the extra rules for any health, food or alcohol wording.
  • Describe how you add alternative text and readable colours to public documents.
Worked example. A client wants a photo from a web search. You say it may be protected and offer a licensed or original image. If a licensed photo costs $15, the invoice shows $15 for the image so the client sees what they paid for.

Activity. Build a table of rule, who sets it, what you must do and where you checked, for ten design situations.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table. Condition: using official public pages. Standard: each row has a dated source; unclear items say 'check the current rule with the local authority'.

Module 5 Price your design work and check it pays 8 hApply

You are paid for results and time. Know your real cost per hour.

  • Time includes briefing, sketches, drafts, changes and file preparation.
  • Hourly cost = yearly costs plus needed pay, divided by billable hours.
  • Compare hourly, per-piece and per-project pricing.
  • Software and licences are costs; count them.
  • Calculate your hourly break-even from yearly costs and your pay target.
  • Compare a per-project logo price with hourly pricing.
  • Draft a change policy: two rounds included, extra rounds charged by the hour.
Worked example. A logo takes 10 hours at $50: 10 x 50 = $500, plus $8 for fonts = $508. If two extra rounds take 3 hours, that is 3 x 50 = $150 more, so the total is $658.

Activity. Time three jobs and set a price for each.

Standard to meet. Task: price three jobs. Condition: from your own time log. Standard: hours, rate and total are shown and a mentor can follow every number.

Module 6 Prepare files, find clients and show proof 6 hCreate

Clients buy from designers they trust, and good files save everyone time.

  • Export the right formats: PDF for print, PNG or JPG for screens, vector for logos.
  • Name files clearly, for example client_project_version_date.
  • Build a portfolio of five pieces with a brief and result beside each.
  • Contact ten local businesses with a specific idea for each; keep a log.
  • Publish the portfolio as one page with a short caption under each piece, and ask clients for permission first.
  • Open every final file on a second device to test it.
  • Track messages, replies and jobs and work out your reply rate.
Worked example. You tell a bakery: 'Your menu board is hard to read from the door. Here is a quick redesign.' You name the file bakery_menu_v2_2026-03-01.pdf. If 10 messages bring 2 replies, the reply rate is 2 / 10 = 20%.

Activity. Build a portfolio and send ten tailored messages.

Standard to meet. Task: run a two-week outreach. Condition: consent-based contact and permission to show work. Standard: ten messages sent, replies logged and a lesson written.

Module 7 Protect ownership, files and quality 6 hEvaluate

A professional designer is careful with rights and checks.

  • Write who owns the final design and what the client may do with it. Ask a legal adviser for large deals.
  • Keep source files safe and backed up.
  • Check text, names, prices, dates and links before delivery.
  • Send print files to the printer only after the client has approved the final proof.
  • List where source files are backed up and how often.
  • Draft a sign-off line: the client approves text and prices in writing before print.
  • Check a small print proof for colour and trim.
Worked example. Your agreement says: 'The client owns the final artwork once paid in full. The designer may show it in a portfolio.' A final check of 12 items at 1 minute each takes 12 minutes, far less than the cost of reprinting.

Activity. Write an agreement template and a final check list.

Standard to meet. Task: produce a template and checklist. Condition: reviewed by a mentor. Standard: scope, fees, rounds, rights and payment dates are in plain words.

Module 8 Run a real design practice for 60 days 8 hCreate

Now you run it: take jobs, measure and decide.

  • Set a goal such as three paid jobs and one repeat client.
  • Record hours, income, changes asked for and late items each week.
  • Ask each client for feedback.
  • Choose: stop, change or grow, and write a 12-month plan.
  • Calculate your effective hourly rate as income divided by hours.
  • List the change rounds each client asked for.
  • Evaluate whether a higher rate would still win jobs.
Worked example. After 60 days: 3 clients, 28 hours, $1,400 billed (28 x $50). Your effective rate is 1,400 / 28 = $50 an hour. You raise your rate to $55 for new clients and limit change rounds.

Activity. Run the pilot and present a 12-month plan to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run a 60-day pilot. Condition: real clients and agreements. Standard: log complete and the decision uses numbers.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
