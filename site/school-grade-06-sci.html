Grade 06: Science

9 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

  • Apply Sort materials by properties and uses.
  • Apply Choose a method to separate a mixture.
  • Understand Classify changes as physical or chemical.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 06 Science, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 9 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

Unit 3 Separation of substances Weeks 9 to 12Apply

In this unit you work on 'Separation of substances', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: choose a method to separate a mixture. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Separation of substances' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: choose a method to separate a mixture. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Separate sand and salt: add water, filter (sand stays), evaporate the filtrate to get salt. Method: Model run for 'Separation of substances'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Make a mixture: 20 g of sand, 5 g of salt and 3 g of iron filings in a dish. Decide the order of separation by property: magnetic, solubility, particle size.
  2. Step 2, Use a magnet wrapped in thin paper to pull out the iron filings. This is magnetic separation. Weigh the filings: 3 g, so all recovered.
  3. Step 3, Add 100 mL of water and stir. Salt dissolves, sand does not. Filter through filter paper: sand stays on the paper as residue, salty water goes through as filtrate. Dry the sand and it weighs about 20 g.
  4. Step 4, Heat the filtrate in an evaporating dish on a low flame until crystals of salt form. This is evaporation. It returns about 5 g of salt. Water vapour escapes, so only salt remains.
  5. Step 5, Choose methods in general: handpicking for stones in rice, winnowing for husk and grain, sieving for flour and bran, sedimentation and decantation for muddy water, distillation for salt water into pure water.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: how do you separate chalk powder from water, and ink colours? Answer: chalk by filtration; ink colours by chromatography on filter paper, since each dye moves at a different speed.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Choose a method to separate a mixture: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 4 Changes around us Weeks 13 to 16Understand

In this unit you work on 'Changes around us', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: classify changes as physical or chemical. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Changes around us' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: classify changes as physical or chemical. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Physical change: ice melts and can freeze again. Chemical change: iron rusts and a new substance forms. Method: Model run for 'Changes around us'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Test ice. Melt an ice cube in a cup at 25 C: it becomes water, the same substance, and can freeze again. This is a physical change, reversible, with no new substance formed.
  2. Step 2, Burn a small piece of paper in a safe metal tray with adult help. It becomes ash and smoke and cannot return to paper. A new substance formed, so this is a chemical change.
  3. Step 3, Add 1 teaspoon of baking soda to 50 mL of vinegar in a bottle. It fizzes and gives carbon dioxide gas. Bubbles and a new gas show a chemical change. Test by lowering a lit splinter: it goes out.
  4. Step 4, Make a table with signs of chemical change: new colour, gas, heat or light, precipitate, new smell. Compare with physical changes: shape, size, state, no new substance. Dissolving salt in water is physical because evaporation brings the salt back.
  5. Step 5, Classify: rusting of iron chemical, curdling of milk chemical, cutting a vegetable physical, boiling water physical, cooking an egg chemical, folding paper physical.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: classify a rotting banana and crushing a can. Answer: rotting is chemical (new substances, smell, colour); crushing is physical (shape changes only).

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Classify changes as physical or chemical: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 8 Electricity and circuits Weeks 29 to 32Apply

In this unit you work on 'Electricity and circuits', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: draw circuit diagrams and test conductors. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Electricity and circuits' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: draw circuit diagrams and test conductors. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: V = I x R. A 6 V cell across a 2 ohm resistor gives I = 6 / 2 = 3 A. Method: Model run for 'Electricity and circuits'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Draw a circuit diagram using symbols: cell as long and short parallel lines, bulb as a circle with a cross, switch as a break with a lever, and lines for wires. Draw a loop with one cell, one bulb and one switch.
  2. Step 2, Draw two cells in series with the long line of one next to the short line of the other. Their voltages add: 1.5 + 1.5 = 3 V. A bulb rated 2.5 V glows brightly on 3 V.
  3. Step 3, Test materials in a gap tester: copper wire, aluminium foil, steel spoon, graphite pencil lead glow the bulb (conductors); plastic ruler, rubber band, dry wood, glass do not (insulators).
  4. Step 4, Record in a table with columns Material, Bulb glows, Type. Note that salt water also lights the bulb since dissolved salts carry charge, while pure dry sugar does not.
  5. Step 5, Add safety: never touch a damaged wire, keep water away from mains sockets, and use only cells in class experiments. An insulator on a plug covers the metal to prevent shocks.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: draw how a bulb, a battery and a switch connect, then say what happens to the lamp if the switch is open. Answer: the loop is broken so no current flows and the lamp is off.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Draw circuit diagrams and test conductors: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 9 Magnets, water and air Weeks 33 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Magnets, water and air', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: explain properties of magnets and the role of water and air. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Magnets, water and air' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: explain properties of magnets and the role of water and air. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: A magnet attracts iron, nickel and cobalt. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract. Method: Model run for 'Magnets, water and air'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Test a bar magnet on 7 items. It picks up a nail, a paper clip and a steel pin but not an aluminium can, plastic comb, copper coin or rubber. Magnets attract magnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
  2. Step 2, Find poles. Dip the bar in iron filings: most cling to the two ends, which are the poles. Hang the bar by a thread; it settles pointing north-south. The end pointing north is the north-seeking pole.
  3. Step 3, Test attraction and repulsion. Bring two north poles together and they push apart. Bring north to south and they pull together. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
  4. Step 4, Test air. Invert a glass over a floating cork in water; the water level inside stays lower, so air takes up space. Squeeze a closed balloon to show air can be compressed. Air also has about 21 per cent oxygen, which we need for breathing and burning.
  5. Step 5, Test water. Measure 100 mL of water in a tall and a wide glass: the same volume takes different shapes. Water dissolves salt and sugar and is needed for all life.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: which items in a pile of a rubber, a steel spoon, a coin of copper and an iron screw will a magnet lift? Answer: the steel spoon and the iron screw.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Explain properties of magnets and the role of water and air: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Using my five senses

On the slide: L1 Basic · Using my five senses

Notes: Name the five senses and use them to describe a safe object. Notes: place a leaf, a smooth stone and a soft cloth in a bag; learners touch and describe each. Never taste or smell unknown things; teach children to ask first. Grades 1 and 2.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Fair tests

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Fair tests

Notes: Describe a fair test by naming what is changed, what is measured and what stays the same. Notes: plant two seed pots, one in light and one in the dark, with equal water and soil. Learners say the one changed factor, and draw both pots after a week. Grades 3 and 4.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Measuring and recording (this course)

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Measuring and recording

Notes: Measure length, mass, time and temperature and record them in a table. Notes: a plant grows 2 cm in 4 days, which is 0.5 cm a day. Learners take three readings of 12, 14 and 13 mm, find the mean of 13, and say why repeats help. Grades 5 and 6.

This course: Grade 06 Science is at this level. Its units include Food: where it comes from and components; Fibres, fabrics and materials; Separation of substances; Changes around us and more.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Variables, graphs and density

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Variables, graphs and density

Notes: Apply a formula and draw a graph from a fair test. Notes: density equals mass over volume; a metal block of 54 g and 20 cm3 has density 2.7 g/cm3. Learners plot mass against volume for three blocks and read the gradient. Dry the benchtop before using water. Grades 7 and 8.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Evidence, anomalies and models

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Evidence, anomalies and models

Notes: Analyse data, spot an anomaly and judge how well a model fits the evidence. Notes: readings of 5.1, 5.3, 5.2 and 7.9 hold one anomaly at 7.9, so the mean of the rest is 5.2. Learners suggest a cause, repeat the trial and write a conclusion that cites the numbers. Grades 9 and 10.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Modelling and curve fitting

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Modelling and curve fitting

Notes: Apply least-squares fitting and error reasoning to link data to a model. Notes: the points (1,2.1), (2,3.9), (3,6.2), (4,7.8), (5,10.1) give slope 1.99 and intercept 0.05, with R squared 0.997. Learners explain what the intercept means and whether a straight line is right.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Study design and sample size

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Study design and sample size

Notes: Evaluate and create a study with a clear hypothesis, controls, sample size and ethics approval. Notes: to detect a 5-unit difference with spread 10 at 5 per cent significance and 80 per cent power needs about 63 per group. Learners write a pre-registered plan and list the risks and consent steps.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Canada (Toronto)

United States

Singapore

Australia

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Mexico (Central America)
