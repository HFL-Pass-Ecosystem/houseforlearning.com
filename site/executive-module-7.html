executive programme

Module 7: Global mobility, expatriate lifecycle economics and repatriation

A 2.5 ECTS executive module, 75 hours.

what you take

Module 7

  • Size

    2.5 ECTS, 75 hours of study.

  • Credit

    Counts in 15 countries. See the table.

how it is marked

Assessment

  • Written memo 30%
  • Live interactive simulation 35%
  • Boardroom presentation 35%

Marking bands: A, B or fail. Presentations use at most 10 slides, with a short live defence.

Weights shown follow the shared pattern for modules 3 to 14, and will be confirmed.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Cost an international assignment from start to return, decide whether it is worth it against a local hire, and plan the return so the person stays.

Condition

Given a pay package, home and host cost-of-living and tax data, and a case describing the employee and family.

Standard

Every cost line, tax figure and total is correct, the choice between assignment and local hire is shown in numbers, and the return plan names who does what by when.

Who it is for: People managers, human resources leaders and finance partners who send staff abroad or bring them home.

eight parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 Why firms send people abroad 9 hUnderstand: classify

People move abroad to fill a skill gap, build a market, share know-how or grow as leaders. The purpose decides the type and length of assignment.

  • Short-term: commonly under 1 year
  • Long-term: commonly 2 to 5 years
  • Commuter: home base, regular trips

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Three cases. A 6-month project fix: short-term, cost about 60,000 and one rotating engineer. A new office needing a 3-year leader: long-term. A weekly flight from city A to city B, 48 weeks a year at 900 each = 43,200 in fares: commuter. If the same flights would exceed 55,000 a year, a move is cheaper. Here fares are 43,200, so commuting stays.

Activity: Sort six described cases into assignment types and justify each by purpose and length.

Standard: Six cases correctly sorted with a purpose and a length for each.

Part 2 Building the cost of an assignment 10 hApply: calculate

The cost is much more than salary. Housing, school, allowances, support and moving all add up.

  • List every cost line
  • Add one-off and yearly costs
  • Express total as a multiple of base salary

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Base salary 90,000. Housing 36,000, school fees 20,000, hardship allowance 18,000, tax support 12,000 yearly = 86,000. Moving 15,000 once. Year 1 = 90,000 + 86,000 + 15,000 = 191,000. Multiple = 191,000 / 90,000 = 2.1 times salary. Years 2 and 3 cost 176,000 each. Three-year total = 191,000 + 176,000 + 176,000 = 543,000.

Activity: Cost a three-year assignment from a package table and show the multiple of salary in each year.

Standard: Each year's total is correct and the three-year sum matches.

Part 3 Cost-of-living and purchasing power 9 hApply: adjust pay

The same salary buys different amounts in different places. A cost-of-living index keeps the person whole.

  • Index home = 100
  • Adjusted pay = salary x host index / home index
  • Allowance = adjusted pay - salary, on the spending share

Worked example: Home index 100, host index 130. Only 60% of salary is spent on local goods, the rest is saved. Salary 90,000. Spending 54,000 x 130 / 100 = 70,200. Extra needed = 70,200 - 54,000 = 16,200. The allowance is 16,200 a year, not 27,000 (30% of the full salary). Applying the index to the whole salary would overpay by 10,800.

Activity: Calculate the cost-of-living allowance for two cities with different indexes and spending shares.

Standard: Both allowances correct and the over-payment risk of using full salary stated.

Part 4 Tax and social cover 9 hApply: calculate

Many firms keep the person's tax at the home level and pay the extra. This keeps the pay fair and makes costs predictable.

  • Hypothetical tax: what they would pay at home
  • Real tax: what is due in the host country
  • The firm pays the difference

Worked example: Taxable pay 150,000 including allowances. Example rates only: hypothetical home tax at 20% of 90,000 base = 18,000, which the employee keeps paying. Host tax at 35% of 150,000 = 52,500. Firm pays 52,500 - 18,000 = 34,500. The 34,500 replaces the 12,000 of tax support already in the 86,000 of other costs, so tax equalisation adds 34,500 - 12,000 = 22,500 a year to those costs. To keep later examples simple, modules 5 and 8 use the 543,000 total from module 2 without this extra and without the allowance from module 3.

Activity: Calculate the firm's tax cost for two assignments with different host rates.

Standard: Hypothetical tax, host tax and firm cost correct for both.

Part 5 Return on an assignment and the risk of failure 9 hEvaluate: judge

An assignment is an investment. We compare the value delivered with the cost and the chance it ends early.

  • Expected value = chance of success x value
  • Early return costs part of the package
  • Compare with the local hire option

Worked example: Value of the assignment if it works: new market revenue 900,000 over 3 years. Chance of success 85%. Expected value = 0.85 x 900,000 = 765,000. Cost 543,000. Early-failure chance 15% with 60% of cost wasted = 0.15 x 0.6 x 543,000 = 48,870 expected waste. Net expected = 765,000 - 543,000 - 48,870 = 173,130. Still worth doing.

Activity: Work out expected value, expected waste and net value for an assignment in a case.

Standard: All three figures correct and the decision stated.

Part 6 Family, partner and adjustment 9 hAnalyse: plan

Most early returns come from the family's struggle to settle. A plan for partner work, schooling and support lowers the risk.

  • Partner income lost
  • Child schooling and language
  • Stages: honeymoon, dip, recovery

Worked example: Partner earns 45,000 a year at home and cannot work in the host country. Over 3 years that is 135,000 lost. The firm offers a 20,000 yearly partner support grant = 60,000 over 3 years, covering 44% of the loss. Adjustment stages in months: honeymoon 0-3, dip 4-9, recovery 10-18. Check-in calls are planned at months 4, 6 and 8, all inside the dip (months 4 to 9).

Activity: Plan family support for a case with a partner income loss, schooling and three check-ins.

Standard: Loss and coverage percentage correct and check-ins placed in the dip months.

Part 7 Repatriation: the return 10 hAnalyse: assess

The return is often the weakest step. People come back changed, the job has changed, and many leave within two years.

  • Plan the next role before leaving
  • Value what was learned
  • Count the cost of losing the person

Worked example: Assume, as an example, that 25% of returners leave within 2 years. Of 20 returners, 5 leave. Replacement cost is 1.5 times salary at 90,000 = 135,000 each, so 5 x 135,000 = 675,000. A re-entry programme costing 3,000 a person (60,000) that cuts leavers from 5 to 2 saves 3 x 135,000 = 405,000. Net gain = 405,000 - 60,000 = 345,000.

Activity: Estimate leaver cost and the net gain from a re-entry programme in a case with 30 returners.

Standard: Number of leavers, replacement cost and net gain all correct.

Part 8 The assignment business case and policy 10 hCreate: write and defend

The case compares an assignment with a local hire over the same time and proposes a policy that the firm can apply fairly.

  • Compare three-year total cost
  • Add value and risk
  • Write a clear policy with limits

Worked example: Assignment: 543,000 over 3 years plus expected waste 48,870 and re-entry 3,000 = 594,870. Local hire at 110,000 a year plus 20,000 hiring = 350,000. Gap = 244,870. Extra value of the assignment needed to justify: knowledge transfer worth 300,000 over 3 years. Net = 300,000 - 244,870 = 55,130. Positive but thin, so policy: assign only if value exceeds 1.5 times the gap. Here 1.5 x 244,870 = 367,305, so this case would not pass.

Activity: Write a one-page case for an assignment against a local hire and present the policy rule in 5 minutes.

Standard: Both totals correct, value stated, a policy rule with a number, and a decision.

slides L1 to L7

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Why send people abroad. Fill a skill gap, build a market, share know-how or grow leaders. Purpose decides type and length. Speaker note: Say: ask learners to match purpose to type. Check: sort three cases. Mistake: sending people without a stated purpose.
  2. Slide 2: L2 The full cost. Salary is only the start. Housing, school, allowances, tax support and moving take the cost to about twice salary. Speaker note: Say: build a package line by line. Check: totals. Mistake: forgetting one-off moving costs.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Cost of living. Adjust only the spending part of pay by the host index. Speaker note: Say: show the 60% spending share. Check: compute the allowance. Mistake: adjusting the whole salary.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Tax equalisation. The person pays home-level tax. The firm pays any extra due abroad. Speaker note: Say: use the 150,000 case. Check: firm's cost. Mistake: forgetting tax on allowances.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Expected value and risk. Chance of success times value, less cost and expected waste, gives the net. Speaker note: Say: walk through 85% and 15%. Check: net value. Mistake: ignoring failure cost.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Family and return. Partner income, schooling and the dip months matter. Plan the next role before the person leaves. Speaker note: Say: mark the dip on a timeline. Check: check-in months. Mistake: planning the return only at the end.
  7. Slide 7: L7 The business case. Compare totals with a local hire, add value and risk, set a rule with a number. Speaker note: Say: show the gap and the value needed. Check: both totals. Mistake: comparing salary alone.
