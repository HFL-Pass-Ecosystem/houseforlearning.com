Start a Blog

Run a pilot of start a Blog in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small start a Blog offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: content, creative and marketing work. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know blog words and readers 6 hUnderstand

A blog is a website with posts. Start with the basic words and the people you write for.

  • Learn the terms: post, page, topic, domain, hosting, draft, publish, link, audience.
  • A niche is a topic you can write about for a long time.
  • Name one reader in one line: who they are, what they need, what worries them.
  • Read five blogs and list the topic, reader and how often they post.
  • Compare a blog, a newsletter and a social page, and say which fits your reader.
  • Write the promise of your blog in one sentence: what the reader gets.
  • List three questions your reader asks that you can answer from real experience.
Worked example. Niche: 'cheap healthy lunches for students'. Reader: a student with a small budget and 20 minutes. If bought lunch costs 12 and yours costs 4, the saving is 12 - 4 = 8 dollars per lunch.

Activity. Read five blogs and fill a table of topic, reader and post count. Write your niche in one line.

Standard to meet. Task: study five blogs. Condition: public pages only. Standard: table complete and niche fits one line.

Module 2 Explain how a post goes from idea to published 6 hApply

A post follows steps. If you can name them, you can write on a schedule.

  • The steps: idea, outline, draft, edit, check facts, add image, publish, share.
  • An outline lists the title, three to five headings and the one point of each.
  • Keep a list of ten ideas and choose one per week.
  • Set a fixed day each week to publish.
  • Batch your work: write two drafts in one sitting and schedule them.
  • Keep a checklist for each post: title, headings, links, image text and spelling.
  • Save every draft with a date so you can return to an earlier version.
Worked example. Idea: 'five lunches under five dollars'. Outline: intro, five lunches, shopping list, summary. If four steps (outline, draft, edit, publish) take 30 minutes each, 4 x 30 = 120 minutes, so one post a week fits two evenings.

Activity. Write an outline for three posts and a four-week calendar.

Standard to meet. Task: plan four weeks. Condition: with one post a week. Standard: each post has a title, outline and date.

Module 3 Write and publish a clear post 10 hApply

Now you apply the basics. Write a post that is clear, true and easy to scan.

  • Put the main point in the first two sentences.
  • Use short words, short paragraphs and clear headings.
  • Use your own photos or free-to-use images and say where they came from.
  • Read aloud and cut every word that does nothing.
  • Write a title that tells the reader what they get and test it on one friend.
  • Add short descriptive text for each image so a screen reader can read it.
  • Link to at least two reliable sources and check that each link opens.
Worked example. Before: 'Many people consider lunch an important meal.' (7 words, no fact). After: 'A good lunch costs less than five dollars. Here are five.' (10 words, one fact and one promise). The after version tells the reader what they get.

Activity. Write and publish your first 500-word post. Edit it twice.

Standard to meet. Task: publish one post. Condition: using a free blog tool. Standard: one clear point, own words, image source noted, no spelling errors.

Module 4 Follow Canadian rules for blogs 8 hEvaluate

Canada has rules for copyright, honest ads, privacy and email. Learn which touch your blog.

  • Copyright: do not copy other people's text or images without permission; quote briefly and say the source.
  • If you are paid or get a free product to write, tell readers clearly. Advertising must be truthful.
  • Collecting emails or using visitor data falls under privacy law; add a plain privacy page and ask consent.
  • Sending commercial email needs consent and an unsubscribe link; check the current anti-spam rules.
  • State who owns your photos and text and keep proof of when you made them.
  • Name any image licence you use and follow its terms.
  • Check the federal and provincial rules for contests and giveaways before you run one.
Worked example. A brand sends you a free pan. Your post says at the top: 'I received this pan for free.' The label comes before any link, so a reader sees it first. You keep the message from the brand in your records.

Activity. Build a table of rule, what you must do and where you checked for ten situations.

Standard to meet. Task: build a rules table. Condition: from official public pages. Standard: each row has a dated source; unclear cases say 'check the current rule with the local authority'.

Module 5 Understand costs and ways to earn 8 hApply

A blog can earn money slowly. Learn the costs and the income types.

  • Costs: domain name, hosting, email tool, image tool, your time.
  • Ways to earn: ads, affiliate links, your own products, paid services, sponsorship.
  • Earnings depend on visitors and trust, not only on posts.
  • Blog income is usually taxable in Canada; keep records and check the tax rules with the tax agency.
  • List which costs are one-time and which repeat each month or year.
  • Work out how many sales you need to cover monthly costs.
  • Ask whether you would write the blog if it earned nothing, and note the answer.
Worked example. 1,000 visitors a month, 2 percent buy a 15 dollar guide: 1,000 x 0.02 = 20 sales, and 20 x 15 = 300 dollars. If monthly costs are 10 dollars, 300 - 10 = 290 left. Break-even is 10 / 15 = 0.67, so one sale covers the costs. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build a one-page budget and an income forecast for six months.

Standard to meet. Task: build a forecast. Condition: with simple spreadsheet. Standard: costs, income types and visitor assumptions all stated.

Module 6 Find readers and grow 6 hCreate

Readers find you through search, sharing and email. Build a steady way to be found.

  • Write titles that match what people type into a search box.
  • Link between your own posts and to reliable sources.
  • Share on one or two social places where your readers are.
  • Offer a simple email sign-up with consent and track the numbers.
  • Check which search words appear in your title, first paragraph and headings.
  • Reply to every comment within two days and thank people who share.
  • Rewrite your oldest post with new facts and link it to newer posts.
Worked example. Title 'How to meal-prep lunches for a week' matches a real search. A post titled 'Lunch ideas' is too vague. If 5 percent of 500 visitors sign up, 500 x 0.05 = 25 sign-ups.

Activity. Publish four posts in a month and track visits, sign-ups and shares.

Standard to meet. Task: grow readers. Condition: with real posts and a free analytics tool. Standard: numbers logged weekly and one lesson written.

Module 7 Protect your readers, your work and your accounts 6 hEvaluate

A professional blogger protects privacy, ownership and sign-ins.

  • Use strong unique passwords and two-step sign-in.
  • Back up posts and images every month.
  • Do not share personal details about other people without consent.
  • Check facts, names and links before publishing, and fix errors openly.
  • Keep a list of every account you use and who can sign in.
  • Remove personal details such as house numbers from photos before posting.
  • Write down how you would restore the site from a backup and test it once.
Worked example. A reader points out a wrong price. You fix it and add: 'Updated: price corrected.' If you back up on the first of each month, the most you can lose is one month of posts, which is 4 posts at one a week.

Activity. Write a privacy page, a one-page content policy and a monthly backup checklist.

Standard to meet. Task: write three documents. Condition: reviewed by a mentor. Standard: each is in plain words and dated.

Module 8 Run a real pilot and decide 8 hCreate

Now you run it for real. Publish, measure and decide how to grow.

  • Set a 60-day goal, such as eight posts and 500 visitors.
  • Every week record posts, visitors, sign-ups and time spent.
  • Ask five readers what they liked and what to change.
  • At the end choose: stop, change or grow, and write a 12-month plan.
  • Set a weekly time budget in hours and record the real hours.
  • Identify the post that drew the most visitors and say why.
  • Write the one thing you will stop doing.
Worked example. After 60 days: 8 posts, 620 visitors, 25 sign-ups. 25 / 620 is about 4 percent of visitors signing up. You decide to focus on the two posts that drew most readers.

Activity. Run the pilot, keep the weekly log and present a 12-month plan to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run a 60-day pilot. Condition: real posts and real readers. Standard: log complete, decision uses numbers, a panel can repeat the plan.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
