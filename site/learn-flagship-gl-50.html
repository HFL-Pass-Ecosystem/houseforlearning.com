Skincare Spa and Clinic Management

Plan and run a small skincare spa or clinic: services, hygiene, consent, scheduling, products, pricing and client care.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner37:00 h of lessons plus practice and sharingSelf-paced with peer circle
  • Understand Explain service menus, scope of practice and hygiene rules.
  • Apply Run client intake, consent and patch-test steps.
  • Analyse Build a schedule and cost per treatment.
  • Evaluate Judge product claims and ingredient labels.
  • Create Launch a 30-day pilot with a client-care review.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and pilot a small skincare spa service menu with hygiene plan, consent forms, pricing, schedule and client feedback log.

Condition

A practice room or real licensed space, a spreadsheet, two peers and a licensed practitioner as mentor. Medical treatments are outside scope.

Standard

Hygiene checklist passes; every service has a scope note; costs and price are shown; consent and patch-test steps are recorded; peers rate Meets or better.

Who it is for: Beauty therapists, spa owners, front-desk staff, small clinic managers. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, quiz, tracker.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Service menu and scope of practice 4 hUnderstand

A spa may sell only what its staff are trained and allowed to do locally. Each menu line needs a named, qualified person.

  • Sort ideas into comfort (massage, cleansing facial), cosmetic care (light exfoliation, masks) and medical or invasive work (injections, deep peels, mole care); keep the third band off the menu.
  • For each service write the training needed, the certificate held and who performs it, and confirm this with the local licensing office.
  • Print a short 'not offered' list so clients know skin disease and prescription care go to a doctor.
  • Set a referral rule: a changing mole, lump, bleeding or open wound means stop and send the client to a doctor that day.
Worked example. A spa drafts 10 services: 7 comfort or cosmetic, 3 medical (deep peel, mole check, scar needling). The 3 are cut, each of the 7 gets a named therapist, and a client with a darkening mole is referred the same day.

Activity. Write a menu of eight services with duration, scope note, qualification and named person, plus a 'not offered' list of three.

Standard to meet. All eight services have a qualification, a named person and a referral line; no medical treatment appears.

Module 2 Hygiene and infection control 5 hApply

Clean practice protects clients and must be provable: a checklist signed daily is evidence, a promise is not.

  • Wash hands for 20 seconds before and after each client, use fresh towels, and bin single-use items after one use.
  • Clean tools with detergent, then disinfect or sterilise by the label's method and contact time; log start, end and initials.
  • Wipe the bed, trolley and handles between clients, keep clean and used items in separate covered boxes, and use a lined, closed bin.
  • Do an opening and a closing check of soap, water, stock, bins and laundry, and keep logs as long as local rules require.
Worked example. At 9:00 the opener checks soap, towels and bins and signs. A tool tray runs a 20-minute cycle from 9:10, is dried and stored covered by 9:40. After 6 clients the log has 18 entries (6 hand-washes, 6 bed wipes, 6 trays), no blanks.

Activity. Write a one-page hygiene checklist and daily log, run it for three practice clients and sign every line.

Standard to meet. Every step has a time and an initials box; three practice days show no blank lines.

Module 3 Client intake, consent and patch tests 5 hApply

Know the client before touching skin: intake finds risks, consent records understanding, a patch test checks a product first.

  • Ask about allergies, medicines, pregnancy, skin conditions and recent procedures; if any answer worries you, pause and ask your qualified mentor.
  • Where the maker advises, test a small amount behind the ear or on the inner forearm and read it at 24 to 48 hours; redness, itch or swelling means do not use it.
  • Explain steps, risks and aftercare in plain words, take written consent, and let the client say no at any time.
  • Keep cards locked or password-protected and follow local privacy rules.
Worked example. A client says she is allergic to tree nuts. The therapist sets aside an almond-oil blend, writes 'nut allergy' in red on the card and uses grape-seed oil. A patch test done 4 June is read 6 June as clear and signed.

Activity. Design a one-page intake and consent form, fill it for a practice client and record a patch test with two dated readings.

Standard to meet. Form covers allergy, medicines, pregnancy, skin condition, consent and patch result; the card has no blank field.

Module 4 Products and ingredients 5 hEvaluate

Choose products by label and evidence, not promise. A good buyer reads, judges a claim and says no.

  • Read ingredients from the top (most to least) and flag fragrance, essential oils, alcohol, strong acids and nut oils.
  • Reject claims like 'erases scars in a week' or 'cures acne'; cosmetics generally may not claim to treat disease. Ask for batch number and expiry.
  • Write the opening date on each jar, discard by the open-jar symbol, and store away from heat and sun.
  • Buy from suppliers who give an invoice and batch record, and keep a sample card per product to trace reactions.
Worked example. A night cream lists fragrance second and claims 'erases scars in 7 days'. The buyer rejects the claim, avoids the fragrance for sensitive skin and picks a fragrance-free cream with a 12-month symbol. The note is dated 9 June.

Activity. Review three product labels and write a safety note for each: allergens, claim check, storage and keep or reject.

Standard to meet. Each note names allergens, storage and expiry rules and a decision; no unproven claim is accepted.

Module 5 Scheduling and capacity 4 hAnalyse

Time is what a spa sells. Count room time and staff time separately or the diary over-promises.

  • Room time = service + cleaning + buffer: a 60-minute facial with 15 minutes cleaning and 5 buffer holds a room 80 minutes.
  • Staff time adds breaks and notes, and one therapist is never booked into two rooms at once.
  • Chart past bookings by hour, put most staff on busy hours and keep one open slot a day for overruns.
  • Send reminders 24 hours ahead, measure no-shows as a percent of bookings and publish a fair policy.
Worked example. A day of 480 minutes less a 60-minute break leaves 420 per therapist. At 80 minutes a facial that is 5 each, so two therapists and rooms give 10 facials, not 12. If 2 are no-shows the rate is 20 percent.

Activity. Build a one-day schedule for two staff and two rooms with six treatments, cleaning, breaks and one open slot.

Standard to meet. No therapist or room overlaps, cleaning follows every client, and totals match the room-time formula.

Module 6 Pricing and profit 5 hEvaluate

A price must cover every cost and leave a margin; copying a rival's menu hides losses.

  • List costs per treatment: staff time, products, laundry, rent and utilities share, booking and card fees, insurance.
  • Margin = price minus cost; margin percent = margin divided by price. Set a target such as 40 to 55 percent and compare nearby prices.
  • Test discounts first: a 10 percent package cut leaves cost unchanged, so margin percent falls faster.
  • Review prices every quarter and tell clients of changes in advance.
Worked example. Facial costs (all figures are estimates in INR; check your market): staff 300, products 80, laundry 30, rent 60, fees 30, insurance 20 = 520 INR. Price 1,100 INR gives margin 580 INR (53 percent). A 10 percent package discount gives a price of 990 INR and leaves 470 INR (47 percent), above the 40 percent target.

Activity. Cost five services line by line, set prices with currency shown, show margin percent and test one package discount.

Standard to meet. All totals add up; margin and margin percent shown for each service; discount test is correct.

Module 7 Client care and reactions 4 hApply

Calm, kind service builds trust and a protocol handles problems. Staff care and call a qualified person; they do not diagnose.

  • Greet by name, explain the steps, check comfort at the start and midpoint, and give written aftercare.
  • On a reaction: stop, rinse with cool water, keep the client seated, record product, time and area, then call your mentor or qualified practitioner.
  • If lips or face swell, breathing is hard or the rash spreads fast, call local emergency services at once.
  • Log each complaint with date, facts and fix, reply within two days and phone clients 48 hours after a new treatment.
Worked example. At 11:20 a client reports burning. Staff stop, rinse for 5 minutes, record the batch and time and phone the mentor at 11:30, who advises a doctor. The log is complete by 11:45 and a follow-up call is made next day.

Activity. Write a one-page reaction protocol with contact numbers and role-play it twice with a peer, timing each step.

Standard to meet. Steps are in order, numbers are listed and each role-play ends with a complete record.

Module 8 Pilot and improve 5 hCreate

Run 30 days of real service on a small scale, on purpose, so mistakes stay cheap and lessons stay clear.

  • Track weekly bookings, rebookings as a percent of clients, complaints and product waste in cost.
  • Rank issues by frequency and seriousness, then fix the top three with a named owner and date.
  • Plan fair pay, breaks and safe disposal of waste under local rules.
  • Collect a three-question card (comfort, results, would you return) and record scores honestly.
Worked example. In 30 days 60 clients visit and 19 rebook (32 percent). Aftercare cards added in week 2 lift rebooking to 45 percent by week 4. Smaller sample jars cut the cost of waste from 2,500 to 2,200 INR, down 12 percent.

Activity. Present a pilot review with four numbers, three dated fixes, feedback scores and a 90-day plan to peers.

Standard to meet. Numbers are real and totals match; three fixes are dated; the feedback average is shown.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: service menu, hygiene plan, intake and consent forms, price sheet, pilot log. Two peers and one mentor mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet'.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why list scope on a service menu?

Model answer: To keep every service within the staff's training, insurance and local licence, and to show clients which medical concerns go to a doctor. (Covered in module 1: Service menu and scope of practice. To pass that module: All eight services have a qualification, a named person and a referral line; no medical treatment appears.)

Q2. Name three hygiene steps between clients.

Model answer: Wash hands, wipe the bed and handles with disinfectant, use fresh towels and single-use items, and log the clean with time and initials. (Covered in module 2: Hygiene and infection control. To pass that module: Every step has a time and an initials box; three practice days show no blank lines.)

Q3. What is a patch test and when is it read?

Model answer: A small amount of product on a small area of skin, such as behind the ear, read after 24 to 48 hours; redness, itch or swelling means do not use it. (Covered in module 3: Client intake, consent and patch tests. To pass that module: Form covers allergy, medicines, pregnancy, skin condition, consent and patch result; the card has no blank field.)

Q4. Name two common allergens or irritants in skincare.

Model answer: Fragrance and nut or seed oils; essential oils and alcohol can also irritate, so read the label from the top. (Covered in module 4: Products and ingredients. To pass that module: Each note names allergens, storage and expiry rules and a decision; no unproven claim is accepted.)

Q5. What do you do first when a client has a reaction?

Model answer: Stop the treatment, rinse with cool water, keep the client calm, record product, time and area, and call a qualified person; call emergency services if lips swell or breathing is hard. (Covered in module 7: Client care and reactions. To pass that module: Steps are in order, numbers are listed and each role-play ends with a complete record.)

Q6. What is room time and how do you work it out?

Model answer: Service time plus cleaning plus buffer, so a 60-minute facial with 15 minutes of cleaning and 5 of buffer takes 80 minutes. (Covered in module 5: Scheduling and capacity. To pass that module: No therapist or room overlaps, cleaning follows every client, and totals match the room-time formula.)

Q7. Which costs go into a treatment price?

Model answer: Staff time, products, laundry, a share of rent and utilities, booking and card fees, and insurance, then a margin on top. (Covered in module 6: Pricing and profit. To pass that module: All totals add up; margin and margin percent shown for each service; discount test is correct.)

Q8. Why are miracle claims a problem?

Model answer: They mislead clients, cannot be proven and may break local advertising and cosmetic rules, since cosmetics generally may not claim to treat disease. (Covered in module 4: Products and ingredients. To pass that module: Each note names allergens, storage and expiry rules and a decision; no unproven claim is accepted.)

Q9. What do you track in a 30-day pilot?

Model answer: Bookings, rebookings as a percent, complaints and product waste, plus client feedback scores, then fix the top three issues with dates. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and improve. To pass that module: Numbers are real and totals match; three fixes are dated; the feedback average is shown.)

Q10. When must you refer a client to a doctor?

Model answer: When there is a changing mole, lump, bleeding, sudden rash, open wound or any medical concern; stop and refer the same day. (Covered in module 1: Service menu and scope of practice. To pass that module: All eight services have a qualification, a named person and a referral line; no medical treatment appears.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Beauty and clinic licensing differ by place; confirm what each person may do.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Write numbers in INR. Personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to name in your evidence: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Consumer rule: Consumer Protection Act 2019.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Write numbers in EUR. Personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: EU and Dutch consumer rules (14-day cooling-off for online sales).

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Write numbers in AED. Personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Write numbers in BRL. Personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Consumer rule: the Consumer Defence Code (CDC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Write numbers in CAD. Personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Write numbers in USD. Personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to name in your evidence: state and community-college credit systems. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Write numbers in SGD. Personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Australia

Language: English. Write numbers in AUD. Personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Write numbers in HKD. Personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Write numbers in GEL or ILS. Personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national qualifications framework of that country. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Write numbers in KYD. Personal data rule: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the local education and training framework. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Write numbers in MXN. Personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to name in your evidence: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Consumer rule: local consumer-protection law.
