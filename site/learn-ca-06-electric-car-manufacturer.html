Electric car manufacturer

Run a pilot of electric car manufacturer in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small electric car manufacturer offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Understand electric vehicle markets 6 hUnderstand

Electric vehicles are sold as cars, vans, buses and special vehicles.

  • Segments include personal vehicles, delivery vans, buses, trucks and small special vehicles.
  • Customers include fleets, cities and households; fleets care most about total cost of ownership.
  • Incentives and rules change, so check the current federal and provincial programmes before you quote any figure.
  • Pick one niche, such as delivery vans in cold climates, and write why it suits you.
  • Ride in or drive one electric vehicle for a week and note range loss in cold weather.
  • Interview five fleet managers: daily distance, depot parking, power at the depot and what they pay now per kilometre.
  • Total cost of ownership = purchase price + running costs over the years - resale value. Write it for one vehicle you would replace.
Worked example. A delivery fleet compares 10-year cost: if an electric van costs 15,000 more to buy but saves 3,000 a year in fuel and servicing, the saving over 10 years is 30,000, so the van is ahead by 15,000 and pays back in 15,000 / 3,000 = 5 years. Teaching figures in CAD; use the fleet's real numbers.

Activity. Write a niche statement and customer profile.

Standard to meet. Niche is narrow and testable.

Module 2 Know vehicle rules 6 hApply

Vehicles must meet safety and environmental standards.

  • Federal motor-vehicle safety standards apply before a vehicle can be sold.
  • Battery transport and storage follow dangerous-goods rules, so check the federal and provincial requirements before you ship or store packs.
  • Provinces register and insure vehicles, so check what each province requires before a vehicle goes on the road.
  • Warranty and recall rules protect buyers; check which federal and provincial rules apply to you.
  • Check charging time on a home plug, a public charger and a fast charger, and compare with spec.
  • A vehicle built in small numbers may have different import or approval steps; check the federal and provincial rule for your case.
  • Keep a certificate file with the standard, the test result, the date and the person who signed.
Worked example. Row: 'Sell a new vehicle | federal safety standards | certify and label | before first sale | certificate on file'. For a 60 kWh battery on a 7 kW home charger, a full charge takes about 60 / 7 = 8.6 hours, which you compare with the stated spec.

Activity. Build a rules table for a small manufacturer.

Standard to meet. Each row has source and date.

Module 3 Design the vehicle systems 10 hApply

Work from needs.

  • Define the use first: range, payload, charging time and cold-weather performance.
  • Size the battery from the energy the vehicle uses per kilometre and the range you need.
  • Plan safety: crash structure, thermal management and battery protection.
  • Document the specifications in one table that everyone can read.
  • List the five parts that fail most often in fleet use and plan spares.
  • Add a margin for ageing and cold weather to the pack size, and state the margin you used.
  • Check payload: weight of the battery, body, driver and cargo against the vehicle's rated limit.
Worked example. Need 250 km at 0.35 kWh per km: 250 x 0.35 = 87.5 kWh usable. Add 20% for cold and ageing: 87.5 x 1.2 = 105 kWh pack. If the cells give 0.15 kWh per kg, the cells alone weigh 105 / 0.15 = 700 kg, which must fit the payload plan.

Activity. Calculate pack size for a chosen use.

Standard to meet. Calculation shown with margin.

Module 4 Cost the product 8 hEvaluate

Cost per vehicle drives the business.

  • Build a bill of materials that lists the battery, motor, body and electronics with a price for each.
  • Add assembly labour and facility cost per vehicle.
  • Add a warranty provision per vehicle.
  • Work out margin and break-even from price, cost and fixed cost.
  • Study one crash test report and note what the structure does.
  • Ask three suppliers for a quote at 100, 500 and 1,000 units and note how the price falls with volume.
  • Test a 10% rise in battery cost and show the new margin.
Worked example. Price 48,000; cost 36,000; margin 12,000 (25%); fixed cost 3.1M per year gives 3,100,000 / 12,000 = 258.3, so break-even is 259 vehicles, shown as about 260. If battery cost is 14,000 of the 36,000, a 10% rise adds 1,400, so cost is 37,400, margin 10,600 and break-even rises to about 293. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build a bill of materials and break-even.

Standard to meet. Units and currency shown.

Module 5 Build to quality 8 hApply

Quality means testing.

  • Inspect incoming parts before they enter the build.
  • Run end-of-line tests on brakes, battery, software and leaks.
  • Trace every vehicle by its number.
  • Take corrective action and record it whenever a test fails.
  • Interview a fleet mechanic about what is hard to repair.
  • Set a pass limit for each test and a sign-off line, so no vehicle leaves without a signed sheet.
  • Count first-pass rate (vehicles that pass the first time divided by vehicles tested) and chart it weekly.
Worked example. End-of-line fail rate 4% mostly connectors; fix with a torque check. At 260 vehicles a year, 4% is about 10 vehicles needing rework; if the torque check halves the fails to 2%, rework drops to about 5.

Activity. Design an end-of-line test sheet.

Standard to meet. Sheet has limits and sign-off.

Module 6 Sell and service 6 hCreate

Service builds trust.

  • Sell through fleets and dealers, and write which one you will start with.
  • Build a service network and a parts supply for your customers.
  • Train technicians before the first vehicles are delivered.
  • Use remote monitoring to spot faults before the customer calls.
  • Write down the software update promise and who delivers it.
  • Plan service in cold regions: where a customer can get help in winter and how long the repair takes.
  • Agree a response time for a stopped vehicle and measure it.
Worked example. Fleet pilot: 3 vans for 90 days; uptime 97%. The 3 vans have 3 x 90 = 270 van-days, and 3% down is about 8 van-days lost, which you compare with the offer's target.

Activity. Plan a fleet pilot offer.

Standard to meet. Offer has measures.

Module 7 Manage safety and supply risk 6 hEvaluate

Batteries and supply chains carry risk.

  • Prepare a battery fire plan and train staff in it.
  • Use more than one supplier for key parts so that one failure does not stop production.
  • Plan material sourcing and the recycling of batteries.
  • Protect connected vehicles from cyber attack with updates and access controls.
  • Compare the weight of your design with two similar vehicles.
  • Ask each supplier where its materials come from and how they are recycled; note anything you cannot verify.
  • Write who you call, and what staff do, in the first ten minutes of a battery incident, and practise it.
Worked example. Risk: 'Cell supplier delay | 4 x 4 = 16 | second source | purchasing | monthly'. A score of 16 out of 25 is high, so the second supplier should be qualified before volume production.

Activity. Write a risk register.

Standard to meet. Rows have owners.

Module 8 Pilot and plan scale 8 hCreate

Prove with a small series.

  • Build five pilot vehicles.
  • Measure range, faults and customer feedback.
  • Set a stop rule: if range is under 85% of spec, redesign.
  • Plan funding and the plant for the next stage.
  • Record every test in one table: date, condition, result, action.
  • Measure range in cold weather, in warm weather and loaded, not only on a good day.
  • Check programme rules and dates for funding yourself, and compare leasing a plant with building one.
Worked example. Range 92% of spec: if the spec is 250 km, 0.92 x 250 = 230 km, above the stop line of 0.85 x 250 = 212.5 km, so proceed. A cold-weather test that gave 200 km would be 80% and trigger a redesign.

Activity. Run the pilot review.

Standard to meet. Review has decision.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What decides battery size?

Model answer: Energy per kilometre times range with a margin. (Covered in module 3: Design the vehicle systems. To pass that module: Calculation shown with margin.)

Q2. Why add a margin to battery size?

Model answer: Cold weather and ageing reduce capacity. (Covered in module 3: Design the vehicle systems. To pass that module: Calculation shown with margin.)

Q3. What is total cost of ownership?

Model answer: Purchase plus running costs over time. (Covered in module 1: Understand electric vehicle markets. To pass that module: Niche is narrow and testable.)

Q4. Who sets vehicle safety standards?

Model answer: The federal government. (Covered in module 2: Know vehicle rules. To pass that module: Each row has source and date.)

Q5. Why trace by vehicle number?

Model answer: To find and fix faults. (Covered in module 5: Build to quality. To pass that module: Sheet has limits and sign-off.)

Q6. What is an end-of-line test?

Model answer: A final check before delivery. (Covered in module 5: Build to quality. To pass that module: Sheet has limits and sign-off.)

Q7. Why diversify suppliers?

Model answer: To avoid a single point of failure. (Covered in module 7: Manage safety and supply risk. To pass that module: Rows have owners.)

Q8. What is break-even?

Model answer: Fixed cost divided by margin. (Covered in module 4: Cost the product. To pass that module: Units and currency shown.)

Q9. What triggers redesign in the pilot?

Model answer: Range under 85% of spec. (Covered in module 8: Pilot and plan scale. To pass that module: Review has decision.)

Q10. Why train technicians?

Model answer: So customers get safe, correct service from the first delivery. (Covered in module 6: Sell and service. To pass that module: Offer has measures.)

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
