flagship course · GL-30

Coworking Space Operations

Run a small shared workspace: set layout and membership, price fairly, keep it safe and welcoming, and measure use and income.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Run a small shared workspace: set layout and membership, price fairly, keep it safe and welcoming, and measure use and income.

  • Understand Explain coworking models and who uses them.
  • Apply Plan a layout and house rules.
  • Analyse Analyse occupancy, price and costs to find break-even.
  • Create Create a membership offer and community routine.
  • Evaluate Evaluate safety, satisfaction and finance monthly.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan a 20-seat coworking space and run a 4-week pilot or full simulation with pricing, rules, a safety check and a community plan.

Condition

A real or practice room plan, a spreadsheet, floor-plan sketch, a mentor with property or hospitality experience and a peer circle. About 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Break-even occupancy calculated; fire and safety checks done with a local adviser; rules published; member satisfaction 4 of 5 or better; cleaning and maintenance logs kept; accessibility checked.

Who it is for: Property managers, community leaders, entrepreneurs, hospitality staff, students. Requirements: None. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Meet building, fire and accessibility rules for your place. Keep exits clear. Insure the space. Protect members' belongings and data.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What coworking is 4 hUnderstand

Shared space with flexible terms. You will be able to define your target members and test whether enough of them exist to cover your space's costs.

  • Hot desk, fixed desk, private room, meeting room.
  • Who uses it: freelancers, small teams, remote staff.
  • What they value: price, quiet, community, internet.
  • Local needs differ.
  • Define who your members will be, such as freelancers and small teams, and survey ten of them about what they need before you choose a space.
  • Calculate how many members you need to cover rent and running costs, for example 24 desks at an average of 120 a month, and check that figure is realistic locally.
  • Visit two coworking spaces and note what members do.
  • Check sockets, light and air flow at busy times.
Worked example. Hot desk 60 a month, flexible desk 80, fixed desk 130, private room 400 for four (estimates in local currency units; check your own market).

Activity. Interview five possible members.

Standard to meet. Notes show top three needs.

Common mistake. Building for yourself instead of members.

Second worked example. Interviews: five freelancers rank quiet zone, fast internet and price first; coffee comes last. The layout follows the answers.

Check yourself. What do your members rank first?

Module 2 Space and layout 6 hUnderstand

Layout drives comfort and safety. You will be able to plan desks, quiet zones and social areas, and check power and connection quality before furnishing.

  • Zones: quiet, social, calls, meeting.
  • Desk area per person and walkways.
  • Power, light, air, sound.
  • Accessible routes.
  • Allow roughly 3 to 5 square metres per desk including walkways, include a quiet zone, a phone booth and a social area, and sketch the plan before buying any furniture.
  • Check power sockets, internet speed and lighting at every desk, and test the connection at the busiest time of day.
  • List quiet rules and test them with members.
  • Plan a first-aid kit and a trained person.
Worked example. 20 seats at 3 square metres = 60 plus 25 for walkways and 20 for meeting = 105 square metres.

Activity. Draw a layout.

Standard to meet. Walkways and exits marked.

Common mistake. Forgetting exits and capacity.

Second worked example. A room for 12 desks needs 36 square metres for the desks, 15 for walkways and 12 for a booth and storage: 63 in all.

Check yourself. What is your capacity?

Module 3 Rules, safety and insurance 8 hApply

Safe, calm and fair. You will be able to set house rules, mark safety equipment and organise the insurance and local-rule paperwork a shared space needs.

  • Fire exits, alarms, extinguishers.
  • Capacity limit.
  • House rules: noise, guests, cleaning, hours.
  • Insurance and liability notes.
  • Write simple house rules on noise, guests, cleaning and opening hours, and mark the fire exits and a first-aid kit where everyone can see them.
  • Ask a qualified adviser which insurance cover and local rules apply to your space, keep every document in one folder, and review them each year.
  • Check accessibility of doors, toilets and desks.
  • Keep a visitors' log.
Worked example. A sign lists the maximum 24 people and the nearest exits.

Activity. Write rules and a safety checklist with an adviser.

Standard to meet. Adviser signs off.

Common mistake. No rules on noise.

Second worked example. A notice shows the 18-person limit, the fire door and the first-aid box; the monthly check of extinguishers is logged.

Check yourself. Which zone solves noise?

Module 4 Costs and pricing 8 hApply

Know break-even. You will be able to find your break-even price per desk and compare the margins of day, hot-desk and fixed-desk tiers.

  • Fixed costs: rent, internet, power, cleaning, staff.
  • Variable: coffee, supplies.
  • Break-even members = fixed costs / price per member.
  • Add a margin for empty seats.
  • List all monthly costs, including rent, utilities, internet, cleaning and supplies, and divide the total by the number of desks to find your minimum price per desk.
  • Offer clear tiers such as day pass, hot desk and fixed desk, and compare the margin of each tier so that no plan loses money.
  • Write a welcome sheet with Wi-Fi and rules.
  • Plan storage for members' belongings.
Worked example. Fixed 1,800; price 120: 15 members, which is 75% of 20 seats; meeting-room income lowers it.

Activity. Build a pricing sheet.

Standard to meet. Break-even computed with currency.

Common mistake. Pricing from guesswork.

Second worked example. Fixed 2,000; day price 15: 134 day passes a month break even; adding 10 monthly members at 90 cuts that to 74.

Check yourself. What is your break-even?

Module 5 Memberships and booking 8 hAnalyse

Easy to join, easy to leave. You will be able to run fair room booking and write a membership agreement that makes joining and leaving simple.

  • Plans with clear terms.
  • Booking tool for rooms.
  • Access: keys or codes; log who enters.
  • Payment and invoices.
  • Use one booking tool where members reserve rooms in one-hour blocks, and set a fair limit, such as four free hours a week of meeting-room use.
  • Write a simple membership agreement stating the notice period, payment date and house rules, and give each new member a welcome pack and a short tour.
  • Add the space to a local map listing.
  • Keep a cleaning log by the door.
Worked example. Day pass 12; week 50; month 120.

Activity. Draft plans and a booking process.

Standard to meet. Plans clear in one page.

Common mistake. Hard-to-leave contracts.

Second worked example. A flexible desk is 80 a month, a fixed desk 130 and a private room 400; a plan can be paused once a year.

Check yourself. How easy is leaving?

Module 6 Community and events 10 hCreate

People stay for people. You will be able to plan a monthly event and introductions that turn separate desk users into a connected community.

  • Welcome routine.
  • Monthly event or skill share.
  • Notice board and group chat.
  • Handle conflicts quickly.
  • Hold one regular event each month, such as a lunch talk, and ask members to suggest topics so the programme reflects what they want.
  • Introduce each new member to two existing members in their first week, and keep a notice board or group chat for requests and offers.
  • Offer a trial day for new members.
  • Ask for one idea from each member every month.
Worked example. Monday coffee and Friday demo: members report knowing five others.

Activity. Plan a month of events.

Standard to meet. Plan fits budget.

Common mistake. Events nobody wants.

Second worked example. A Wednesday skills lunch draws 14 members; six say they found a client or partner through it in two months.

Check yourself. Which event will you run?

Module 7 Operations 6 hEvaluate

The dull parts matter. You will be able to run daily checklists and a repair log so the space stays clean, stocked and dependable.

  • Cleaning schedule and supplies.
  • Maintenance log.
  • Internet checks and backup link.
  • Opening and closing checklist.
  • Create a daily checklist covering opening, cleaning, supplies and locking up, and rotate who signs it off so no duty is missed.
  • Log every repair and complaint with its date, aim to fix urgent items within 24 hours, and tell the affected member what was done.
  • Review pricing every quarter.
  • Check insurance and licence renewals.
Worked example. Speed tested daily: 100 Mbit/s target; 2 dips fixed with a router change.

Activity. Keep logs for two weeks.

Standard to meet. Logs complete.

Common mistake. Cleaning logs missing.

Second worked example. A cleaning log is signed twice a day; a missed Thursday check is fixed by a reminder on the manager's phone.

Check yourself. Where is the log?

Module 8 Measure and improve 4 hCreate

Numbers and voices. You will be able to track four monthly numbers and act on member feedback to improve occupancy and retention.

  • Occupancy = seat-days used / seat-days available.
  • Revenue per seat.
  • Survey: satisfaction and needs.
  • Plan 90 days.
  • Track occupancy, revenue per desk, member churn and satisfaction each month, and show the same four numbers in one simple chart.
  • Ask members for feedback twice a year, act on the most common suggestion, and report back on what you changed and what you could not.
  • Prepare for outages with a backup link.
  • Write a 90-day plan with occupancy and satisfaction targets.
Worked example. 420 of 600 seat-days = 70%; revenue per seat 98 for the month; satisfaction 4.3 out of 5.

Activity. Report on the pilot.

Standard to meet. Three changes with dates.

Common mistake. Survey but no action.

Second worked example. 250 of 400 desk-days used = 62.5%; revenue per desk 71; satisfaction 4.5. Action: extend evening hours to lift use.

Check yourself. What did you change?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The noisy caller

A member takes calls in the quiet zone.

Question: What do you do?

Model answer: Remind kindly, point to booths, apply the rule, and review the sign.

Case 2. The empty desks

Occupancy is 40%.

Question: Options?

Model answer: Offer day passes, run a trial week, partner with a local group and review price.

Glossary

Hot desk
A shared desk used on a first-come basis.
Fixed desk
A desk reserved for one member.
Occupancy
Share of seats in use.
Break-even
Where income equals costs.
Walkway
Clear path through the space.
House rules
Agreed behaviour in the space.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name three coworking plans.

Model answer: Hot desk, fixed desk and private room. (Covered in module 1: What coworking is. To pass that module: Notes show top three needs.)

Q2. Break-even formula?

Model answer: Fixed costs divided by price per member. (Covered in module 4: Costs and pricing. To pass that module: Break-even computed with currency.)

Q3. Why a capacity sign?

Model answer: Safety and fire rules. (Covered in module 3: Rules, safety and insurance. To pass that module: Adviser signs off.)

Q4. Occupancy formula?

Model answer: Seat-days used divided by seat-days available. (Covered in module 8: Measure and improve. To pass that module: Three changes with dates.)

Q5. What do members value?

Model answer: Price, quiet, community and internet. (Covered in module 1: What coworking is. To pass that module: Notes show top three needs.)

Q6. Why record logs?

Model answer: They prove care and help fix patterns. (Covered in module 7: Operations. To pass that module: Logs complete.)

Q7. Who signs off safety?

Model answer: A qualified local adviser or authority. (Covered in module 3: Rules, safety and insurance. To pass that module: Adviser signs off.)

Q8. Why events?

Model answer: Community keeps members. (Covered in module 6: Community and events. To pass that module: Plan fits budget.)

Q9. Name one house rule.

Model answer: Quiet in call zones. (Covered in module 3: Rules, safety and insurance. To pass that module: Adviser signs off.)

Q10. How price a day pass?

Model answer: Higher per day than monthly, to encourage membership. (Covered in module 5: Memberships and booking. To pass that module: Plans clear in one page.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Building use, fire, accessibility and lease rules differ by place; check them early. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
