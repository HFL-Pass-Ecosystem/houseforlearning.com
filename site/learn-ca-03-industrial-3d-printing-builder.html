Industrial 3d printing builder

Run a pilot of industrial 3d printing builder in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small industrial 3d printing builder offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: advanced technology and manufacturing. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Choose parts that suit printing 6 hUnderstand

Industrial printing pays when parts are complex, low-volume or need fast change. It loses to moulding at high volume.

  • Good fits include custom brackets, jigs and fixtures, spare parts that are no longer made, lightweight lattices, and medical and dental models.
  • Poor fits are thousands of identical simple parts, where moulding or bulk machining costs less per part.
  • Compare three routes for one part: machining, moulding, printing; write cost per part at 10, 100 and 10,000 units.
  • Find customers among machine shops, repair depots, clinics, product designers and makers of low-volume equipment.
  • Check the build file with a slicer preview before printing, and keep the preview image with the job record.
  • Ask the customer for the part's size, load, working temperature, finish and number needed, and write the answers on the quote request.
  • Count total cost, not only the unit price: moulding adds a tool cost up front, so divide it by the number of parts.
Worked example. A bracket costs 260 to machine, 12 to mould (tooling 18,000) and 150 to print. For 10 parts, printing costs 1,500, machining 2,600 and moulding 18,000 + 120 = 18,120. Moulding only beats printing past 18,000 / (150 - 12) = about 131 parts; at 5,000 parts moulding is far cheaper per part.

Activity. Compare the three routes for two real parts and recommend one.

Standard to meet. Cost at three volumes shown for each part.

Module 2 Know print rules, safety and standards 6 hApply

Printing with metal powders, resins and heat has real hazards and quality rules.

  • Metal powders can burn or explode; follow storage, grounding and fire rules and keep safety data sheets.
  • Resins and fumes need ventilation, gloves and eye protection; dispose of waste as hazardous if rules say so.
  • Parts for aircraft, medical and pressure uses need certified material and process records; check the customer's standard before you quote.
  • Provincial workplace health and safety rules require trained machine operators; keep training records and check the provincial rule for your trade.
  • Name one test per load direction (pull, bend, impact) and run it on a printed coupon before you quote a safety-related part.
  • Put the safety data sheet for each material in a binder at the machine, and teach staff to find the first-aid and spill steps.
  • Check the provincial rule for noise, air quality and waste handling in your building, and ask the landlord and insurer what they require.
Worked example. Row: 'Store titanium powder | fire code and provincial workplace safety rules | sealed containers, grounded, signage | before first delivery | inspection note'. For a 3-person shop with 4 materials, that is 4 safety data sheets and 3 training records, 7 documents to file before the first job.

Activity. Build a rules table for a print shop of three people.

Standard to meet. Every hazard row has a control and a training record.

Module 3 Prepare, print and finish a part 10 hApply

Quality comes from good files, good settings and careful finishing.

  • Check the model: closed surface, wall thickness above the machine's minimum, overhangs supported.
  • Orient the part to put strength along the load and minimise supports.
  • Record layer height, material lot, temperature, speed and build time for each job.
  • Finish: remove supports, heat-treat or cure if needed, smooth, measure against the drawing.
  • Ask each customer what happens if the part fails; use the answer to set inspection depth.
  • Print a small test piece first to check fit when a tolerance is tight, and measure it before you print the full part.
  • Dry materials that absorb moisture and store them in sealed boxes; record the storage and drying time on the job sheet.
Worked example. A bracket is printed flat and fails along layers at 600 N; printed upright it holds 1,100 N. That is 1,100 / 600 = 1.83 times stronger, so orientation almost doubled strength. Record both results and the angle in the job sheet.

Activity. Print one part two ways, test and record results.

Standard to meet. Settings recorded; both parts measured; best orientation justified.

Module 4 Price a job and prove margin 8 hEvaluate

Machine hours, material, labour and failure rate set the price.

  • Machine rate = machine cost divided by expected hours of use, plus power and maintenance per hour.
  • Add the material used including supports and waste, post-processing labour and inspection.
  • Add a failure allowance: if 1 in 10 builds fails, add about 11% to cost.
  • Set the quote price as cost plus target margin, and show the customer a breakdown.
  • Margin = price minus cost. Break-even parts = monthly fixed cost divided by margin per part, rounded up.
  • Test your quote with a 20% rise in failures and with a 10% rise in material price. Write what happens to margin.
  • Keep a monthly list of the three parts you could not print well and why; it guides your next training topic.
Worked example. Cost per part: machine time 70, material 45, post-processing 25, inspection 10 = 150; a 1 in 10 failure rate adds 150 x 0.111 = about 17, so 167. Price 420 gives margin 253 (60%). Fixed cost 25,000 a month breaks even at 25,000 / 253 = about 99 parts. If failures rise from 10% to 12%, the allowance becomes about 20 and margin falls to 250. Teaching figures in CAD.

Activity. Build the quote sheet and test a 20% rise in failures.

Standard to meet. Units shown; failure allowance included.

Module 5 Run the shop with traceable quality 8 hApply

Customers trust parts they can trace.

  • Give every part a job number tied to material lot and build file.
  • Measure critical dimensions with calibrated tools and keep certificates.
  • Schedule maintenance: laser or nozzle checks, filter changes, calibration prints.
  • Track first-pass yield: good parts divided by started parts.
  • Time every job from file received to part shipped; the median is your honest lead time.
  • Calibrate scales and callipers at fixed dates and log them.
  • Keep rejected parts in a labelled bin until you have written the cause, then recycle or dispose as the rules require.
Worked example. Weekly page: 38 jobs, first-pass yield 89% (about 34 good), top failure warping; action: add a heated enclosure. Next week compare: if yield rises to 94%, 36 of 38 jobs pass, and the change paid off.

Activity. Keep a 4-week shop log with yield.

Standard to meet. Every job traces to a lot; action chosen from data.

Module 6 Win customers and design partners 6 hCreate

Show the part, the saving and the speed.

  • Offer a sample print of the customer's own part at low cost.
  • Quote with lead time in days; fast turnaround is your edge.
  • Partner with designers who can redesign parts for printing.
  • Join local manufacturing clusters and trade days.
  • Show a before-and-after case: the old part, the printed part, the time saved and the cost compared.
  • Write a one-page capability sheet: machines, materials, size limits, tolerances and lead times.
  • Follow up each sample within three days, ask one question about the fit and record the answer.
Worked example. Funnel: 25 shops visited, 9 samples, 4 repeat jobs. Rates: samples 9/25 = 36%, repeats 4/9 = 44%. Overall 4 of 25 visits (16%) became repeat customers, so 50 visits might give about 8.

Activity. Run a 4-week funnel and book 5 conversations.

Standard to meet. Funnel numbers recorded.

Module 7 Manage ethics, IP and risk 6 hEvaluate

Printing makes copying easy; handle design rights with care.

  • Print only designs you own or have a licence for.
  • Refuse parts that can be misused to harm, such as weapons components.
  • Keep customer files secure and delete on agreement.
  • Keep a risk register that covers machine breakdown, a powder incident, a customer file leak and the loss of one big customer.
  • Offer one-page design tips so customers send better files.
  • Put ownership of designs in the quote: who owns the file, the part and any improvements. Ask a lawyer to review your terms.
  • Write down what you will say to a customer asking for a part you must refuse, and keep a log of refusals.
Worked example. Risk: 'Single machine breaks for 2 weeks | 3 x 4 = 12 | service contract and partner shop agreement | owner | quarterly'. If that machine earns 10,000 a week, a two-week stop puts 20,000 of sales at risk, which is why the partner agreement matters.

Activity. Write an eight-row risk register.

Standard to meet. Each row has owner and review date.

Module 8 Pilot and scale the shop 8 hCreate

Prove a small cell before you add machines.

  • Pilot 60 days with 3 customers and measures: yield, turnaround, margin.
  • Stop rule: margin below 30% after 40 jobs means re-quote or stop.
  • Add a second machine only when the first runs above 60% use.
  • Plan funding from manufacturing grants and an equipment lease.
  • Send a feedback message three days after delivery and file the answer.
  • Machine use = hours printing divided by hours available; track it weekly from the machine log.
  • Check the funding programmes' rules and dates yourself, and compare lease cost with buying.
Worked example. Result: 41 jobs, margin 44%, use 58%. Margin is above the 30% stop line, but use is below 60%; decision: hold until use passes 60%. At 160 machine hours available a week, 58% means about 93 hours printing and 60% means 96 hours.

Activity. Run the pilot and present review.

Standard to meet. Review has measures and decision.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
