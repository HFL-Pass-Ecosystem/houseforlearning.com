Courses in other hobbies

Run a real courses in other hobbies pilot in Canada: eight modules from user need to review, with rules, costs, risks and a seven-level ladder.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a 30 to 90 day pilot of a small courses in other hobbies offer for real users in Canada and keep a full record.

Condition

About 58 hours over 3 to 6 months. A free spreadsheet, a phone or computer, one mentor, a peer circle and at least five real users. Practice partners are agreed locally.

Standard

Rules table complete with dated sources; cost sheet checked by a mentor; risk register and privacy note written; pilot reviewed with evidence; at least 70% on the ten-question check.

Field: health, care, wellness and education. Who it is for: students, career changers, returners, newcomers to Canada, small-business starters. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, rules table, cost sheet, risk register, rubric, ten-question check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know the words of teaching a hobby 6 hUnderstand

A hobby course helps adults or children learn a skill for pleasure. Start with the words.

  • Learn the terms: lesson plan, outcome, level, material, demo, practice, feedback, class size.
  • An outcome says what a learner can do at the end, such as 'knit a flat square'.
  • Beginners need small steps, plain words and quick wins.
  • Pick one hobby you know well and list five things a beginner must learn first.
  • Write an outcome for each of five steps using verbs such as cast, fold, mix, draw or tune.
  • Describe your typical beginner in three lines: age, experience and what they hope to make.
  • Compare 'beginner' and 'intermediate' in your hobby by what a learner can already do.
Worked example. Outcome: 'By the end of class, you can cast on and knit ten rows.' A weak outcome is 'understand knitting' because you cannot see it. A 90-minute class with 5 outcomes gives 90 / 5 = 18 minutes each, which is too little, so cut to 3 outcomes at 30 minutes each.

Activity. List five outcomes for a beginner class in your hobby, each starting with a verb.

Standard to meet. Task: write five outcomes. Condition: for one beginner class. Standard: each starts with a verb that can be seen, such as make, draw, name or play.

Module 2 Explain how a class is planned and run 6 hApply

A good class has a shape. Learners should do more than listen.

  • The shape: welcome, show, try, feedback, try again, wrap-up.
  • Plan your time so showing stays short, about 10 to 15 minutes, and most of the class is practice.
  • List materials and tools and who brings them.
  • Plan for slow and fast learners with an easy and a harder task.
  • Draw a one-page timeline with minutes beside each part.
  • Keep an extra challenge ready for learners who finish early, and a simpler version for anyone who is stuck.
  • Set up the room before learners arrive: seats, light and tools at each place.
Worked example. A 90-minute pottery-style class: 10 welcome, 15 demo, 45 practice, 10 feedback, 10 clean-up and next steps. Check: 10 + 15 + 45 + 10 + 10 = 90. Showing takes 15 of 90 minutes, about 17%, so learners spend most of the time doing.

Activity. Write a 90-minute plan with times and a materials list.

Standard to meet. Task: write a lesson plan. Condition: for one class of eight learners. Standard: times add to 90, outcomes are stated, and materials and safety notes are listed.

Module 3 Teach a short lesson to a standard 10 hApply

Now you teach. Show clearly, let learners try, and give kind, specific feedback.

  • Show the skill slowly, then say what you did and why.
  • Break a skill into steps of one action each.
  • Check understanding by asking learners to show you, not just nod.
  • Give feedback on one thing at a time: what worked, what to try next.
  • Demonstrate each step twice, once at normal speed and once slowly with your words.
  • Ask 'Can you show me?' instead of 'Does everyone understand?'
  • Evaluate your lesson with three questions to learners: what was clear, what was unclear, what next.
Worked example. Instead of 'Good job', say: 'Your stitches are even. Next, try to keep your yarn tension the same on the return row.' For a 20-minute mini lesson, plan 3 minutes to show, 12 to practise and 5 for feedback: 3 + 12 + 5 = 20.

Activity. Teach a 20-minute mini lesson to three people and collect their feedback.

Standard to meet. Task: teach a 20-minute lesson. Condition: to three volunteers. Standard: every learner completes the outcome and gives one useful comment.

Module 4 Follow Canadian rules for classes and content 8 hEvaluate

Teaching to the public has rules about safety, advertising and other people's work.

  • Advertising must be true. Do not promise results you cannot guarantee.
  • Copyright protects patterns, songs, books and images. Use your own material or material you have a licence for; check the federal copyright rules before you copy anything.
  • Check the municipal rules for running classes at home or in a public space, including business licences and zoning.
  • Check whether your activity needs a certification or extra rules, such as food, fire, tools or children; check the current rule with the local authority.
  • List which of your materials are your own and which come from someone else.
  • Ask your insurer whether your cover includes classes at home or in a rented space.
  • Describe how you credit sources on handouts.
Worked example. You want to hand out a pattern from a book. You make your own version instead, or ask the owner for written permission. Keep the reply with your files, and if the owner does not answer, treat that as a no.

Activity. Make a table of rule, who sets it, what you must do and where you checked, for ten things in your class.

Standard to meet. Task: build a class rules table. Condition: using official public pages. Standard: each row has a source and date; unclear items say 'check the current rule with the local authority'.

Module 5 Cost and price a class 8 hApply

You earn what is left after materials, space and time.

  • Costs: materials per learner, space, tools, advertising, your prep and teaching time.
  • Price per learner = total costs plus your pay, divided by the number of learners, plus a small safety margin.
  • Know your break-even number of learners.
  • Sales tax may apply once you pass the small-supplier limit; check it with the tax agency.
  • Calculate materials per learner from real receipts.
  • Compare your price with two similar local classes and explain any difference.
  • Check break-even as fixed costs divided by price minus materials per learner.
Worked example. Costs: space 60, materials 8 per learner, your time 90. With 8 learners: 60 + 8 x 8 + 90 = 214, and 214 / 8 = 26.75, so the price is about $27. At 8 learners income is 8 x 27 = $216, only $2 above cost, so a safety margin matters; break-even with fixed costs of 150 is 150 / (27 - 8) = 7.9, so 8 learners.

Activity. Cost out a class and show the break-even number of learners.

Standard to meet. Task: price one class. Condition: with a simple spreadsheet. Standard: costs, price and break-even are shown and a mentor can follow every number.

Module 6 Find learners and promote honestly 6 hCreate

People join when they trust you and see what they will make.

  • Describe the class in plain words: who it is for, what they will make, what to bring, how long.
  • Use photos of real work with permission.
  • Reach local groups, libraries and community boards with their permission.
  • Keep a list of enquiries and replies; send messages only to people who agreed to receive them.
  • Draft a class title that says what learners make, such as 'Make a small watercolour card'.
  • Ask two practice learners for a short comment and written permission to use it.
  • Compare three places to advertise by cost and by how many local people they reach.
Worked example. A poster says: 'Beginner watercolour, 2 hours, all materials included, 8 spaces. Make a small card to take home.' If you contact 10 places and 3 reply, that is 30%. If each reply brings about 2 learners, 3 x 2 = 6 learners, two short of 8 spaces.

Activity. Create a one-page class description and promote it to ten local places.

Standard to meet. Task: promote one class. Condition: with permission and honest claims. Standard: the page states audience, outcome, time, price and what is included, and enquiries are logged.

Module 7 Keep learners safe and protect your work 6 hEvaluate

Safety and fairness come first.

  • Do a risk check: tools, heat, chemicals, allergies, floor space and first aid.
  • Ask learners about allergies and needs before class.
  • Write a short policy for cancellations, refunds and photos.
  • Protect learners' details and keep records of attendance.
  • Write first aid steps for a small cut, a burn and a fall, and keep a kit within reach.
  • Describe your refund rule, for example a full refund if you cancel and a set notice period if the learner cancels.
  • Ask a parent or guardian for written consent for any child, and check the provincial rule on supervision.
Worked example. A woodcraft class lists: safety glasses worn, tools put away after use, first aid kit at the door. With 6 learners and yourself, you need 6 + 1 = 7 pairs of safety glasses. Check them before each class.

Activity. Write a risk list and a one-page policy.

Standard to meet. Task: produce a risk list and policy. Condition: reviewed by a mentor. Standard: each risk has a control and the policy covers refunds, photos and privacy.

Module 8 Run a pilot course and decide 8 hCreate

Now you run a real course, measure and decide what to do next.

  • Set a pilot goal: for example one four-week course with six learners.
  • Track attendance, cost, income and feedback each week.
  • Ask each learner what they made and what to improve.
  • Decide: stop, change or grow, and write a 12-month plan.
  • Calculate profit as income minus costs, and profit per learner.
  • Describe what you would change about the room, timing and price.
  • Evaluate whether a second course has enough interest by counting the waiting list.
Worked example. After four weeks: 6 learners, 5 finished, income $162 (6 x $27), cost $102. Profit is 162 - 102 = $60, or 60 / 6 = $10 per learner. You plan a second course with a waiting list.

Activity. Run the pilot and present your plan to a panel of three.

Standard to meet. Task: run a four-week pilot. Condition: real learners and consent. Standard: log complete, outcomes met by most learners, decision uses numbers.

assessment

Evidence: value statement, rules table, core task record, cost sheet, 4-week log, funnel, risk register and privacy note, pilot review and 12-month plan. A mentor and two peers mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at "Not yet".

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a learning outcome?

Model answer: What a learner can do at the end of the class.

Q2. What shape does a good class have?

Model answer: Welcome, show, try, feedback, try again, wrap-up.

Q3. Why have learners try during class?

Model answer: People learn by doing, not only listening.

Q4. What makes feedback useful?

Model answer: It is specific and about one thing at a time.

Q5. Why be careful with patterns, songs and images?

Model answer: They are protected by copyright.

Q6. How do you find break-even?

Model answer: Fixed costs divided by the price minus materials per learner.

Q7. What should a class page state?

Model answer: Audience, outcome, time, price and what is included.

Q8. What do you ask learners before class?

Model answer: About allergies and needs.

Q9. What do you do when unsure about a rule?

Model answer: Check the current rule with the local authority.

Q10. What do you measure in a pilot?

Model answer: Attendance, cost, income and feedback.

ten locations and global

Your place

The course is written for Canada. The same method works elsewhere; local rules, currency and levels change. Pick your place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registering: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Levels frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registering: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Levels frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registering: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Levels frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registering: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Levels frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registering: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Levels frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registering: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Levels frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registering: registration with ACRA. Data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Levels frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registering: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Levels frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registering: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Levels frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registering: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Levels frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Registering: a trade and business licence or company registration. Data rule: the Data Protection Act. Levels frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico (Central America)

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Registering: tax registration (RFC) and company or sole-trader registration. Data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Levels frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
