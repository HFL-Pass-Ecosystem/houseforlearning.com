flagship course · GL-32

Influencer Marketing

Plan honest creator partnerships: find fitting creators, agree clear briefs and disclosure, and measure real results, or become a creator who is trusted.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L3 Foundation54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Plan honest creator partnerships: find fitting creators, agree clear briefs and disclosure, and measure real results, or become a creator who is trusted.

  • Understand Explain how creator partnerships work and the rules on disclosure.
  • Apply Find and vet creators for fit and honesty.
  • Analyse Analyse reach, engagement and cost per result.
  • Create Create a brief, a contract outline and a campaign plan.
  • Evaluate Evaluate a campaign and decide what to repeat.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and run a small creator campaign (or run your own creator channel) for 4 weeks with brief, disclosure and results report.

Condition

A product or cause, two to five small creators or a practice campaign, a spreadsheet, a mentor with marketing experience and a peer circle. About 8 to 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Written brief and agreement; disclosure on every post; results measured against a target; no fake followers or bought engagement; payment terms clear; audience data respected.

Who it is for: Marketers, small-business owners, creators, students. Requirements: None. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Follow advertising and disclosure rules. Never buy followers or fake engagement. Respect creators and audiences, especially children.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 How it works 4 hUnderstand

Creators share a message with their own audience. After this module you can describe the main creator types and sponsored-post model, so you choose partners by fit and trust instead of follower counts alone.

  • Types: nano, micro, mid, large by audience size.
  • Pay: product, fee, commission or a mix.
  • Trust and fit matter more than size.
  • Rules on disclosure.
  • Write a one-line promise for the campaign, such as 'ten new sign-ups from creators with under 20,000 followers in four weeks', and test every later decision against that sentence.
  • List three channels where your audience already spends time, then check each for ten minutes to see whether creators there post about products like yours.
  • Read how three real campaigns were described and note which felt honest.
  • Write three questions you will ask every creator before agreeing.
Worked example. A nano creator with 4,000 loyal followers may sell more than a large account with 400,000.

Activity. Compare three creator types on five points.

Standard to meet. Table done.

Common mistake. Choosing by follower count.

Second worked example. A nano creator with 4,000 followers reaches 1,600 people per post, with 120 clicks; a large account with 400,000 followers reaches 20,000 with 150 clicks. Cost per click favours the small account.

Check yourself. What matters more than size?

Module 2 Set the goal 6 hUnderstand

Start with what you want. You will turn a vague wish into one measurable campaign goal with a budget and deadline, which lets you judge every later choice against it.

  • Awareness, visits, sales, sign-ups.
  • One main goal and a number.
  • Budget in your currency.
  • Timeframe.
  • Turn each goal into a number and a date, for example 200 link clicks and 15 sales by day 30, so you can judge success without arguing about feelings afterwards.
  • Pick one main goal only; if you chase awareness, sales and sign-ups together, the brief becomes vague and no creator can tell what you actually want.
  • Ask each creator for audience age, place and top interests, not just follower totals.
  • Set a rule for what is off-limits: health claims, other brands, unsafe use.
Worked example. Goal: 30 sign-ups in 4 weeks with a 300 budget (10 per sign-up).

Activity. Write goal, budget and measure.

Standard to meet. Goal specific and numeric.

Common mistake. Vague goals.

Second worked example. A bakery wants 50 new customers from one creator post. At 12 per customer it sets a cap of 600, tells the creator the target in the brief, and checks after two weeks that cost per customer stayed under 12.

Check yourself. What is your target?

Module 3 Find and vet 8 hApply

Check fit and honesty. You will be able to screen creators for real engagement and matching audiences, which protects your budget from inflated accounts and mismatched followers.

  • Content fit with your values.
  • Audience match: place, age, interest.
  • Engagement quality: real comments.
  • Past partnerships and disclosure habits.
  • Review at least twelve recent posts per creator and count how many comments are genuine sentences; a sudden jump in followers with few real replies is a warning sign of bought followers.
  • Ask each shortlisted creator for a screenshot of their audience ages and countries from the last 30 days and compare it with your target customer.
  • Agree who owns the content and for how long it may be reused.
  • Put the agreed label wording in the brief in plain words.
Worked example. Likes 5,000 but comments are all emojis: poor quality; skip.

Activity. Vet five creators with a checklist.

Standard to meet. Each has evidence.

Common mistake. Ignoring fake engagement.

Second worked example. Of 5 creators vetted, one has 6,000 likes but 40 emoji-only comments; you drop it and keep the two with real replies.

Check yourself. How did you vet?

Module 4 The brief 8 hApply

Be clear but leave room. You will write a one-page brief that sets clear boundaries yet leaves creative room, so the resulting posts sound natural to the creator's audience.

  • Message, must-haves, must-avoids.
  • Deliverables and dates.
  • Disclosure wording.
  • Review and approval steps.
  • Keep the brief to one page: goal, audience, three must-say points, two things to avoid, deadline, and how to label the post as sponsored.
  • Share one example of content you like and one you dislike, since creators understand a concrete sample faster than an abstract description of tone.
  • Use calendars so posts do not land the same day.
  • Prepare answers to questions the audience is likely to ask.
Worked example. Brief: one video, 30 to 45 seconds, show use of the product, include "Ad" label, publish by 12th.

Activity. Write a one-page brief.

Standard to meet. Contains all items.

Common mistake. Over-controlling the creator.

Second worked example. A one-page brief: product shown in use, 30 to 45 seconds, label 'Ad', link in the first line, publish by the 12th, we review before posting.

Check yourself. What is flexible?

Module 5 Agreement and payment 8 hAnalyse

Protect both sides. You will draft a written agreement covering deliverables, dates, content rights and payment steps, so both you and the creator know what happens when plans change.

  • Fee, payment dates, usage rights.
  • What if content is late or off-brief?
  • Exclusivity (if any).
  • Data and privacy.
  • Put the deliverables in writing with counts and dates, for example one video and two stories by 14 March, and state how many revisions are included before work starts.
  • Agree the payment schedule in advance, such as half on posting and half after seven days, and record who owns the content and for how long it may be reused.
  • Monitor comments for 48 hours after each post and answer politely.
  • Report complaints to the creator at once, not at month end.
Worked example. Pay 50% on agreement, 50% on posting; rights for 3 months.

Activity. Draft an agreement outline.

Standard to meet. Terms are fair.

Common mistake. No written terms.

Second worked example. A fitness coach agrees a flat fee of 200 plus 5 per sign-up the creator brings. If 20 people join, the creator earns 300 in total; the agreement also says each post must carry a clear 'paid partnership' label.

Check yourself. What did you agree?

Module 6 Run the campaign 10 hCreate

Coordinate and watch. You will launch with tracked links, check each live post against the agreement and fix problems quickly, keeping the campaign accurate and the creators properly disclosed.

  • Calendar and approvals.
  • Track links and codes per creator.
  • Reply to comments.
  • Fix issues quickly.
  • Give each creator a unique link or discount code so every click and sale can be matched to the right person, and test the link yourself before the first post goes live.
  • Check the live post within 24 hours for the sponsored label, the correct link and the agreed wording, and message the creator politely the same day if anything is off.
  • Compare cost per result across creators in a table.
  • Thank creators publicly when the campaign works.
Worked example. Each creator gets a code; sales by code are visible.

Activity. Run the campaign.

Standard to meet. Links and codes work.

Common mistake. Forgetting tracking.

Second worked example. Each creator gets a code. Code A gives 14 sign-ups, code B 9, code C 3, so next time budget moves to A.

Check yourself. What is your code?

Module 7 Measure 6 hEvaluate

Numbers and notes. You will calculate cost per result for each creator and read the numbers after a fair waiting period, so your next campaign rests on evidence, not impressions.

  • Reach, engagement rate, clicks, sales.
  • Cost per result = spend / results.
  • Quality of comments.
  • Lessons.
  • Compare cost per result across creators by dividing what you paid by the sales or sign-ups each one produced, then rank them rather than relying on likes alone.
  • Wait at least seven days after posting before judging results, because many viewers act late, and record the final numbers in a simple table you can reuse.
  • Keep contact details securely and only for the campaign.
  • Never ask creators for personal data on their followers.
Worked example. Spend 300; 38 sign-ups; cost per result 7.90; best creator 5.10.

Activity. Report results.

Standard to meet. Calculations correct.

Common mistake. Judging on views alone.

Second worked example. A book shop spends 240 across three creators and gets 48 orders. Cost per order is 5.00; one creator drove 30 orders at 3.20 and the other two 8.00 each, so next round the shop rebooks the first creator.

Check yourself. What is yours?

Module 8 Be a creator, too 4 hCreate

If you are the creator, protect your trust. You will assemble a media kit and a disclosure habit for your own channel, which keeps your audience's trust while you accept paid partnerships.

  • Accept only fitting offers.
  • Disclose always.
  • Keep honest tone.
  • Track your own results.
  • Build a one-page media kit with your best three posts, audience facts, and the types of partnership you accept, and keep the numbers updated every month.
  • Add a clear disclosure line to every sponsored post you make, and keep a log of each agreement so you can prove what was promised if a question arises.
  • Write down three changes for the next campaign.
  • If you are the creator, publish your own disclosure and rules page.
Worked example. You decline a high-fee offer that does not fit; followers notice and trust grows.

Activity. Write your own creator rules.

Standard to meet. Rules include disclosure.

Common mistake. Accepting every offer as a creator.

Second worked example. As a creator you decline a 400 offer for a product that failed your test. Followers notice; the next honest review earns 6% more clicks.

Check yourself. What will you decline?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The off-brief post

A creator posts a claim not in the brief.

Question: Response?

Model answer: Ask for a correction, use the contract and keep records.

Case 2. The undisclosed ad

A creator does not label a paid post.

Question: What do you do?

Model answer: Request an immediate label, pause payment and review the process.

Brief

Interactions divided by reach.
Usage rights
How you may reuse content.

A code tracking sales.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why can small creators win?

Model answer: Their audiences often trust them more. (Covered in module 1: How it works. To pass that module: Table done.)

Q2. What belongs in a brief?

Model answer: Message, dos and don'ts, deliverables, dates and disclosure. (Covered in module 4: The brief. To pass that module: Contains all items.)

Q3. Why disclose?

Model answer: It is honest and often required. (Covered in module 1: How it works. To pass that module: Table done.)

Q4. Cost per result?

Model answer: Spend divided by results. (Covered in module 7: Measure. To pass that module: Calculations correct.)

Q5. Why vet comments?

Model answer: To see if engagement is real. (Covered in module 3: Find and vet. To pass that module: Each has evidence.)

Q6. Why a written agreement?

Model answer: It protects both sides. (Covered in module 5: Agreement and payment. To pass that module: Terms are fair.)

Q7. Should you buy followers?

Model answer: No. (Covered in module 3: Find and vet. To pass that module: Each has evidence.)

Q8. What if a creator is off-brief?

Model answer: Use the agreement's fix or end terms. (Covered in module 5: Agreement and payment. To pass that module: Terms are fair.)

Q9. Name a goal type.

Model answer: Awareness, visits, sales or sign-ups. (Covered in module 2: Set the goal. To pass that module: Goal specific and numeric.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Disclosure rules and age limits for audiences differ by place: follow the local authority. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
