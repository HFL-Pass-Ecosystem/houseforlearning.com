Grade 12 Science: Chemistry

6 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 12 Science Chemistry, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 6 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

6 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Solutions and electrochemistry Weeks 1 to 6Apply

In this unit you work on 'Solutions and electrochemistry', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: calculate colligative properties and cell potentials. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Solutions and electrochemistry' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: calculate colligative properties and cell potentials. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: 10 g of salt in 90 g of water: solution mass 100 g, so concentration = 10 / 100 x 100 = 10 percent by mass. Method: Model run for 'Solutions and electrochemistry'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Calculate molality. 18 g of glucose (180 g/mol) is 0.1 mol, dissolved in 500 g of water. m = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 mol/kg. Mole fraction of glucose = 0.1 / (0.1 + 27.8) = 0.0036.
  2. Step 2, Find the boiling point elevation. delta Tb = Kb m = 0.52 x 0.2 = 0.104 K, so the solution boils at 100.104 C. Freezing point depression with Kf = 1.86: delta Tf = 0.372 K, freezing at -0.372 C.
  3. Step 3, Calculate molar mass from osmotic pressure. A solution of 2 g solute in 1 L gives pi = 0.0246 atm at 300 K. M = wRT / (pi V) = 2 x 0.0821 x 300 / 0.0246 = 2002 g/mol, a polymer-like value.
  4. Step 4, Apply the van't Hoff factor. 0.1 m NaCl has i near 2, so delta Tf = 2 x 1.86 x 0.1 = 0.372 K, twice that of glucose at 0.1 m. Relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the solute mole fraction.
  5. Step 5, Calculate a cell potential. Zn + Cu2+ gives Zn2+ + Cu. E cell = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10 V. Nernst at 298 K: E = 1.10 - (0.0592/2) log([Zn2+]/[Cu2+]). With [Zn2+] = 0.01 and [Cu2+] = 1, E = 1.10 + 0.0592 = 1.159 V.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: find delta Tf for 0.5 m glucose in water (Kf = 1.86). Answer: 1.86 x 0.5 = 0.93 K, so freezing at -0.93 C.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Calculate colligative properties and cell potentials: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 2 Chemical kinetics and surface chemistry Weeks 7 to 12Apply

In this unit you work on 'Chemical kinetics and surface chemistry', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: determine rate laws. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Chemical kinetics and surface chemistry' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: determine rate laws. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Rectangle 12 cm by 5 cm. Perimeter = 2 x (12 + 5) = 34 cm. Area = 12 x 5 = 60 square cm. A cuboid 12 x 5 x 3 has volume 180 cubic cm. Method: Model run for 'Chemical kinetics and surface chemistry'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Determine the rate law from data. For A + B gives products: expt 1 [A] 0.1, [B] 0.1, rate 2e-3; expt 2 [A] 0.2, [B] 0.1, rate 4e-3; expt 3 [A] 0.1, [B] 0.2, rate 8e-3. Doubling A doubles the rate, so order 1; doubling B quadruples it, order 2.
  2. Step 2, Write rate = k [A][B]^2, overall order 3. Find k: 2e-3 = k x 0.1 x 0.01, so k = 2e-3 / 1e-3 = 2 L^2 mol^-2 s^-1.
  3. Step 3, Use a first-order reaction. Rate constant k = 0.0693 min^-1. Half-life t1/2 = 0.693 / k = 10 min. After 30 min, [A] = [A]0 / 2^3 = 12.5 per cent of the start.
  4. Step 4, Use the Arrhenius equation. Suppose the rate doubles for a 10 K rise from 300 K to 310 K. Then ln 2 = (Ea/R)(1/300 - 1/310) = (Ea/R)(1.075e-4), giving Ea = 0.693 x 8.314 / 1.075e-4 = 53.6 kJ/mol.
  5. Step 5, Explain surface chemistry. Adsorption is physisorption (weak van der Waals) or chemisorption (bonds). Freundlich: x/m = k P^(1/n). Catalysts like Fe in the Haber process lower activation energy, and enzymes are specific biological catalysts.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a first-order reaction has k = 0.0231 min^-1. Find its half-life. Answer: 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 min.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Determine rate laws: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 3 d- and f-block elements and coordination compounds Weeks 13 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'd- and f-block elements and coordination compounds', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe transition metals and complexes. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'd- and f-block elements and coordination compounds' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe transition metals and complexes. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Transition metals show variable oxidation states: iron forms Fe2+ and Fe3+. The complex ion of copper with ammonia is deep blue. Method: Model run for 'd- and f-block elements and coordination compounds'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Write electron configurations. Fe is [Ar] 3d6 4s2 and Fe3+ is [Ar] 3d5. Cu is [Ar] 3d10 4s1, an exception for stability. Transition metals show variable oxidation states since 3d and 4s energies are close.
  2. Step 2, Calculate magnetic moment. Using mu = sqrt(n(n+2)) BM. Fe3+ with 5 unpaired electrons: sqrt(35) = 5.92 BM. Ti3+ with 1 unpaired: sqrt(3) = 1.73 BM. Zn2+ with 0 unpaired is diamagnetic.
  3. Step 3, Study KMnO4. In acid, MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- gives Mn2+ + 4H2O. Oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+: 5Fe2+ + MnO4- + 8H+ gives 5Fe3+ + Mn2+ + 4H2O. 1 mol of MnO4- oxidises 5 mol of Fe2+.
  4. Step 4, Name coordination compounds. [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 is hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride. Oxidation state of Co: x + 6(0) = +3 for the complex ion, so x = +3. The coordination number is 6 because six ligands donate electron pairs.
  5. Step 5, Apply crystal field theory. In an octahedral complex d orbitals split into t2g and eg. [Fe(CN)6]4- is low spin (strong field) and diamagnetic; [FeF6]3- is high spin with 5 unpaired. Colour comes from d-d transitions.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: find the oxidation state of Cr in K2Cr2O7. Answer: 2(+1) + 2x + 7(-2) = 0, so x = +6.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe transition metals and complexes: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 4 Haloalkanes, haloarenes, alcohols, phenols and ethers Weeks 19 to 24Understand

In this unit you work on 'Haloalkanes, haloarenes, alcohols, phenols and ethers', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe preparation and reactions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Haloalkanes, haloarenes, alcohols, phenols and ethers' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe preparation and reactions. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: CH3CH2Br is bromoethane; CH3CH2OH is ethanol, which can be oxidised to ethanoic acid. Method: Model run for 'Haloalkanes, haloarenes, alcohols, phenols and ethers'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Prepare haloalkanes. CH3CH2OH + SOCl2 gives CH3CH2Cl + SO2 + HCl, a clean method since the by-products are gases. Alternatively ROH + HCl with ZnCl2 (Lucas reagent) is used.
  2. Step 2, Compare SN1 and SN2. A primary halide such as 1-bromobutane reacts with OH- by SN2, one concerted step with inversion and rate = k[RX][OH-]. Tert-butyl bromide reacts by SN1 through a carbocation, rate = k[RX], giving a racemic mix if chiral.
  3. Step 3, Reactions of alcohols. Ethanol with acidified K2Cr2O7 gives ethanal then ethanoic acid. Dehydration with conc. H2SO4 at 443 K gives ethene; at 413 K it gives diethyl ether. Lucas test: tertiary alcohols turn cloudy at once, secondary after about 5 minutes, primary not at room temperature.
  4. Step 4, Reactions of phenol. Phenol is acidic (pKa about 10) and reacts with NaOH to give sodium phenoxide. With bromine water it gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol as a white precipitate. Reimer-Tiemann gives salicylaldehyde.
  5. Step 5, Ether preparation by Williamson: C2H5ONa + CH3Cl gives C2H5OCH3 + NaCl. Use a primary alkyl halide, because a tertiary one gives elimination.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: name the organic product when propan-2-ol is oxidised with acidified potassium dichromate, and say why it cannot oxidise further. Answer: propanone, a ketone, which has no hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe preparation and reactions: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 5 Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and amines Weeks 25 to 30Understand

In this unit you work on 'Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and amines', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe reactions and tests. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and amines' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe reactions and tests. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: A solution that turns blue litmus red is an acid. Acid + base gives salt + water, for example hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride + water. Method: Model run for 'Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and amines'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Recognise the groups. Aldehydes have C=O at the chain end: ethanal CH3CHO. Ketones have C=O inside the chain: propanone CH3COCH3. Carboxylic acids have -COOH: ethanoic acid CH3COOH. Amines have -NH2: methylamine CH3NH2. Name each from its longest chain, then ending -al, -one, -oic acid or -amine.
  2. Step 2, Describe oxidation. Warm ethanol with acidified potassium dichromate and it is oxidised to ethanal, then to ethanoic acid; the orange mixture turns green. Propan-2-ol gives propanone, which resists further oxidation. Reduction goes the other way: ethanal with sodium borohydride gives ethanol. Safety: dichromate is toxic, so use a water bath, not a flame.
  3. Step 3, Tell aldehydes from ketones. Warm with ammoniacal silver nitrate: an aldehyde gives a silver mirror, a ketone gives nothing. Warm with alkaline copper(II) solution: an aldehyde turns blue to brick-red solid, a ketone stays blue. 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine gives an orange precipitate with both. Use tiny amounts, wear goggles, rinse silver mixtures away at once.
  4. Step 4, Carboxylic acids are weak acids. Ethanoic acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate fizzes: CH3COOH + NaHCO3 -> CH3COONa + H2O + CO2, and the gas turns limewater cloudy. With ethanol and a little concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst it forms ethyl ethanoate, a fruity-smelling ester. Masses for the ester reaction: 60 + 46 = 106 and 88 + 18 = 106, so the masses balance. Safety: wear goggles, because concentrated sulfuric acid burns skin.
  5. Step 5, Amines are weak bases because nitrogen has a lone pair. CH3NH2 + H2O <=> CH3NH3+ + OH-, so it turns red litmus blue. With hydrochloric acid it makes the salt methylammonium chloride, CH3NH3Cl, which dissolves in water. Primary, secondary and tertiary amines have one, two or three carbon groups on N. Amines smell fishy and are irritants, so use a fume cupboard.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a liquid gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, no silver mirror, and no colour change with warm acidified dichromate. Name its class and one example. Model answer: it is a ketone, because it has a carbonyl group but resists oxidation; propanone is an example.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe reactions and tests: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

Unit 6 Biomolecules and polymers Weeks 31 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Biomolecules and polymers', taught in a way that is with exam-style questions and written reasoning. The target is clear: describe carbohydrates, proteins and polymers. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Biomolecules and polymers' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe carbohydrates, proteins and polymers. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Water is H2O: 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. Mass = 2 x 1 + 16 = 18 units. Check atoms balance on both sides of an equation. Method: Model run for 'Biomolecules and polymers'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Classify carbohydrates. Glucose is a reducing aldohexose giving C6H12O6; fructose is a ketohexose. Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose joined by a glycosidic link and is non-reducing. Starch is a polymer of alpha glucose; cellulose of beta glucose.
  2. Step 2, Test: Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with glucose; Fehling's gives a red precipitate of Cu2O. Sucrose gives neither until hydrolysed with dilute acid to give glucose and fructose, called inversion.
  3. Step 3, Proteins. Amino acids join by peptide bonds: glycine + alanine gives glycylalanine + H2O. Levels of structure: primary (sequence), secondary (alpha helix and beta sheet via hydrogen bonds), tertiary and quaternary. Denaturation changes shape, as when an egg is boiled.
  4. Step 4, Nucleic acids: DNA has deoxyribose and bases A, T, G, C; RNA has ribose and U instead of T. A pairs with T by 2 hydrogen bonds, G pairs with C by 3. DNA of 20 per cent A has 20 per cent T and 30 per cent G and C each.
  5. Step 5, Polymers. Addition: n CH2=CH2 gives polythene. Condensation: hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid gives nylon-6,6 with loss of water. Natural rubber is polyisoprene; vulcanisation with sulphur adds cross-links. Teflon comes from tetrafluoroethene.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a DNA sample has 30 per cent adenine. Give percentages of T, G and C. Answer: T 30, G 20, C 20 per cent.

Activity. About 40 to 50 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a timed paper and an error log.

Standard to meet. Describe carbohydrates, proteins and polymers: at least 70 percent on an exam-style task, with the method shown.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Solids, liquids and gases

On the slide: L1 Basic · Solids, liquids and gases

Notes: Name the three states of matter and say what happens when a substance is heated. Notes: warm ice in a beaker of warm tap water and record the temperature each minute. Use no flame, and handle warm water with care. Learners say which change is melting.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Atoms, elements and compounds

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Atoms, elements and compounds

Notes: Describe atoms, elements and compounds and read simple chemical formulae. Notes: for water, H2O, learners count 2 hydrogen and 1 oxygen, which is 3 atoms in one molecule. They sort symbols into elements and compounds on cards. Wear goggles whenever any chemical is out, even for show-and-tell.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Balanced equations and the mole

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Balanced equations and the mole

Notes: Calculate relative formula mass and balance simple equations. Notes: balance 2H2 + O2 gives 2H2O by counting atoms on both sides. Water has Mr 18, so 36 g is 2 mol. Learners repeat this for carbon dioxide, Mr 44, and check that 88 g is 2 mol.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Acid-base titration

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Acid-base titration

Notes: Apply stoichiometry to find an unknown concentration from a titration. Notes: 25.0 cm3 of sodium hydroxide needs a mean 20.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol/dm3 hydrochloric acid. Moles of acid are 0.00200, equal to moles of alkali, so the alkali is 0.0800 mol/dm3. Wear goggles; use dilute solutions and rinse spills at once.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Energy changes and rates (this course)

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Energy changes and rates

Notes: Analyse a calorimetry result and link energy change to bond breaking and making. Notes: 50 g of water warms by 6.5 K, so q equals 50 times 4.18 times 6.5, which is 1358.5 J. For 0.025 mol that gives about 54.3 kJ/mol released. Learners list heat loss as the main error and suggest a lid.

This course: Grade 12 Science Chemistry is at this level. Its units include Solutions and electrochemistry; Chemical kinetics and surface chemistry; d- and f-block elements and coordination compounds; Haloalkanes, haloarenes, alcohols, phenols and ethers and more.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Thermodynamics and kinetics

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Thermodynamics and kinetics

Notes: Apply Gibbs energy and rate laws to predict whether and how fast a reaction goes. Notes: with change in H of +40 kJ/mol and change in S of +110 J/K/mol at 298 K, G is +7.22 kJ/mol, not spontaneous. It becomes spontaneous above 40000/110, about 364 K. Learners explain why a positive G says nothing about speed.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Method validation in analysis

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Method validation in analysis

Notes: Evaluate an analytical method for accuracy, precision and detection limit, and design a validation plan. Notes: with blank noise of 0.002 absorbance and a calibration slope of 0.250 per mg/L, the limit of detection is 3.3 times 0.002 over 0.250, which is 0.0264 mg/L. Write a hazard assessment and waste plan before any lab work.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.
