Grade 06: Social science

4 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L3Secondary4 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Remember, Understand
  • Understand Describe how early people lived and early cities began.
  • Understand Describe the solar system, latitudes and longitudes.
  • Remember Read maps and describe major landforms.
  • Understand Describe diversity, government and local governance.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 06 Social science, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 4 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

4 units with worked examples

Unit 1 History: early humans to ancient civilisations Weeks 1 to 9Understand

In this unit you work on 'History: early humans to ancient civilisations', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: describe how early people lived and early cities began. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'History: early humans to ancient civilisations' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe how early people lived and early cities began. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Early humans moved from hunting and gathering to farming; Indus Valley cities had planned streets and drains. Put four events on a timeline. Method: Model run for 'History: early humans to ancient civilisations'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe early people. For most of history, humans were hunter-gatherers in the Old Stone Age. They lived in small groups, in caves or shelters, and moved to follow animals and ripe plants. They made tools from stone, bone and wood, and learned to control fire for warmth, cooking and protection.
  2. Step 2, Add more of their lives. They wore animal skins, painted animals on cave walls, and made spears and scrapers. The group shared food and looked after children. We learn this from archaeologists, who study tools, bones and paintings and take care not to disturb a site.
  3. Step 3, Describe the farming change. About 10,000 years ago, people in the New Stone Age began to grow wheat and barley and keep sheep and goats. They settled in villages, built houses, made pots, and stored grain. A food surplus meant some people could do other jobs.
  4. Step 4, Describe how cities began. Early cities grew beside rivers: the Nile in Egypt, the Tigris and Euphrates in Mesopotamia and the Indus in South Asia. Fertile mud, irrigation and surplus food supported thousands of people, along with traders, builders and rulers. The wheel appeared around 3500 BCE.
  5. Step 5, Describe city features. Sumerian cities had temples, markets and cuneiform writing pressed into clay from about 3200 BCE. Indus cities around 2600 BCE had planned streets, brick houses and drains. Such a city was founded about 2600 + 2000 = 4,600 years before the year 2000 CE.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: how many years before 2000 CE was a city founded in 3000 BCE, and name one reason it grew by a river. Answer: 3000 + 2000 = 5,000 years. A river gave water, fertile soil for crops, and a way to trade.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Describe how early people lived and early cities began: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 2 Geography: Earth in space Weeks 10 to 18Understand

In this unit you work on 'Geography: Earth in space', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: describe the solar system, latitudes and longitudes. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Geography: Earth in space' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe the solar system, latitudes and longitudes. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Earth rotates once in 24 hours (day and night) and orbits the Sun in about 365 days (a year). Earthquakes happen where plates meet; rivers and wind erode land. Method: Model run for 'Geography: Earth in space'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe the solar system. The Sun is a star at the centre. Eight planets travel around it in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. Earth is about 150 million km from the Sun, so light takes about 150,000,000 / 300,000 = 500 s, or 8 minutes, to reach us.
  2. Step 2, Describe Earth's motions. Earth spins on its tilted axis once in 24 hours, causing day and night, and this is rotation. It goes around the Sun once in about 365 and a quarter days, which is revolution, so we usually add a leap day every four years. Try it with a globe and a torch, never shining it into eyes.
  3. Step 3, Describe latitudes. These are imaginary circles parallel to the Equator, measured in degrees north or south. The Equator is 0 degrees and each pole is 90 degrees. The Tropic of Cancer is 23.5 degrees north, and the Arctic Circle is 66.5 degrees north. One degree of latitude is about 111 km. The UAE lies at about 22 to 26 degrees north.
  4. Step 4, Describe longitudes. These are lines from pole to pole, measured east or west of the prime meridian at 0 degrees. There are 360 degrees in a full circle, so the Earth turns 15 degrees every hour (360 / 24 = 15). The UAE lies at about 51 to 56 degrees east.
  5. Step 5, Use longitude for time. A place at 75 degrees east is 75 / 15 = 5 hours ahead of the prime meridian. Dubai at about 55 degrees east is 55 / 15, about 3 hours 40 minutes ahead by the Sun. The UAE uses 60 degrees east as its standard line, which is 4 hours ahead.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: when it is noon at the prime meridian, what is the local solar time at 75 degrees west? Answer: 75 / 15 = 5 hours behind, so it is 7 a.m.

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Describe the solar system, latitudes and longitudes: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 3 Geography: maps and landforms Weeks 19 to 27Remember

In this unit you work on 'Geography: maps and landforms', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: read maps and describe major landforms. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Geography: maps and landforms' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: read maps and describe major landforms. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Scale 1:50,000: 2 cm on the map is 1 km on the ground. Contour lines close together show a steep slope. Method: Model run for 'Geography: maps and landforms'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Use latitude and longitude. The Equator is 0 degrees latitude. Dubai is near 25 degrees North and 55 degrees East; Mumbai is near 19 degrees North and 73 degrees East. The Tropic of Cancer, 23.5 degrees North, crosses both India and the UAE.
  2. Step 2, Work a scale problem. On a map, 1 cm stands for 25 km. A road measuring 6.4 cm is 6.4 x 25 = 160 km long. A larger scale such as 1:50,000 shows more detail for a small area than 1:5,000,000.
  3. Step 3, Compute time from longitude. The Earth turns 15 degrees each hour. The UAE uses UTC+4 and India UTC+5:30, so when it is 10:00 in Dubai it is 11:30 in Mumbai. India's time is 1 hour 30 minutes ahead.
  4. Step 4, Read a contour map. Contour lines join points of equal height. If the lines are spaced 20 m apart and packed closely, the slope is steep; if they are wide apart, it is gentle. A closed ring with rising values marks a hill.
  5. Step 5, Describe landforms with an example. Mountains: Himalayas, with Everest at about 8,850 m (check the current figure). Plateau: Deccan. Plains: Indo-Gangetic. Desert: Thar. Model answer: 'A plateau is a flat-topped highland with steep sides; the Deccan lies in southern India.'
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a map scale is 1 cm = 10 km, and two towns are 7.5 cm apart. Model answer: 'The distance is 7.5 x 10 = 75 km. The towns are 75 km apart on the ground.'

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Read maps and describe major landforms: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

Unit 4 Civics: diversity and government Weeks 28 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Civics: diversity and government', taught in a way that is with notes, a worked line and a self-mark. The target is clear: describe diversity, government and local governance. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Place it: find where and when 'Civics: diversity and government' happens, on a map, a timeline or in a local example.
  • Gather: read or listen to two short sources and note who is speaking and why.
  • Use it: describe diversity, government and local governance. Give a reason and one piece of evidence for every point.
  • Weigh it: say what another person might think, and why, before you decide.
Worked example. Solved: Diversity is many languages, faiths and customs in one country; equality is the same rights for everyone. Give one example of each from your own area. Method: Model run for 'Civics: diversity and government'. Question: what happened, or what is the situation, and why? Sources: two short extracts, a map or table. Notes: who, what, where, when, why. Answer: three sentences, each with a reason and a piece of evidence. Check: is there a point of view that you have not considered? What a good answer looks like: facts are correct, reasons are linked to evidence, and one other point of view is named fairly.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe diversity. People differ in language, food, dress, festivals, family customs and ideas. A class of 30 may speak 8 languages at home. India's Constitution lists 22 languages (check the current figure), and the UAE is home to people of a great many nationalities. Diversity gives us more to learn from each other.
  2. Step 2, Describe respect. Judging all people of one group as the same is a stereotype, such as 'girls cannot play cricket'. A prejudice can lead to discrimination, when someone is treated unfairly. We judge a person by what they do, not by group. Unity in diversity means different people live together with respect.
  3. Step 3, Describe what government does. Government makes laws, runs public services and keeps order. In a democracy, people choose it by voting. It has three parts: the legislature makes laws, the executive carries them out, and the judiciary settles disputes and decides if a law has been broken.
  4. Step 4, Describe levels of government. A country has government at the national level, the state or regional level, and the local level for a village or town. Each level has its own tasks. National government looks after defence and foreign matters, while local government looks after the nearest streets, water and lights.
  5. Step 5, Describe local governance. In villages, a council called the panchayat is elected by the villagers, with ward members and a head. A town has a municipality with elected councillors. With 10 wards, 10 members are chosen. They manage water, roads, street lights and rubbish, and they meet publicly so people can ask questions.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: say whether each is local or national: street-light repair, defence of the country, a law for the whole country. Answer: local, national, national (made by the national lawmaking body).

Activity. About 30 to 40 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: a worked task and a corrected copy.

Standard to meet. Describe diversity, government and local governance: at least 8 of 10 marks on a short task, with one line of reasoning for each answer.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Community helpers

On the slide: L1 Basic · Community helpers

Notes: Name three community helpers and say what each does. Notes: use pictures of a nurse, a bus driver and a shopkeeper; learners match each helper to a tool and say one way they help the neighbourhood.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Needs, wants and rules

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Needs, wants and rules

Notes: Tell needs from wants and say why rules help a group. Notes: sort ten picture cards into needs and wants, then vote on one class rule and count the hands: 14 for and 6 against, so 20 voters took part.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Government and markets (this course)

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Government and markets

Notes: Describe local, regional and national government and how a simple market works. Notes: follow one pencil from maker to shop; if it costs 4 to make and sells for 6, the margin is 2, which is 50 per cent of cost. Learners name one service each level provides.

This course: Grade 06 Social science is at this level. Its units include History: early humans to ancient civilisations; Geography: Earth in space; Geography: maps and landforms; Civics: diversity and government.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Rights, duties and prices

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Rights, duties and prices

Notes: Explain how supply and demand affect price and why rights come with duties. Notes: if a bad season cuts mangoes from 100 to 60 crates while buyers stay the same, price tends to rise; learners draw the two lines and predict direction before stating a duty tied to a right.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Reading social data

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Reading social data

Notes: Interpret a table of census or survey data and compare how two institutions work. Notes: a population rising from 2.4 to 2.7 million in ten years grew by 12.5 per cent; learners give two possible reasons and one limit of the data.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Theory and research methods

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Theory and research methods

Notes: Apply core social science theories and methods to a social problem. Notes: compare a survey with interviews for one question. Practice: an economy of 600 billion with 50 million people is 12,000 per person, and learners explain what an average hides, such as unequal shares.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Causal research design

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Causal research design

Notes: Design and critique a causal study with ethics, consent and clear limits. Notes: in a before-and-after comparison, the treated group rises 8 points and the comparison group 3, so the estimated effect is 5 points if trends would have run parallel. Learners test that assumption.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
