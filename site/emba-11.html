executive MBA subject 11

Entrepreneurship

Year 1, term D. 8.5 ECTS, about 212 hours, 10 parts. Part 1 is free.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

Task, condition and standard

Task

Develop and pitch a tested business idea with a model, unit economics and a funding ask.

Condition

Given four weeks, a small test budget and access to at least ten possible customers.

Standard

The idea is backed by customer evidence, the unit economics work, and the pitch is clear in five minutes.

Who it is for: Anyone planning to start a business, a side business or a new venture inside a firm.

how this subject works

Free entry, voice and your learning style

Part 1 is free to try. Read it, listen to it and answer it before you decide. Join the programme, or this subject on its own, to open the other parts.

10 parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1 Opportunity recognition 21 hUnderstand: spot

Good opportunities start with a problem many people have, feel often and would pay to solve.

  • Look for frequent, painful, paid-for problems
  • Changes in rules, technology or habits open new gaps
  • Write the problem before the solution

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Shop owners spend 5 hours a week on stock counts. At 10 per hour that is 50 a week, 2,600 a year. A tool at 300 a year is an easy saving.

Activity: List five problems you see and score them for frequency, pain and willingness to pay.

Standard: Five problems scored and the top one explained.

Part 2 Customer discovery 21 hApply: interview

Talk to customers before you build. Ask about their past behaviour, not their opinions of your idea.

  • Ask "when did you last..." not "would you buy..."
  • Talk to at least ten people
  • Look for patterns and stop when you hear the same answers

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Of 12 shop owners, 9 had counted stock by hand in the last week and 7 had lost money from errors: 75% and 58%. The problem is real.

Activity: Interview ten possible customers using past-behaviour questions.

Standard: Ten interviews logged and a pattern stated with percentages.

Part 3 Business model design 21 hCreate: design

A business model shows who you serve, what you offer, how you reach them, how you earn and what it costs.

  • Customer, value, channel, revenue, cost
  • Key partners and resources
  • Change one box and check the others still fit

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Model: small shops, stock counting app, sold through a wholesaler, 25 a month, cost 8 per user. Margin 17 per user per month.

Activity: Draw your business model on one page.

Standard: All boxes filled and they fit together.

Part 4 Lean experiments and MVP 21 hApply: test

A minimum viable product is the smallest thing that tests your riskiest belief. Test, learn, change.

  • Name the riskiest belief
  • Design a cheap test with a pass mark
  • Decide in advance what you will do if it fails

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Belief: shops will pay 25 a month. Test: landing page with a pre-order button shown to 200 shops. Pass mark 5%. Result 14 sign-ups, 7%: pass.

Activity: Run one cheap test with a pass mark set in advance.

Standard: Pass mark written before the test and the result reported honestly.

Part 5 Unit economics 21 hApply: calculate

Unit economics show whether each customer makes or loses money. If one customer loses money, more customers lose more.

  • Acquisition cost per customer
  • Margin per customer per month
  • Months to pay back acquisition cost

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Acquisition cost 85, margin 17 a month: payback 5 months. If customers stay 24 months, lifetime margin = 408.

Activity: Calculate payback and lifetime margin for your idea.

Standard: Both correct and one way to improve them named.

Part 6 Funding and investors 21 hEvaluate: choose funding

Money can come from savings, customers, grants, loans or investors. Each has a price in cost, control or obligation.

  • Customer money is the cheapest funding
  • Loans must be repaid regardless of success
  • Investors take a share of ownership

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. You raise 100,000 for 20% of the company. The value after the investment is 100,000 / 0.20 = 500,000.

Activity: Compare three funding sources for your idea.

Standard: Cost and control of each stated and one chosen.

Part 7 Founding team and equity 21 hAnalyse: agree

Co-founders should agree roles, time, ownership and what happens if someone leaves, in writing, at the start.

  • Split ownership by contribution and commitment
  • Use vesting so shares are earned over time
  • Write it down

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Shares vest over 4 years with a 1-year cliff. A co-founder leaving after 2 years keeps half of their shares.

Activity: Write a one-page founders agreement outline.

Standard: Roles, split, vesting and exit terms included.

Part 8 Growth and scaling 21 hEvaluate: plan growth

Grow when unit economics work. Scaling a loss-making model only makes the loss bigger.

  • Find one channel that works and push it
  • Watch payback as you grow
  • Hire for the next stage, not the last

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. At 100 customers with payback of 5 months, you can grow. At 1,000, acquisition cost rises to 120: payback becomes about 7 months, still acceptable if customers stay 24 months.

Activity: Plan the next 12 months of growth with one main channel.

Standard: Channel chosen with numbers and a payback limit set.

Part 9 Legal structure and IP 21 hUnderstand: choose

The legal form affects tax, liability and how you raise money. Protect names, code and designs early.

  • Limited company separates personal and business risk
  • Register your name and key marks
  • Keep code and designs owned by the company

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. A sole trader with 50,000 of debt is personally liable. A limited company with the same debt usually limits loss to what was invested, unless personal guarantees were given.

Activity: Choose a legal form and list what you must protect.

Standard: Form justified and at least three protections listed.

Part 10 Pitch day 21 hCreate: pitch

A pitch tells a short story: problem, solution, proof, model, team, ask.

  • Five minutes, ten slides at most
  • Show customer evidence
  • End with a clear ask

Worked example: Worked-example figures, in your currency. Ask: 100,000 for 20%, to reach 1,000 shops in 12 months with payback of 7 months.

Activity: Deliver a five-minute pitch to a panel.

Standard: Every section present, numbers correct, questions answered.

ten checks

Check yourself

Say or write your answer first, then read the model answer.

  1. Q1. What makes a good opportunity?
    Model answer: A frequent, painful problem people will pay to solve. (Covered in part 1: Opportunity recognition.)
  2. Q2. Why ask about past behaviour?
    Model answer: Opinions about the future are unreliable; past actions are facts. (Covered in part 2: Customer discovery.)
  3. Q3. 9 of 12 said yes. Percentage?
    Model answer: 75%. (Covered in part 2: Customer discovery.)
  4. Q4. Name the five core boxes of a business model.
    Model answer: Customer, value, channel, revenue, cost. (Covered in part 3: Business model design.)
  5. Q5. Why set the pass mark before the test?
    Model answer: So you cannot move the goal after seeing results. (Covered in part 4: Lean experiments and MVP.)
  6. Q6. Acquisition cost 85, margin 17 a month. Payback?
    Model answer: 5 months. (Covered in part 5: Unit economics.)
  7. Q7. 100,000 for 20%. Value after investment?
    Model answer: 500,000. (Covered in part 6: Funding and investors.)
  8. Q8. 4-year vesting, leave after 2 years. Share kept?
    Model answer: Half. (Covered in part 7: Founding team and equity.)
  9. Q9. Why not scale a loss-making model?
    Model answer: More customers make a bigger loss. (Covered in part 8: Growth and scaling.)
  10. Q10. What does a limited company protect?
    Model answer: Personal assets from business debts, in most cases. (Covered in part 9: Legal structure and IP.)
