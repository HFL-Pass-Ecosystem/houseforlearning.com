Earthing and Ground Potential Rise

Understand how earthing keeps people safe, what ground potential rise is, and how to review test records.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner26 hours (1560 minutes) of lessons plus practiceMentor review available
  • Explain why earthing and bonding protect people and equipment.
  • Identify the parts of an earthing system on a drawing.
  • Calculate a simple ground potential rise from fault current and resistance.
  • Evaluate a test record for completeness and plausibility.
  • Communicate the risk and the next action to a non-expert.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Mark up a drawing, calculate a simple rise, judge three test records and explain the risk in 60 seconds.

Condition

Sample drawings and records supplied; calculator; mentor review; no live equipment. About 3 weeks part-time.

Standard

All calculations correct with units; every record judged with a reason; the explanation is understood by a non-expert peer.

Safety: this course gives awareness and method. It does not qualify you to design, test or approve an earthing system. Work on live or fault-carrying equipment is done only by authorised, competent people under your local rules.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Why we earth: the three jobs 3 hours (180 minutes)Understand

An earthing system gives fault current a safe, low-resistance path back to its source, holds exposed metal near earth potential, and lets protection operate quickly. Three jobs: protect people, protect equipment, keep the supply stable.

  • Fault current: when insulation fails, current flows through the fault path. A good earth path makes the current large enough for a protective device to trip quickly.
  • Touch voltage: the voltage between a metal part a person touches and the ground under their feet. Step voltage: the voltage between two feet on the ground.
  • Bonding: joining metal parts so they rise and fall together, so no dangerous difference appears between them.
  • Earthing is not a substitute for protection devices; it makes them work.
Worked example. A pump casing becomes live through damaged insulation. With good earthing, a large fault current flows and the breaker trips in a fraction of a second. With poor earthing, the casing stays live at a dangerous voltage while the breaker does not trip.

Activity. Draw one simple diagram of a fault in a small installation, showing the fault path, the protective device and where touch voltage appears. Label each in plain words.

Standard to meet. Diagram shows a complete loop from source to fault and back; three terms used correctly.

Module 2 Soil and resistance basics 3 hours (180 minutes)Understand

How well current spreads into the ground depends on soil resistivity, electrode size and shape, and moisture.

  • Soil resistivity changes with moisture, temperature, salts and layers. Dry sand and rock are poor; moist clay is better. Measure; do not guess.
  • Electrode resistance falls as you add length, depth or parallel electrodes, but with diminishing returns. As a rule of thumb, parallel rods spaced about their own length apart overlap less; the engineer decides the spacing.
  • Seasonal change: measure in the driest season, or allow a margin.
  • Measurement methods are done by a competent person with a calibrated instrument and a written procedure.
Worked example. Illustration with example values: two rods 3 m long placed close together do not give half the resistance of one; spaced further apart they come closer to it. The calculation and the measurement must be done by a qualified engineer; this course teaches you to read and question the numbers.

Activity. Rank the three sites in a table of soil readings (ohm-metres) against a target resistance and write which one needs the most extra electrode length and why.

Standard to meet. Rank correctly, give a reason that mentions moisture or resistivity, and claim no final design.

Module 3 Parts of an earthing system 3 hours (180 minutes)Apply

Learn the names and purposes of each part and read them on a drawing.

  • Earth electrode: rod, plate or buried conductor in contact with soil. Earthing conductor: links the installation to the electrode. Main earthing terminal: the one point where earthing and bonding meet.
  • Protective conductor: runs with the circuits to every exposed metal part. Equipotential bonding: links pipes, structural steel and other conductive parts.
  • Earth mat or grid: a buried mesh of conductors used at substations to spread current and limit voltages across the site.
  • Records: label every connection point, record test dates and results.
Worked example. On a drawing: rod (E1), earthing conductor (colour depends on local rules), main terminal (MET), bonding to water pipe, protective conductors to the distribution board.

Activity. Mark up a sample single-line drawing with the six parts above and give each one sentence.

Standard to meet. Identify all six parts correctly and keep the labels readable.

Module 4 Ground potential rise: what it is 4 hours (240 minutes)Analyse

When a large fault current flows into an earth system, the ground around it rises in voltage compared with a distant point (remote earth). This rise is called ground potential rise.

  • Order of magnitude: GPR is roughly the earth fault current that flows into the earth system times the earth system resistance. A small resistance still gives a big voltage at high current.
  • Transferred potential: a metal path (a fence, a cable sheath, a pipe) can carry that rise to a remote point and give a shock where none is expected.
  • People and the public: GPR near substations and pylon bases is why we separate or bond systems carefully, and why isolation rules exist for communications and pipes entering sites.
  • Control methods: lower resistance, grading conductors, insulating or isolating metallic paths, bonding intentionally, limiting fault current and clearing faults fast. Every design is verified by a competent engineer.
Worked example. Example values (illustration only, not for design): 5,000 A flowing into an earth system of 0.5 ohm gives a rise of 2,500 V relative to remote earth. At the edge of the site, the voltage falls with distance; the gradient across your stride is the step voltage.

Activity. Calculate the rise for three given fault currents and resistances; list two metal paths in a sample site plan that could transfer potential.

Standard to meet. Calculate three results correctly with units, name two transfer paths, and write a short note saying a qualified engineer designs and approves the controls.

Module 5 Reading test results and inspection records 4 hours (240 minutes)Evaluate

You will not design the system, but you must judge whether a record is complete and whether results are plausible.

  • A complete record has: date, instrument and calibration date, method, weather and soil condition, positions, values and the person's name.
  • Compare to the design target and to the last result. A sudden rise suggests a broken connection or corrosion.
  • Visual inspection: corrosion at joints, loose clamps, cut conductors, missing labels.
  • Escalate: any result outside the design limit goes to the responsible engineer promptly, as your procedure requires.
Worked example. Example values: last year 2.1 ohm; this year 6.8 ohm on the same electrode. Likely causes: dry season, loose joint, corrosion. Action: retest, inspect joints, tell the engineer.

Activity. Review three sample records; mark each complete or incomplete, plausible or suspicious, and write the next action.

Standard to meet. Judge three records with reasons, and name the escalation for any suspicious result.

Module 6 Touch and step voltage in practice 3 hours (180 minutes)Analyse

When the ground around an earth system rises in voltage, a person can be exposed between a touched part and the ground, or between two feet. Learn to work out the simple difference and see who is at risk.

  • Touch voltage is the voltage of the touched part minus the voltage of the ground where the person stands.
  • Step voltage is the difference between the ground voltage at two foot positions; it falls the further you are from the electrode.
  • People at greatest risk are those close to fences, poles, pump houses and substations during a fault, especially when wet.
  • Controls include grading conductors, insulating surfaces, keeping people away and clearing faults fast. A qualified engineer chooses and approves them.
Worked example. Example values (illustration only, not for design): a fence post rises to 300 V relative to remote earth in a fault, and the ground one metre away is at 120 V. A person touching the post there sees a touch voltage of 300 - 120 = 180 V.

Activity. Calculate the touch voltage for each of three pairs of readings (part voltage and ground voltage), rank the three by risk and write one control for the worst case.

Standard to meet. Calculate three subtractions correctly with units, let the ranking follow the numbers, name the control, and note that a qualified engineer approves it.

Module 7 Bonding and special locations 3 hours (180 minutes)Apply

Some places need extra care because water, metal and people are close together. Learn where bonding is checked and what a record should show.

  • Equipotential bonding links incoming metal pipes, structural steel and other conductive parts so they stay at the same voltage.
  • Bathrooms, kitchens, swimming areas, farms, workshops and fuel stores each have their own local rules.
  • Check for missing links, loose clamps, painted-over joints and unlabelled conductors.
  • Do not make or change bonding yourself unless you are competent and authorised.
Worked example. A metal water pipe enters a workshop and is not linked to the main earthing terminal. A fault on a machine could raise the machine above the pipe, so a person touching both is at risk. Action: record it, tell the responsible engineer, and have a competent person correct it.

Activity. Review a sample workshop plan and list three metal parts that need bonding, then write one line on what you would record for each.

Standard to meet. Name three parts correctly, record what, where and who was told on each record line, and attempt no repair as the reviewer.

Module 8 Safe work, communication and next steps 3 hours (180 minutes)Create

Earthing work is designed, tested and verified only by qualified, authorised people. Your job in this course is awareness, clear records and clear communication.

  • Safe isolation and permits come first: test dead before touching any conductor, and never test or open live earth connections without authority.
  • Storms and faults: stay away from earth electrodes and fences during faults or lightning.
  • Explain to a non-expert in 60 seconds why earthing and bonding matter at home and at work.
  • Next courses: selecting a cable, checking a protection setting, preparing a site test record.
Worked example. A 60-second explanation: 'If a wire inside a washing machine touches the metal case, the earth wire gives the current an easy path, the breaker trips, and you do not get a shock.'

Activity. Write and record (or present to a peer) a 60-second explanation, a one-page checklist for reviewing earthing records, and a three-item next-step plan.

Standard to meet. Explain it so that a non-expert peer understands; write a checklist of at least eight items; put dates in the plan.

Eleven-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the three jobs of an earthing system.

Model answer: Protect people, protect equipment, keep the supply stable (and let protection operate).

Q2. What is touch voltage?

Model answer: The voltage between a touched metal part and the ground under the person's feet.

Q3. Why measure soil resistivity in the driest season?

Model answer: Resistance is usually highest then, so the result is closer to the worst case.

Q4. What is the main earthing terminal for?

Model answer: The single point where earthing and bonding conductors meet.

Q5. Define ground potential rise.

Model answer: The voltage rise of an earth system compared with remote earth during a fault current.

Q6. Calculate GPR for 2,000 A and 0.4 ohm.

Model answer: About 800 V (2,000 x 0.4); example values only, a qualified engineer designs and verifies the real system.

Q7. What is transferred potential and one example?

Model answer: A metal path carrying the rise elsewhere; example a metal fence or cable sheath.

Q8. Name three items a test record must show.

Model answer: Date, instrument and calibration, method, values, person (any three).

Q9. An example result rose from 2 to 7 ohm. What do you do?

Model answer: Retest, inspect joints, and report to the responsible engineer promptly (example: the same day).

Q10. Who designs and approves earthing controls?

Model answer: A qualified, authorised engineer; this course gives awareness only.

Q11. A metal water pipe enters a workshop and is not linked to the main earthing terminal. What do you do?

Model answer: Record it, tell the responsible engineer, and have a competent person correct it; do not make or change bonding yourself. Bonding links metal parts so they stay at the same voltage, so a person touching two of them is not at risk. (Covered in module 7: Bonding and special locations.)
