Task, condition and standard

Task

Size a small wind option for a site with free wind data.

Condition

About 60 hours over 1-3 months. Own tools, free spreadsheet and form tools, and a real practice partner. Mentor and peer circle available. Currency written on every number.

Standard

Evidence reviewed by a mentor; at least 70% on the 10-question check; two written peer reviews given; every number carries its currency and unit.

Who it is for, requirements and materials

  • Who it is for: learners aged 16 and over, small-business starters, workers moving to a new field, and teams (see B2B, B2C, D2C).
  • Requirements: finish Biofuel Production Overview first, or show equivalent work.
  • Materials included: lesson text, worked examples, case exercise, 10-question check, templates in My page.
  • Teaching team: a trainer from our network, a mentor who reviews your evidence, and a peer circle. Names are given at enrolment.

lesson by lesson

9 modules, in the order you do them

Module 1 How wind makes power and where it is worth it IdeaStudy · 2 hUnderstand

Goal: learn the basic physics and pick a site to study.

  1. Learn the rule: power in the wind = half x air density x swept area x wind speed cubed. Doubling the wind speed gives eight times the power.
  2. Learn the limit: no turbine can take more than about 59 percent of the wind's power (the theoretical limit for any wind turbine). Real small turbines get less.
  3. Choose one real site (a farm, school, shed or village hall) and write what it needs in kWh per month (with units).
  4. Write a one-page question: can a small turbine supply a useful share, and is wind or sun better here?

Evidence you keep: your notes on the rule and the site description.

Module 2 Kick-off circle IdeaShare and mentoring · 3 hUnderstand

Goal: say your goal aloud and agree the evidence you will produce.

  1. Each learner reads their one-page plan aloud (3 minutes).
  2. Peers ask three questions: what is the average wind at hub height, what blocks the wind, what happens when it is still.
  3. The mentor agrees the evidence list for this module: a site wind record, a sizing sheet, a cost and payback sheet, a safety list and a one-page recommendation.

Evidence you keep: your revised plan and the agreed evidence list.

Module 3 Collect wind data for the site ActionPractice · 14 hApply

Goal: find the wind speed at your site from free public data.

  1. From a free public wind atlas or weather portal, download monthly average wind speed for your location (note the height of the data).
  2. Correct to hub height: speed at height h = known speed x (h / known height) to the power 0.14 for open ground (a rough rule; say so).
  3. Mark obstacles on a sketch: buildings, trees, hills. Turbulence near obstacles lowers output.
  4. Judge: below about 4 m/s average small wind is rarely worth it; say what you find and why.

Evidence you keep: the data table, hub-height calculation and obstacle sketch.

Module 4 Resource assessment, safety and grid basics ActionStudy · 2 hApply

Short modules:

  • Resource assessment: average speed matters but so does its spread; use a power curve and a wind speed distribution to estimate energy.
  • Safety: falling masts, blade failure, ice throw, noise and shadow flicker; keep distance, use trained crews, and check planning and aviation rules.
  • Grid basics: off-grid systems use a battery; grid-connected systems need an approved inverter and permission from the network operator.
  • Capacity factor: yearly energy divided by rated power x 8,760 hours; small turbines often reach low capacity factors.

Check: 10 questions at the end of the module.

Module 5 Size the turbine and estimate yearly energy ActionPractice · 14 hApply

Goal: estimate yearly output with a simple method.

  1. Choose a swept area (rotor diameter squared x 0.785). Use a realistic power coefficient of 0.25 to 0.35 for a small turbine.
  2. Estimate average power = 0.5 x 1.2 kg/m3 x area x (average speed cubed) x coefficient. Warning: using average speed cubed under-counts gusty sites; note this limit.
  3. Energy per year = average power x 8,760 h. Compare with the site's use.
  4. Run a low, middle and high case and show the spread.

Evidence you keep: the sizing sheet with three cases.

Module 6 Site-review circle ValueShare and mentoring · 6 hEvaluate

Goal: challenge each other's numbers.

  1. Swap sizing sheets. Check the arithmetic and the assumptions.
  2. Ask: is the wind at the right height, is the site open, is the coefficient honest.
  3. A mentor with energy experience marks the weakest assumption in each sheet.
  4. Correct your sheet.

Evidence you keep: the peer checks and the corrected sheet.

Module 7 Cost it and write the recommendation ValuePractice · 14 hEvaluate

Goal: decide whether wind, sun or neither is the better choice.

  1. List costs: turbine, mast, foundation, inverter or battery, install, permits, upkeep. Currency on every line.
  2. Simple payback = net cost divided by yearly saving. Yearly saving = energy produced x price per kWh.
  3. Compare with a solar option of similar cost using free solar data.
  4. Write a one-page recommendation with three risks and one decision.

Evidence you keep: cost sheet, payback and recommendation.

Module 8 Review room EmpowermentShare and mentoring · 3 hCreate

Goal: show your evidence and judge two peers fairly.

  1. Present to a review room in 8 minutes: the site, the wind data, the estimate, the payback and the recommendation.
  2. Give two peers written feedback: one thing that is strong, one thing to fix.
  3. Get a written note from the site owner or a local energy adviser.

Evidence you keep: slides or notes, two peer reviews, and the note.

Module 9 Check and next step RegenerationStudy · 2 hCreate

Goal: close the module and plan what comes next.

  1. Take the 10-question check. Pass mark is 70% (draft).
  2. Write a 90-day next-step plan: what you will do, where, with whom, and what you will learn next. Good follow-on courses: Solar Power Design and Installation Basics, Water Management and Conservation, Greenhouse Gas Auditing.
  3. Contribute back: share your sizing sheet or obstacle checklist so the next learner starts higher. Accepted items earn credits (rates will be confirmed before opening).

Evidence you keep: check result and the 90-day plan.

practice material and assessment

Worked examples, case, check and local law pointers

Worked examples
  1. Rotor diameter 3 m: area = 3.14159 x 3 x 3 / 4 = 7.07 m2.
  2. At 5 m/s: power = 0.5 x 1.2 x 7.07 x 125 x 0.3 = 159 W. At 10 m/s: power = 1272 W, which is 8 times more (2 cubed).
  3. Hub-height correction: 4.0 m/s measured at 10 m, hub at 20 m: 4.0 x (20/10)^0.14 = 4.41 m/s.
  4. Yearly energy at average power 100 W (rough): 0.1 kW x 8,760 h = 876 kWh. At a price of 0.20 per kWh (currency shown) saving = 175. Turbine cost 2,400 gives simple payback 14 years.
Case exercise

A village hall uses 600 kWh a month and has an open field with 4.5 m/s average wind at 10 m. Questions: What is the hub-height speed at 15 m? What share of use could a 3 m turbine supply? Is solar better here? What are the three biggest risks? Write a one-page recommendation.

Check questions (draft set, 10)
  1. How does wind speed change power? 2. What is the theoretical maximum share of wind power a turbine can take? 3. Why does obstacle turbulence matter? 4. What is capacity factor? 5. Name two safety risks of turbines. 6. Why correct wind data to hub height? 7. What is simple payback? 8. Why must a grid-connected system be approved? 9. Why show low, middle and high cases? 10. What is your recommendation?

Rules for this course

No brand names or logos of other companies on any page, file or slide. No personal data in shared files. Paper study only. No climbing, no electrical work, no construction. Check planning, aviation and network rules with an adviser.
