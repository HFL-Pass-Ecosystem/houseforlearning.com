flagship course · GL-10

Cloud Migration as a Service

Plan and run a safe move of one small business workload to cloud services, with cost, security and rollback decided before the move.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5 Specialist54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Plan and run a safe move of one small business workload to cloud services, with cost, security and rollback decided before the move.

  • Understand Explain cloud models, shared responsibility and when moving is not wise.
  • Apply Inventory an existing workload and its dependencies.
  • Analyse Analyse cost, risk and downtime of two migration options.
  • Create Create a migration plan, test plan and rollback plan.
  • Evaluate Evaluate the result after cut-over against agreed measures.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and complete a trial migration of one small workload (for example a website, shared files or a small app) to a cloud service, with a rollback.

Condition

A small real or practice workload, a free or trial cloud account, a spreadsheet, a mentor with operations experience and a peer circle. About 12 weeks part-time.

Standard

Inventory complete; cost compared over 12 months; backup restored once as a test; cut-over done in the agreed window; user complaints within the agreed limit; rollback written.

Who it is for: IT staff, small-business owners, support engineers, college learners. Requirements: Basic computer and network knowledge. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Use practice data or data you own. Never copy customer data to a trial account. Never store passwords or keys in documents.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Cloud models and shared responsibility 4 hUnderstand

Know what you rent and what you must still protect.

  • Models: infrastructure, platform and software as a service.
  • The provider secures the building and hardware; you secure accounts, data and settings.
  • Public, private and mixed (hybrid) clouds.
  • When not to move: tight latency, special rules, tiny savings.
  • Draw a two-column chart of what the provider looks after, such as buildings and hardware, and what you look after, such as accounts, settings and data.
  • Compare three service types: rented machines you manage, a managed platform you configure, and ready software you simply use, noting which duties shift to you in each.
  • Write down which cloud services you already use without knowing, such as free email or file sharing.
  • Note the exit route: how you would leave a provider if prices rise.
Worked example. A shop moves email to a software service: the provider patches servers, but the shop still must set strong sign-in and remove leavers.

Activity. Draw a responsibility table: provider versus you for five items.

Standard to meet. Table is correct for each model.

Common mistake. Assuming the provider secures everything.

Second worked example. A shop leaves a storage area public by mistake; that is the shop's responsibility, not the provider's.

Check yourself. List three things you still secure after the move.

Module 2 Inventory and dependencies 6 hUnderstand

You cannot move what you have not listed.

  • List servers, apps, data size, users, licences and links to other systems.
  • Mark what must run together.
  • Find hidden items: scheduled jobs, printers, old shares.
  • Record owner and business importance.
  • List every application, its owner, its data size in gigabytes, its users and the other systems it calls; aim for one row per item in a simple sheet.
  • Trace dependencies by asking what breaks if each item stops, and group items that must move together so you do not split a pair across two places.
  • Interview users about which systems they cannot work without, and rank them.
  • Note licence terms; some software cannot move without a new licence.
Worked example. A web shop depends on a payment link, an email sender and a nightly stock file. Missing the last would break orders.

Activity. Build an inventory of at least 8 items with owners.

Standard to meet. Dependencies drawn and checked with the owner.

Common mistake. Forgetting hidden links such as a nightly job.

Second worked example. The nightly stock export fails after the move because the old server had the schedule.

Check yourself. Which hidden items did your inventory find?

Module 3 Choose the approach 8 hApply

Lift as-is, tweak, or rebuild: each has cost and risk.

  • Rehost (as-is) is quick; replatform (small change) saves more; rebuild costs most.
  • Compare on cost, time, risk and skill needed.
  • Pick by business need, not by fashion.
  • Decide a move order: low risk first.
  • Sort each workload into move as is, adjust slightly, rebuild, replace with ready software, or retire, and give a one-line reason for each choice.
  • Start with a low-risk, low-value workload as a pilot so the team learns the process before touching anything the business depends on.
  • Run a pilot with a low-risk system first, such as shared files.
  • Record skills needed and who will learn them.
Worked example. Option A: rehost, 2 weeks, risk low, saving 10%. Option B: rebuild, 3 months, saving 35%. For a small firm Option A first.

Activity. Score two options on a one-to-five table.

Standard to meet. Choice backed by scores and a reason.

Common mistake. Choosing a rebuild when a rehost would do.

Second worked example. A 12-person firm rehosts first and plans a rebuild later, saving three months.

Check yourself. Why did you choose your option?

Module 4 Cost model 8 hApply

Cloud bills surprise people who skip the sums.

  • Count compute, storage, data out, support and backups.
  • Use the provider's free calculator, then add 20% margin.
  • Compare with current costs over 12 months, including staff time.
  • Set budget alerts.
  • Estimate monthly cost as machine hours, storage in gigabytes, data transfer out and support, and compare with your current yearly cost divided by twelve.
  • Add a 15 percent buffer for items you forgot, and set a budget alert at 80 percent of your monthly limit so overspend is noticed early.
  • Include staff time for the move and for training.
  • Compare reserved and pay-as-you-go prices for steady loads.
Worked example. Current: 400 per month. Cloud: 220 compute + 60 storage + 30 backup = 310; plus 20% margin = 372. Saving 28 per month, 336 a year (state currency).

Activity. Build a 12-month cost table.

Standard to meet. All cost lines shown with currency; margin included.

Common mistake. Comparing prices without data-out and support.

Second worked example. A cheap plan charges for data leaving; adding 40 GB a month costs 4.00.

Check yourself. Which cost line surprised you?

Module 5 Security and data 8 hAnalyse

Move the data safely and keep it private.

  • Strong sign-in with a second step; least access.
  • Encrypt data in transit and at rest.
  • Know where data is stored; follow local data law.
  • Log who did what.
  • Classify data into public, internal and confidential, and decide for each class where it may be stored and who may reach it.
  • Turn on multi-step sign-in for every admin account on day one and use separate accounts for daily work and for administration.
  • Check the provider's location for data and the law that applies.
  • Set automatic alerts for odd sign-ins and large downloads.
Worked example. Staff accounts: 12. Admin rights needed by 2. Remove admin from 10 and turn on the second step for all 12.

Activity. Write a security checklist and tick it for your workload.

Standard to meet. Checklist complete; one gap fixed.

Common mistake. Skipping the second sign-in step for admins.

Second worked example. Two admin accounts without a second step are fixed before the move.

Check yourself. How many accounts lack a second step now?

Module 6 Test, back up and rehearse 10 hCreate

A test move shows problems when they are cheap.

  • Copy a small slice first.
  • Restore a backup once to prove it works.
  • Time the move: size divided by speed.
  • Write the cut-over steps minute by minute.
  • Restore a backup into a clean area and open the files; a backup that has never been restored is only a hope.
  • Rehearse the full move on a copy at least once and time each step, so you know whether the cut-over fits inside the agreed window.
  • Rehearse the rollback as well as the move.
  • Keep a list of who must be called if a test fails.
Worked example. 200 GB over 20 MB/s is 10,000 seconds, about 2.8 hours; plan a 4-hour window.

Activity. Run a trial move and a restore test.

Standard to meet. Restore works; time measured.

Common mistake. Never restoring a backup.

Second worked example. First restore fails because the key is missing; fix the key process before cut-over.

Check yourself. How long did your restore take?

Module 7 Cut-over and rollback 6 hEvaluate

Move in a quiet window with a way back.

  • Tell users in advance.
  • Freeze changes, move, test, switch.
  • Decide a go or no-go point; roll back if tests fail.
  • Keep the old system for an agreed period.
  • Write a numbered cut-over script with times, owners and checks, and agree a go or no-go point, such as 30 minutes before the window closes.
  • Define rollback triggers in numbers, for example error rate above 2 percent or login failures over 10, and keep the old system untouched until day seven.
  • Send two user notices: one week and one day before.
  • Keep a short list of known issues ready for the help desk.
Worked example. At 1 hour 45 minutes login fails for 5 of 12 users; the no-go rule says roll back at 2 hours; fix found, continue.

Activity. Write cut-over and rollback plans.

Standard to meet. Plans have owners, times and go or no-go rules.

Common mistake. No rollback rule.

Second worked example. Go or no-go at 2 hours; at 1h50 five users cannot sign in; roll back with a clear plan.

Check yourself. What is your no-go rule?

Module 8 Measure and hand over 4 hCreate

Prove the move worked and teach the owner.

  • Compare speed, cost, complaints and downtime with goals.
  • Update the inventory and costs.
  • Train users and write a one-page guide.
  • Plan 90 days of improvements.
  • Compare after 30 days against the baseline for speed, uptime, cost and user complaints, and present the four numbers on one page.
  • Hand over a runbook with accounts, backups, alerts and contacts, and run a short walk-through so the owner can do each task without you.
  • Review the first bill against the plan within 30 days.
  • Schedule a six-month check for unused resources and security settings.
Worked example. Downtime 1.5 hours against 4 allowed; complaints 2 against 5 allowed; cost 7% lower.

Activity. Write a results page and a 90-day plan.

Standard to meet. Results compare to targets with numbers.

Common mistake. Declaring success without numbers.

Second worked example. Cost fell 7% and downtime was 1.5 hours against 4 allowed.

Check yourself. What are your three measured results?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The Friday move

A firm plans a move on Friday afternoon with only one technician.

Question: What is the risk?

Model answer: Problems appear when staff are leaving and help is thin. Move in a quiet window with two people and a rollback plan.

Case 2. The bill shock

A trial bill is three times the estimate.

Question: What do you check?

Model answer: Data egress, always-on test machines and storage tiers; add budget alerts and shut down unused resources.

Glossary

Rehost
Moving as-is.
Replatform
Moving with small changes.
Cut-over
The moment users switch to the new system.
Rollback
Returning to the old system.
Shared responsibility
Split of security duties between provider and customer.
Egress
Charges for data leaving the cloud.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: inventory, option scores, cost table, security checklist, restore test, cut-over and rollback plans, results page. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What does shared responsibility mean?

Model answer: The provider secures the platform; you secure accounts, data and settings.

Q2. Why take an inventory first?

Model answer: You cannot plan or price what you have not listed.

Q3. Name three migration approaches.

Model answer: Rehost, replatform and rebuild.

Q4. What costs hide in cloud bills?

Model answer: Data out, support, backups and extra storage.

Q5. Why test a restore?

Model answer: A backup you cannot restore is not a backup.

Q6. How do you estimate transfer time?

Model answer: Data size divided by speed.

Q7. What is a go or no-go point?

Model answer: A time at which you decide to continue or roll back.

Q8. Why keep the old system a while?

Model answer: It gives a safe way back if problems appear late.

Q9. What protects accounts best?

Model answer: Strong sign-in with a second step and least access.

Q10. What proves success?

Model answer: Measured results against agreed goals.

ten locations and global

Your place

Data location and privacy rules differ by place: check where the provider stores data and which law applies. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a Brainport-area small firm.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC). Partner to practise with: SEBRAE small-business support, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or a SkillsFuture-linked training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a TAFE or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico (Central America)

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
