How long can a cable be?

A longer cable has more resistance, so less fault current flows. If the fault current gets too small, the protection may not trip quickly. This lesson shows the idea with a worked example.

Learning material, not a design or safety instruction. Electrical work must be done by qualified people following the maker's instructions and local rules. Test dead before touching, and never rely on this page for a live-work decision.

The idea

  • In a short circuit between a live conductor and neutral or earth, the current flows out along the cable and back again. This path is called the fault loop.
  • The longer the cable and the thinner its conductor, the more resistance in the loop and the less current flows in a fault.
  • Protective devices trip fast only above a certain current. If the cable is too long, the fault current can fall below that level and the device trips slowly or not at all.

A worked example

  • Take an example copper cable of 4 mm² and 100 m, with a 230 V supply to earth and a 32 A circuit (all worked-example figures).
  • Loop resistance is about 2 × 0.0225 × 100 ÷ 4 = 1.125 ohms. Fault current is about 230 ÷ 1.125 = 204 A.
  • A breaker of this size may need roughly 160 to 320 A to trip instantly. At about 204 A it may trip slowly, so the cable is near the limit.
  • This is an illustration with rounded example numbers. Real designs are done and verified by qualified people, using the supply source resistance, warmer cable resistance, and the maker's tables.

What sets the longest workable length

  • The cable cross-section: thicker conductors allow longer runs.
  • The protective device: its type and its rating.
  • The supply voltage, and the room temperature.
  • The number of cores and how the cable is laid.

A second check: voltage drop

Even when protection works, a long cable drops voltage under load. Motors and electronics need near-rated voltage, so check voltage drop separately from the fault-current check.

If either check fails, the options a qualified designer considers are a thicker cable, a shorter route, or a device with a lower trip level.

task, condition, standard

What you will be able to do

  • Task. Estimate whether a cable run is short enough for its protective device to trip quickly in a fault, and check voltage drop separately.
  • Condition. Given cable size, length, supply voltage and device rating, using rounded teaching figures, with real designs checked by qualified people against the maker's tables and local rules.
  • Standard. The learner calculates loop resistance and fault current for a run, compares it to the device trip level, and proposes a fix when the run is too long.

four parts

The parts, with worked examples

Part 1: The fault loop (15 minutes; Understand: Explain)

In a fault, current runs out along one conductor and back along another. That round trip is the fault loop, and its resistance decides how big the fault current gets.

  • Loop length is twice the cable length, because current goes out and comes back.
  • Resistance rises with length and falls as the conductor gets thicker.
  • Less fault current means a protective device may trip slowly or not at all.

Worked example. Take a copper cable of 2.5 mm² and 40 m. Use 0.0225 ohm square millimetres per metre for copper (a rounded teaching figure, as are all the numbers in this lesson). The loop resistance is 2 × 0.0225 × 40 ÷ 2.5 = 1.8 ÷ 2.5 = 0.72 ohms. Doubling the length to 80 m would double this to 1.44 ohms. Halving the conductor to 1.25 mm² would also double it. Length and size pull in opposite directions.

Activity. Calculate loop resistance for three lengths of 2.5 mm² cable: 20 m, 40 m and 60 m. Plot or list the results and describe the pattern.

Standard. Learner uses the loop formula correctly and describes the effect of length and size.

Part 2: Fault current and trip level (15 minutes; Apply: Calculate)

Once you have the loop resistance, the basic rule current = voltage ÷ resistance gives the fault current. Compare it with what the device needs to trip fast.

  • Fault current is supply voltage divided by loop resistance in this simple model.
  • A device trips fast only above a trip level, often several times its rating.
  • Real designs also include source resistance and warm cable resistance, so a margin is needed. All figures here are example values; a qualified electrician must verify any real design against the maker's tables and local rules.

Worked example. Continue with the 0.72 ohm loop and a 230 V supply. Fault current is 230 ÷ 0.72 = about 319 A. Suppose the 16 A device needs about 10 times its rating, so 160 A, to trip instantly. Since 319 is above 160, it passes with a margin of about 2 times. Now try 6 mm² over 120 m: loop is 2 × 0.0225 × 120 ÷ 6 = 0.9 ohms, giving 230 ÷ 0.9 = 256 A. A 32 A device needing 320 A fails, since 256 is below 320.

Activity. Run the same check for 4 mm² over 60 m with a 20 A device needing 10 times its rating. State pass or fail and the margin.

Standard. Learner calculates fault current, compares with the trip level and states pass or fail.

Part 3: What sets the longest workable length (15 minutes; Analyse: Compare)

Four things set how long a run can be. Changing one of them is how you fix a run that fails.

  • Conductor cross-section: thicker allows longer.
  • Protective device type and rating: a lower trip level allows longer.
  • Supply voltage and temperature: warm cable has higher resistance.
  • Core count and how the cable is laid also affect the result.

Worked example. The failing 6 mm² run at 120 m gave 256 A against 320 A needed. Option one: move up to 10 mm². Loop is 2 × 0.0225 × 120 ÷ 10 = 0.54 ohms and fault current is 230 ÷ 0.54 = about 426 A. That passes. Option two: keep 6 mm² and ask for a device with a lower trip level, for example one needing 5 times its rating, so 160 A, and 256 passes. Option three: shorten the route to 80 m, giving 0.6 ohms and 383 A, which also passes. Three fixes, each with a cost.

Activity. For the failing run, list the three fixes. Choose one and say what extra it costs (money, space, or a different device).

Standard. Learner names the four factors and proposes at least two valid fixes with numbers.

Part 4: Voltage drop as a second check (15 minutes; Evaluate: Check)

A run can pass the fault check and still be poor, because a long cable also drops voltage under load. Both checks must pass.

  • Voltage drop is load current times loop resistance in this simple model.
  • Motors and electronics need voltage near their rating.
  • If either check fails, the run needs to be corrected by a qualified person before use.

Worked example. Take the 2.5 mm² 40 m run with 0.72 ohms loop resistance and a 20 A load. Drop is 20 × 0.72 = 14.4 V. As a share of 230 V that is 14.4 ÷ 230 = about 6.3 per cent. Many designs aim for a smaller share, so this run may be a concern for sensitive loads even though it passed the fault check. Moving to 4 mm² gives loop 0.45 ohms, drop 9 V, about 3.9 per cent. Check the allowed limit in the national wiring rules that apply where you work; a qualified electrician must confirm it.

Activity. Calculate voltage drop as a number and a percentage for a 10 A load on 60 m of 2.5 mm² cable. Say whether you would accept it without checking the local limit.

Standard. Learner computes drop in volts and per cent and keeps it as a separate check from fault current.

The seven-level ladder, slide by slide

  1. Slide 1: L1 Basic: Long cable, weak fault. Two cables of different lengths with arrows showing strong and weak current. Speaker note: Open with the idea that distance weakens the fault current. Keep it visual. No formulas yet.
  2. Slide 2: L2 Explain: The loop. Diagram of current going out along one conductor and back along another. Speaker note: Explain why loop length is double. Work 2 × 0.0225 × 40 ÷ 2.5 = 0.72 ohms on the board.
  3. Slide 3: L3 Apply: Fault current. Worked sum 230 ÷ 0.72 = 319 A next to a trip level of 160 A. Speaker note: Walk through pass. Then show the 6 mm² 120 m case at 256 A failing against 320 A. Remind learners these are rounded teaching figures.
  4. Slide 4: L4 Analyse: Three fixes. Table comparing 10 mm², lower trip device and a shorter route with resulting amps. Speaker note: Learners compare 426, 256 against 160, and 383 A. Discuss cost and practicality of each fix.
  5. Slide 5: L5 Design: Pick a cable. Brief: 90 m run, 230 V, 20 A device needing 200 A. Choose a size. Speaker note: Learners try sizes and show the working. Real designs use maker tables, and qualified people take the decisions.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Evaluate: Voltage drop. Same run with a 20 A load showing a 14.4 V drop and 6.3 per cent. Speaker note: Show that passing one check does not pass both. Ask what limit applies locally and who would know.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Create: Check sheet. Blank sheet with boxes for size, length, loop, fault current, trip level, drop, verdict. Speaker note: Learners build their own check sheet and test it on an invented run. Close by repeating that final design sits with qualified people and the local rules.

where it leads

Jobs this supports

  • Electrical designer. Sizes cables and chooses protection so faults clear quickly and voltage stays healthy.
  • Estimator. Prices cable runs and spots long routes that will need larger sizes.
  • Inspection and test technician. Measures loop values on site and compares them with the design, following local rules.
  • Project engineer. Reviews layouts and makes sure cable routes and ratings match the design checks.
