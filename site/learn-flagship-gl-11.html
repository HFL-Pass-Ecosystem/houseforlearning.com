flagship course · GL-11

Biometric Sensor and Security Solutions

Choose, set up and test a fair, private biometric entry system for a small site, and know when not to use biometrics at all.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5 Specialist54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Choose, set up and test a fair, private biometric entry system for a small site, and know when not to use biometrics at all.

  • Understand Explain how fingerprint, face and other biometric checks work and fail.
  • Apply Set up a small test system with consent and a non-biometric alternative.
  • Analyse Analyse false accepts and false rejects from a trial.
  • Create Create a privacy notice, data plan and fallback process.
  • Evaluate Evaluate whether biometrics are the right tool for the need.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design and trial a small access-control solution with a consent process, error measurements and a no-biometric alternative.

Condition

Practice kit or simulator, a small group of consenting volunteers (at least 10), a spreadsheet, a mentor with security experience and a peer circle. About 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Written consent held; error rates calculated from at least 100 attempts; template stored securely and deleted on request; alternative path works; privacy notice approved by the mentor.

Who it is for: Security staff, facilities managers, IT support, small-site owners, college learners. Requirements: Basic computer skills. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Biometric data is highly sensitive. Use only volunteers who consent in writing. Never copy templates out of the device. Follow local law and delete data when asked.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What biometrics are 4 hUnderstand

A biometric check compares a live trait with a stored template. You will explain enrolment, templates and matching, and compare common traits, so you can describe what a biometric check actually does.

  • Types: fingerprint, face, iris, voice, vein pattern.
  • Enrol once, then match each time; a score decides pass or fail.
  • Templates are numbers, not photos, but still personal data.
  • Match decisions use a threshold you can move.
  • Learn the difference between enrolment, which stores a template, and matching, which compares a new sample with it; each step has its own error sources.
  • List trait options such as fingerprint, face, iris and voice, and note for each one how easily it can be copied, how it changes with age and what hardware it needs.
  • Learn the difference between identification (who are you?) and verification (are you who you say?).
  • Compare how often each trait changes: faces and voices change more than fingerprints.
Worked example. A fingerprint reader gives a score of 0 to 100. Threshold 80: a score of 78 is rejected, 85 accepted.

Activity. Compare three biometric types on ease, cost and risk.

Standard to meet. Table has at least four points each.

Common mistake. Believing a match score is a yes or no fact.

Second worked example. Score 78 against threshold 80 is a reject; at threshold 75 it passes. Same person, different rule.

Check yourself. What threshold did you choose and why?

Module 2 Need first, tool second 6 hUnderstand

Ask whether a simpler lock solves the problem. You will state a security need in one sentence and test whether cards or codes already meet it before considering biometrics.

  • Define who must enter, how often and what is at risk.
  • List alternatives: key, card, code, guard.
  • Biometrics suit high-risk or high-volume places, not every door.
  • Count cost over three years, not just purchase price.
  • Write the need as a sentence with who, where and what is at stake, such as 'staff opening the store room, 20 people, low risk', before looking at products.
  • Compare simpler options such as cards or codes against biometrics on cost, speed, loss and misuse, and keep the simple one if it meets the need.
  • Ask users what would make them comfortable; their answer shapes the privacy notice.
  • Check whether a mobile phone unlock or a smart card already meets the need.
Worked example. A storeroom with 4 users and low-value goods: a key card at 15 each beats a 600 reader (prices are estimates in your currency; check your market).

Activity. Write a need statement and compare two tools.

Standard to meet. Need statement is specific; cost over 3 years shown.

Common mistake. Buying a reader before stating the need.

Second worked example. A store room with four users is served by cards at a fraction of the cost.

Check yourself. What is your need in one sentence?

Module 3 Privacy, consent and law 8 hApply

Trust is the product. Collect the least and say so. You will draft a consent note and a retention rule that collect the least data, protecting both users and the organisation's credibility.

  • Written, informed consent; no pressure.
  • Offer an equal non-biometric way in.
  • Store templates encrypted; never share or sell.
  • Set a retention time and a deletion route.
  • Write a plain consent note saying what is collected, why, how long it is kept, who sees it and how a person can say no or ask for deletion.
  • Store templates rather than raw images where possible, encrypt them, and set a deletion date so data does not sit around after its purpose ends.
  • Keep consent forms apart from the templates so one leak does not link name to data.
  • Name a person responsible for deletion requests.
Worked example. A volunteer withdraws: their template is deleted promptly, within the time promised in the notice, and the log shows the date.

Activity. Write a one-page privacy notice and consent form.

Standard to meet. Notice covers purpose, storage, deletion and alternative.

Common mistake. Hiding the alternative path in small print.

Second worked example. The notice puts "ask for a card" in the first line, not the last.

Check yourself. Where is the alternative described?

Module 4 Setting up a trial 8 hApply

Install carefully; protect the device. You will run a small, well-recorded trial with volunteers under varied conditions, giving you evidence rather than a supplier's claims.

  • Fit at right height and light; keep sensors clean.
  • Change default codes; limit admin rights.
  • Enrol volunteers with two samples each.
  • Keep a log of faults.
  • Enrol 20 to 30 volunteers, record conditions such as light and dry or wet hands, and run at least 100 genuine attempts in total, split across your volunteers.
  • Fix the placement of the sensor at a comfortable height and test it with left and right hands and with people of different heights.
  • Take photos of the installation for the log, avoiding any faces.
  • Test with gloves, dust and bright sunlight, which are the usual failures.
Worked example. Reader is fitted in direct sun; face reads fail at noon. Move it under a hood and failures drop.

Activity. Install or simulate the reader and enrol volunteers.

Standard to meet. All volunteers enrolled; log started.

Common mistake. Skipping the second enrolment sample.

Second worked example. Two samples cut false rejects from 6% to 3% in the trial.

Check yourself. How many samples per person?

Module 5 Measure errors 8 hAnalyse

Accuracy is measured, not promised. You will calculate false accept and false reject rates from trial counts and tune a threshold to the error that costs more.

  • False accept: wrong person let in. False reject: right person refused.
  • Rates = errors divided by attempts.
  • Moving the threshold trades one error for the other.
  • Test with different people, lighting and wet or dry hands.
  • Compute the false accept rate, where a wrong person is let in, and the false reject rate, where the right person is turned away, from your trial counts.
  • Move the threshold in small steps and plot both rates; the point where they meet shows the trade-off, and your choice depends on which error costs more.
  • Make sure at least 100 attempts include different people, not one person many times.
  • Record the threshold used for each test run.
Worked example. Out of 200 right-person attempts 6 are rejected: 3%. Out of 100 impostor attempts 1 is accepted: 1%.

Activity. Run 100 or more attempts and compute both rates.

Standard to meet. Both rates calculated and explained.

Common mistake. Testing only with easy cases.

Second worked example. Dry-hand and wet-hand trials show rates of 2% and 11%.

Check yourself. What conditions did you test?

Module 6 Security of the whole system 10 hCreate

Attackers go after the weak link, not the reader. You will map the whole chain from sensor to door controller and list a likely attack at each link, adding liveness checks where justified.

  • Spoofing: photos, copies; ask about liveness checks (tests that the trait comes from a living person).
  • Protect cables, power and the network.
  • Log entries and review them weekly.
  • Plan for lost power and for a person who cannot enrol.
  • Map the chain from sensor to template storage to the door controller, and list one possible attack at each step, such as a fake finger or intercepted data.
  • Add a liveness check or a second factor where the risk justifies it, and keep logs of failed attempts so repeated tries trigger an alert.
  • Keep the network for the readers separate from the guest Wi-Fi.
  • Test the door release on power loss with the safety officer present.
Worked example. Power fails: door locks release for fire safety and a guard checks the log afterwards.

Activity. Write a threat table with five items.

Standard to meet. Each threat has a control.

Common mistake. Forgetting power loss.

Second worked example. Fire-safety rules often require doors to release on power loss (check your local rule); the log records every release.

Check yourself. What happens to your door in a power cut?

Module 7 Fairness and fallback 6 hEvaluate

Systems must work for everyone. You will compare error rates across groups and design a respectful non-biometric alternative, so the system serves everyone without penalty.

  • Some people cannot use a trait: injury, age, work wear.
  • Check results across groups for gaps.
  • Offer card or code and keep it equally fast.
  • Handle complaints quickly.
  • Compare error rates across groups such as age bands and skin tones in your trial, and flag any group whose rate is more than double the average.
  • Provide a non-biometric route, such as a card and a staff check, and make sure using it carries no penalty or delay beyond a few minutes.
  • Review complaints and add each to the fairness log.
  • Measure the time to enter through each route; the alternative should be about as quick.
Worked example. Rejection rates are 2% for office staff and 9% for cleaners with dry hands: fix with a moisture tip and an alternative.

Activity. Compare results across at least two groups.

Standard to meet. Gap named and a remedy proposed.

Common mistake. Ignoring group differences.

Second worked example. Rates of 2% and 9% for two groups lead to a card option and a hand-care tip.

Check yourself. Which group had the highest rejection rate?

Module 8 Decide, report and review 4 hCreate

Say yes, no or not yet with evidence. You will write a one-page recommendation separating measured from estimated figures and schedule a re-check, so your yes, no or not yet stays honest.

  • Compare error rates, cost and privacy risk with the need.
  • Write a one-page recommendation.
  • Plan a six-month review.
  • Delete trial data and record when.
  • Summarise on one page the need, trial size, error rates, privacy steps, cost and your recommendation, and mark which numbers are measured and which are estimates.
  • Schedule a review at six months to repeat the error checks, because wear, software updates and new users change results over time.
  • Plan a re-test every six months, because hardware ages.
  • Write down what would make you stop using biometrics.
Worked example. Recommendation: use biometrics for the server room only; cards elsewhere.

Activity. Write the recommendation and 90-day plan.

Standard to meet. Decision backed by numbers; deletion recorded.

Common mistake. Keeping trial data "just in case".

Second worked example. Templates are deleted on the planned date and the log records it.

Check yourself. When will your trial data be deleted?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The reluctant employee

An employee does not want a fingerprint reader.

Question: What must you do?

Model answer: Offer an equal alternative at once, with no penalty, and record the choice.

Case 2. The photo trick

A tester opens the face reader using a photo.

Question: What does this show?

Model answer: The system lacks a liveness check; add it or choose another method and record the weakness.

Glossary

Template
Stored numbers made from a trait.
Threshold
The score that decides pass or fail.
False accept
Wrong person let in.
False reject
Right person refused.
Liveness check
A test that the trait comes from a live person.
Enrolment
First recording of a person's trait.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a template?

Model answer: The stored number set made from a trait, used for matching. (Covered in module 1: What biometrics are. To pass that module: Table has at least four points each.)

Q2. Define false accept.

Model answer: The wrong person is let in. (Covered in module 5: Measure errors. To pass that module: Both rates calculated and explained.)

Q3. Define false reject.

Model answer: The right person is refused. (Covered in module 5: Measure errors. To pass that module: Both rates calculated and explained.)

Q4. What does moving the threshold do?

Model answer: Trades false accepts against false rejects. (Covered in module 5: Measure errors. To pass that module: Both rates calculated and explained.)

Q5. Why offer an alternative?

Model answer: Some cannot use biometrics and consent must be free. (Covered in module 3: Privacy, consent and law. To pass that module: Notice covers purpose, storage, deletion and alternative.)

Q6. What must consent be?

Model answer: Written, informed and freely given. (Covered in module 3: Privacy, consent and law. To pass that module: Notice covers purpose, storage, deletion and alternative.)

Q7. How do you compute an error rate?

Model answer: Errors divided by attempts. (Covered in module 5: Measure errors. To pass that module: Both rates calculated and explained.)

Q8. When are biometrics not needed?

Model answer: When a simpler, cheaper lock gives enough safety. (Covered in module 2: Need first, tool second. To pass that module: Need statement is specific; cost over 3 years shown.)

Q9. What is spoofing?

Model answer: Fooling the sensor with a copy. (Covered in module 6: Security of the whole system. To pass that module: Each threat has a control.)

Q10. What happens to data when asked to delete?

Model answer: It is deleted and the date recorded. (Covered in module 3: Privacy, consent and law. To pass that module: Notice covers purpose, storage, deletion and alternative.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Biometric rules are strict in many places and may need a legal basis or a regulator's view: check before any pilot. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

ladder L1 to L7

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Basics of access

On the slide: L1 Basic · Basics of access

Notes: Explain how a lock, a key card, a PIN and a fingerprint each prove who you are, and what each can get wrong. Notes: look at the doors in your building and list how each is opened. Standard: a one-page list a visitor could follow.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Awareness

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Awareness

Notes: Describe what a biometric is, why it cannot be changed if leaked, and when its use feels fair or unfair to the person using it. Notes: discuss two cases, a school gate and a gym, and write one rule you would set for each. Standard: your rules name consent, a choice without biometrics and who can see the data.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Foundation

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Foundation

Notes: Compare a card, a PIN and a biometric reader for one need, such as a staff door, on cost, error, privacy and fallback. Notes: paper exercise with a scoring table and a written reason for the choice. Standard: scores come from stated numbers, and the fallback is described.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Practitioner

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Practitioner

Notes: Run a small trial of an entry solution with a simulator or a card system, count false accepts and false rejects over at least 50 attempts, and test the no-biometric route. Notes: simulator allowed; no real fingerprints or faces stored. Standard: error rates are calculated and the alternative route works.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Specialist

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Specialist

Notes: Design and trial a small access-control solution with a consent process, measured errors and a no-biometric alternative, for a site of 20 people. Notes: write the notice, the retention period and the spoofing test you ran. Standard: error rates reported, consent form tested on a reader, alternative route in daily use.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Advanced

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Advanced

Notes: Design access control for several sites, with a standards check, an audit trail and an independent test. Notes: add a testing lab report and a data-protection review for each site. Standard: the audit trail answers who entered, when and on whose approval, and the review is dated.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Super expert

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Super expert

Notes: Set the enrolment, template and fallback standard for biometric systems and train the installers who apply it. Notes: publish consent and spoofing-test templates and review false-match and false-reject figures each year.
