flagship course · GL-09

Nanosatellites and Space Data

Understand how small satellites work and use open Earth-observation data to answer a real question about land, water or weather.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L5 Specialist54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Understand how small satellites work and use open Earth-observation data to answer a real question about land, water or weather.

  • Understand Explain what a nanosatellite is, how it orbits and what it can sense.
  • Apply Download and open a free open-data image set and read its labels.
  • Analyse Analyse change over time using bands, dates and simple indices.
  • Create Create a one-page data story for a decision maker with maps and numbers.
  • Evaluate Evaluate the limits of the data: cloud, resolution and revisit time.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Answer one real land, water or weather question with free open satellite data and present the result with its limits.

Condition

A computer, a free mapping and image tool, open data from a public space agency or open archive, a free spreadsheet, a mentor and a peer circle. About 12 weeks part-time.

Standard

Data source and licence recorded; two dates compared with a calculated index; limits stated; a decision maker confirms the one-page story is understandable.

Who it is for: Environment, farming, planning and data learners; students; analysts moving into space data. Requirements: Basic spreadsheet skills. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Use only open data and follow its licence. Do not try to identify individuals or private activity from images.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 What a nanosatellite is 4 hUnderstand

Small satellites make space affordable. Learn the building blocks. You will describe a nanosatellite's main parts and size classes, so you can judge what a small craft can and cannot observe.

  • Classes by mass: nano is roughly 1 to 10 kg; a common cube is 10 cm per side.
  • Parts: power from panels, computer, radio, sensors and a way to point.
  • Orbit height decides how often it passes and how sharp the picture is.
  • Why many small satellites together can give frequent views.
  • Compare sizes: a one-unit cube measures about 10 centimetres per side, and several units can be joined, so a three-unit craft is roughly 30 centimetres long.
  • Note that small craft usually carry one or two sensors for a narrow job, and trade size for numbers, so a group of ten can revisit a place far more often than one large craft.
  • Compare a nanosatellite with a large one on mass, cost and lifetime; small ones often last one to five years.
  • Learn why antennas and batteries are the usual limits.
Worked example. A 3-unit cube is 30 cm long. Three units are 3 times the volume of one: more room for a camera and battery.

Activity. Draw a block diagram with arrows showing power, data and commands.

Standard to meet. Diagram shows all main parts and what flows between them.

Common mistake. Thinking "nano" means weak; many missions only need small, cheap sensors.

Second worked example. A 3-unit cube with a camera and a magnetometer is enough to study vegetation patterns, not planes.

Check yourself. What is one thing a small satellite can do well and one thing it cannot?

Module 2 Orbits and passes 6 hUnderstand

An orbit decides what is seen and when. You will predict passes over your location and relate orbit height to revisit time, so you know when fresh data can realistically exist.

  • Low Earth orbit is a few hundred kilometres; one lap takes about 90 minutes.
  • Revisit time: how long until the satellite sees the same place again.
  • A radio pass over a ground station lasts only minutes, so data waits on board.
  • Time of day affects light and shadow.
  • A craft in low orbit circles Earth in about 90 minutes at roughly 500 kilometres height, so it passes a given spot only a few times a day or less.
  • Use a free pass-prediction page to list the next five passes over your location, and note time, maximum height in the sky and duration in minutes.
  • Use a free sky-tracking tool to see real passes of small satellites over your town.
  • Note that cloud over the ground station does not stop radio, but weather can affect some links.
Worked example. At 90 minutes a lap there are 16 laps a day (24 divided by 1.5). Each lap crosses a different strip.

Activity. Find a free pass predictor and note three passes over your town.

Standard to meet. Three passes recorded with time and direction.

Common mistake. Forgetting that revisit time limits how fast you can see change.

Second worked example. A 5-day revisit means a flood lasting two days may never be seen.

Check yourself. What revisit time does your question need?

Module 3 Sensors and bands 8 hApply

Different light bands reveal different things. You will choose a light band and pixel size that fit a question, avoiding images too coarse or too costly for the subject.

  • Visible bands show what the eye sees; near-infrared shows plant health.
  • Resolution: one pixel can be 10 m or 3 m wide.
  • Radar sees through cloud and at night.
  • Match sensor to question.
  • Match band to question: visible light for roads and buildings, near-infrared for plant vigour, thermal for heat, and radar when cloud blocks the view.
  • Check pixel size before downloading: a 10-metre pixel cannot show a single car, but it can show a whole field's greenness, so match scale to subject.
  • Check the width of the area each image covers (the swath); wider means fewer passes.
  • Compare spectral bands on a sample image and write what each shows.
Worked example. A pixel of 10 m covers 100 square metres, so a house of 80 square metres is smaller than one pixel.

Activity. List four questions and the best sensor for each.

Standard to meet. Each question has a sensor with a reason.

Common mistake. Picking a sensor because it is famous, not because it fits.

Second worked example. Cloudy season: choose radar, because optical shows only cloud.

Check yourself. Which sensor did you pick and why?

Module 4 Finding and opening open data 8 hApply

Open data is free but has licences and labels. You will find open scenes with low cloud, read their metadata and respect their licences, so every dataset you use is documented and citable.

  • Search public archives; choose area and dates.
  • Record the source, licence and date for every file.
  • Open the image in a free tool; check bands and coordinates.
  • Keep files small by cropping to your area.
  • Search an open archive for your area and date range, filter cloud cover to under 20 percent, and save the scene identifier in your notes for later citation.
  • Read the metadata file for date, sensor, pixel size and processing level before opening the image, because wrong assumptions here carry into every later step.
  • Keep a data log: file name, date, cloud percent, size and licence.
  • Use small test crops before downloading a whole scene.
Worked example. Cropping a scene of 100 km by 100 km down to 10 km by 10 km leaves 1% of the area and a far lighter file.

Activity. Download two dates for your area and record the licence.

Standard to meet. Source and licence recorded; files open correctly.

Common mistake. Ignoring licence terms on open data.

Second worked example. Licence needs credit and forbids resale; add the credit line to the story.

Check yourself. What is the licence of your data?

Module 5 Reading change over time 8 hAnalyse

Compare two dates with a simple index. You will compare two same-season dates with a simple index and a stated threshold, separating real change from ordinary seasonal growth.

  • A plant index is (near-infrared minus red) divided by (near-infrared plus red).
  • Values near 0 are bare ground; higher values are greener.
  • Check cloud and season before you compare.
  • Compare same season in different years.
  • Compare images from the same season, such as two Junes a year apart, so natural growth cycles do not look like real change.
  • Compute a simple index for both dates, subtract one from the other, and mark pixels that change by more than a stated threshold such as 0.2.
  • Plot your index values on a simple chart; a picture of change is more persuasive than a table.
  • Repeat the calculation for a control area that should not have changed.
Worked example. Near-infrared 0.50, red 0.10: index = 0.40 / 0.60 = 0.67, healthy vegetation. Later 0.35 and 0.20: 0.15 / 0.55 = 0.27, much lower.

Activity. Calculate the index for 10 sample points on two dates.

Standard to meet. 10 points calculated; one clear change found.

Common mistake. Comparing a wet-season image to a dry-season one.

Second worked example. Index fell from 0.62 to 0.35, but the second date is dry season. Compare the same month across years.

Check yourself. Are your two dates in the same season?

Module 6 Quality and limits 10 hCreate

Every dataset has limits. Name them. You will list the limits of your data, such as cloud, resolution and missing ground checks, and test one result against a local source.

  • Cloud and haze block views; mark them.
  • Resolution limits what you can claim.
  • Revisit gaps hide short events.
  • Ground truth: check with a walk or local report.
  • Write a limits box listing cloud cover, pixel size, revisit gap and any ground truth you lack, each with one line on how it could mislead.
  • Where you can, check one result against a photo, a map or a local source; even a single ground check raises confidence more than another hour of processing.
  • Write one sentence per limit saying how it could mislead a decision.
  • Ask a local expert whether the pattern matches what they see on the ground.
Worked example. The scene shows a lower index in March, but cloud covered 30% of it: say so, and test with another date.

Activity. Write a limits table with five rows.

Standard to meet. Each row has limit, effect and a check.

Common mistake. Hiding the limits.

Second worked example. Limits row: "30% of the scene under cloud; effect: index too low; check: use clear dates".

Check yourself. What is your biggest limit?

Module 7 The one-page data story 6 hEvaluate

Decision makers need the answer, a picture and a next step. You will condense an analysis into a one-page story with a question, a labelled map, three findings and a recommendation a decision maker can use.

  • Headline sentence with a number.
  • Two maps: before and after, same scale.
  • Three bullet findings with units.
  • Limits and one recommended action.
  • Structure the page as question, one map or chart, three findings in plain words and one recommendation, and keep the whole page under 250 words.
  • Label every chart with date, source and units, and add a scale bar, so nothing on the page needs a spoken explanation.
  • Write the headline last, after you have seen all results.
  • Use colour scales that are readable for colour-blind viewers.
Worked example. Headline: "Green cover fell 12% in the east block between March and October; check irrigation."

Activity. Create your one-page story.

Standard to meet. Answer first; maps same scale; limits stated.

Common mistake. A map without a scale or date.

Second worked example. Both maps use the same scale bar and are dated; readers can compare at a glance.

Check yourself. Do both maps share scale and legend?

Module 8 Review and next 90 days 4 hCreate

Test the story with a real reader and plan growth. You will test your story on a real reader, log what you would change, and set practical goals for widening your skills.

  • Show it to a decision maker; ask what they would do next.
  • Give two peers written feedback.
  • Share your data-source list with the next learner.
  • Plan 90 days: another area, another index, a skill to add.
  • Keep a log of each project with question, data used, time taken and what you would change, and review it after every third project.
  • Set three 90-day goals, such as one new sensor type, one local partner who uses your results and one published page, and check them monthly.
  • Offer the reader a follow-up in 30 days with a new image date.
  • Add your method as a short recipe so someone else can repeat it.
Worked example. A reader asks "so what?". You add a recommended action in the headline.

Activity. Run the review and write the plan.

Standard to meet. Reader quote recorded; plan dated.

Common mistake. Ending without a decision.

Second worked example. The recommended action: "Inspect the east block this week".

Check yourself. What should the reader do next?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The cloudy month

Your chosen month has 60% cloud.

Question: What do you do?

Model answer: Choose clearer dates, use radar if available, or say the question cannot be answered with that month.

Case 2. The small plot

A farmer asks about a 400 m2 plot with 10 m pixels.

Question: What is the problem?

Model answer: The plot is about 4 pixels, too few to judge; advise a different data source or ground survey.

Glossary

Pixel
The smallest square in an image.
Band
A slice of light the sensor records, such as red.
Revisit time
Time until the satellite sees the same place again.
Index
A number made from bands to describe something like plant health.
Ground truth
A check on the ground.
Open data
Data anyone may use under a stated licence.

assessment

How you are checked

Your evidence: data log with licences, two-date index table, limits table, one-page story and reader quote. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What is a nanosatellite?

Model answer: A satellite of roughly 1 to 10 kg, often built from small cube units. (Covered in module 1: What a nanosatellite is. To pass that module: Diagram shows all main parts and what flows between them.)

Q2. Why is low orbit useful?

Model answer: Closer means sharper pictures and shorter radio delay. (Covered in module 2: Orbits and passes. To pass that module: Three passes recorded with time and direction.)

Q3. What is revisit time?

Model answer: How long until the satellite sees the same place again. (Covered in module 2: Orbits and passes. To pass that module: Three passes recorded with time and direction.)

Q4. Why use radar?

Model answer: It sees through cloud and at night. (Covered in module 3: Sensors and bands. To pass that module: Each question has a sensor with a reason.)

Q5. How is the plant index calculated?

Model answer: (near-infrared minus red) divided by (near-infrared plus red). (Covered in module 5: Reading change over time. To pass that module: 10 points calculated; one clear change found.)

Q6. Why record licence?

Model answer: It says how you may use and share the data. (Covered in module 4: Finding and opening open data. To pass that module: Source and licence recorded; files open correctly.)

Q7. Why check cloud?

Model answer: Cloud changes the numbers and can fake a change. (Covered in module 6: Quality and limits. To pass that module: Each row has limit, effect and a check.)

Q8. What goes in a headline?

Model answer: The answer, with a number. (Covered in module 7: The one-page data story. To pass that module: Answer first; maps same scale; limits stated.)

Q9. What is ground truth?

Model answer: A check on the ground that confirms what the image seems to show. (Covered in module 6: Quality and limits. To pass that module: Each row has limit, effect and a check.)

Q10. Name one limit of 10 m pixels.

Model answer: Objects smaller than 10 m cannot be seen as separate things. (Covered in module 3: Sensors and bands. To pass that module: Each question has a sensor with a reason.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Open-data licences and what you may publish differ by country and agency: read each licence. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

ladder L1 to L7

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Basics of space

On the slide: L1 Basic · Basics of space

Notes: Explain satellite and orbit in plain words, and why some satellites move across the sky while others hover over one place. Notes: use a sky-watching app to find one pass. Standard: three sentences a friend can repeat.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Awareness of space data

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Awareness of space data

Notes: Describe what open satellite data exists (images, weather, ocean, land cover), who publishes it and what licence it carries. Notes: browse a public archive together and write down the licence. Standard: three datasets named with what each shows and its licence.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: First data task

On the slide: L3 Foundation · First data task

Notes: Open and read an image: find a place, read its bands or colours, and note its date, scale and cloud cover. Notes: practise with a small crop of your own town. Standard: a note listing date, resolution and one limit of the image.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Analyst basics

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Analyst basics

Notes: Compare the same place on two dates and describe the change, stating cloud, season and resolution limits. Notes: spreadsheet only, with a table of the numbers you read. Standard: change described in a sentence with a number and one reason it may be wrong.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Specialist

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Specialist

Notes: Answer one real land, water or weather question with free open satellite data and present the result with its limits. Notes: choose a question you can check on the ground, such as a lake edge or a green area. Standard: question, data source, method, result and three stated limits on one page.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Advanced analyst

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Advanced analyst

Notes: Build a time series and automate the repeated steps with free tools, with a data log and a check against ground truth. Notes: script the download and the table, and test the script on a second place. Standard: the series covers at least 12 dates and the script runs unaided on another place.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Super expert

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Super expert

Notes: Lead method and teach teachers. Notes: review data sources yearly.
