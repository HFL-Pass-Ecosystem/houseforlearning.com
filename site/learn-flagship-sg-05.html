Transport and Mobility Services

Plan a small transport or mobility service, check safety and cost, and trial it with real passengers or goods. You learn to read demand, build a timetable, check licences and vehicles, work out cost per trip, protect privacy and analyse delays.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 140 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Plan a small transport or mobility service, check safety and cost, and trial it with real passengers or goods. You learn to read demand, build a timetable, check licences and vehicles, work out cost per trip, protect privacy and analyse delays.

  • Understand Explain demand, supply and timetable.
  • Apply Plan and record trips.
  • Analyse Analyse punctuality and cost.
  • Create Create an improvement plan.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan and trial one small mobility service for 30 days and report safety checks, punctuality, passenger counts and cost per trip, with a ranked improvement plan.

Condition

About 140 hours over 3 to 6 months. A licensed partner vehicle or public transit data, free tools, a mentor and a peer circle.

Standard

Route and timetable design; safety and rule check; punctuality data; cost per trip; improvement plan; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Follow your local law and get written permission before you work with real customers, premises, data or money.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (140 h guided)

Module 1 Demand and supply 10 hUnderstand

Demand is how many people want to travel and supply is how many seats you offer. They rarely match all day. Counting trips by hour shows where the gaps are.

  • Count the number of people travelling in each hour of the day on at least two ordinary days.
  • Compare the count with the seats available in that hour to find the load percentage.
  • Mark the hours where demand is above 100% of seats and the hours where seats are mostly empty.
  • Look for reasons behind peaks, such as school start times or work shifts.
  • A common mistake is to plan from the daily total, which hides the busy hours.
Worked example. From 8 to 9 am, 62 people want a ride and the bus has 50 seats. Load is 62 divided by 50, which is 1.24, or 124%, so 12 people are left behind and the peak needs extra capacity.

Activity. Count trips for a full day on each of two ordinary days at one stop or on one route, and produce an hourly table with seats, demand and load percentage.

Standard to meet. Cover every service hour in your table, calculate the load percentages correctly and name the two busiest and two quietest hours.

Module 2 Routes and timetable 16 hCreate

A good route is simple and a good timetable is regular. Stops should be close to where people need to go. Even gaps between services are easy to remember.

  • Place stops within a short walk of homes, schools and shops, and avoid detours that add little demand.
  • Use an even gap, such as every 15 minutes, so passengers can remember the times without looking.
  • Add a layover at each end so late arrivals do not delay the next trip.
  • Work out the vehicles you need as round-trip time including layover divided by the gap, rounded up.
  • A common mistake is to schedule trips with no layover, so one delay passes down the whole day.
Worked example. A 15-minute gap gives 4 departures an hour, or 12 departures in 3 hours. A round trip takes 40 minutes plus 5 minutes layover, which is 45 minutes, so you need 45 divided by 15, which is 3 vehicles.

Activity. Draft a timetable for one route with stop times, gaps, layover and the number of vehicles needed, and explain your choices in a short note.

Standard to meet. Space the gaps evenly in your timetable, show the layover and vehicle calculation correctly, and check that a peer can read it without help.

Module 3 Safety and licensing 16 hApply

Only licensed drivers and vehicles in good condition may carry passengers. You check licences and run daily vehicle inspections. An emergency plan is ready before the first trip.

  • Check each driver licence and the vehicle documents against the rules, and note the expiry dates.
  • Run a daily inspection of at least 12 items, such as brakes, lights, tyres, seat belts and doors.
  • Do not run a vehicle that fails an item that affects safety, and record what was done.
  • Write an emergency plan with contact numbers, a first-aid kit location and steps for a breakdown or accident.
  • Check with your local authority about the licences, insurance and rules for carrying passengers or goods.
Worked example. A daily inspection has 12 items and 11 pass. The brake light fails, so the vehicle does not run until it is repaired, and the record shows the fault, the fix and who signed it off.

Activity. Create a daily checklist, a licence record sheet and an emergency plan, then complete one practice inspection on a real vehicle with the owner.

Standard to meet. Cover at least 12 items in your checklist, get the owner to sign the practice inspection and confirm your plan with your local authority.

Module 4 Cost per trip 16 hEvaluate

Knowing your cost per trip lets you set a fare and see if the service can last. Costs include fuel or energy, the driver, upkeep and insurance. You divide by trips and by passengers.

  • List the costs for a period: fuel or charging, driver time, repairs and tyres, insurance share and booking or admin.
  • Divide the total cost by the number of trips to get cost per trip.
  • Divide cost per trip by the passengers on average to get cost per passenger.
  • Compare cost per passenger with the fare and decide whether the service covers its costs.
  • A common mistake is to ignore upkeep and insurance because they are not paid every day.
Worked example. One trip costs S$18 and carries 10 passengers, so cost per passenger is 18 divided by 10, which is S$1.80. A fare of S$3 brings in S$30 for the trip, which leaves S$12 above cost.

Activity. Complete a cost sheet for your trial with each cost, cost per trip, cost per passenger and break-even passengers per trip.

Standard to meet. Calculate correctly, give every cost a source and show the break-even number of passengers per trip.

Module 5 Run the trial 36 hApply

You now run the service and record every trip. Honest data on lateness, loads and incidents is the base for improvement. Do not tidy the numbers.

  • Record the planned and actual departure and arrival times for every trip.
  • Count passengers on each trip and note the stop where the loading was heaviest.
  • Record every incident or complaint with the time, place and what was done.
  • Calculate punctuality as the trips on time divided by the total trips, and decide in advance what on time means, such as within 3 minutes.
  • A common mistake is to change the definition of on time after seeing the results.
Worked example. Of 100 trips in the trial, 82 arrive within 3 minutes of schedule. Punctuality is 82 divided by 100, which is 82%, and the 18 late trips are listed with the cause.

Activity. Run the trial for 30 days and submit a full trip log, a punctuality calculation and an incident list.

Standard to meet. Log every trip with times and passenger counts, and calculate your punctuality figure using your original definition.

Module 6 Data and privacy 12 hApply

Passenger data can reveal where people live and work. You collect only what you need and keep it safe. Counting is better than naming.

  • Collect counts of passengers, not names, unless a name is needed for the service.
  • Store the data in a protected file and limit who can open it.
  • Delete data you no longer need after the agreed period and note the date you did so.
  • Tell passengers what you count and why in a short, plain notice.
  • Check with your local authority about the data protection rules that apply to you.
Worked example. Over a week you record 480 anonymous tap counts across 5 stops. There are no names, so the 480 counts show demand, but they cannot be linked to a person.

Activity. Write a one-page data note covering what you collect, why, how it is stored, who sees it and when it is deleted.

Standard to meet. Cover all five points in your note, collect no more than needed and have your mentor review it.

Module 7 Analyse delays 16 hAnalyse

To fix delays you first find where and why they happen. A table of delay by stop and by cause points to the real problem. Fix the cause, not the symptom.

  • Calculate the delay at each stop as actual time minus planned time and record it for every trip.
  • Add up the delays by stop and find the stop where most delay builds up.
  • List the causes you saw, such as slow loading, traffic or late start, and count each one.
  • Rank the causes by how many late trips each one explains.
  • A common mistake is to blame traffic for everything without checking the data.
Worked example. Of 100 trips, 18 were late. At stop 4, 11 of those 18 lost time to slow loading, which is 11 divided by 18, or about 61%, so stop 4 is where to look first.

Activity. Build a delay table by stop and by cause, draw one chart and write two sentences naming the main cause and the evidence for it.

Standard to meet. Match your table totals to your trip log, label the chart with units and support your main cause with at least 50% of late trips.

Module 8 Improve 18 hEvaluate

Improvement is about picking the fixes that give the most benefit for the cost. You compare options, choose, and set a date. You also decide how you will measure success.

  • List at least three possible fixes and the number of late trips each could remove.
  • Estimate the cost of each fix in money and effort.
  • Divide the benefit by the cost to rank the fixes, and choose the best one or two.
  • Write next steps with dates and the measure of success for each.
  • A common mistake is to pick the cheapest fix even when it solves little.
Worked example. Fix A removes 6 late trips a week for S$40 and fix B removes 2 for S$5. Fix A gives 6 divided by 40, or 0.15 per S$1, and fix B gives 2 divided by 5, or 0.4 per S$1, so B is better value although A removes more.

Activity. Present an improvement plan with at least three fixes ranked by impact against cost and a timeline.

Standard to meet. Base your ranking on numbers from your trial and give each chosen fix a date and a measure.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why do you count trips by the hour?

Model answer: Demand changes through the day, and hourly counts show where seats are short or empty. (Covered in module 1: Demand and supply. To pass that module: Cover every service hour in your table, calculate the load percentages correctly and name the two busiest and two quietest hours.)

Q2. Why use even gaps between services?

Model answer: Passengers can remember the pattern and plan without looking up each time. (Covered in module 2: Routes and timetable. To pass that module: Space the gaps evenly in your timetable, show the layover and vehicle calculation correctly, and check that a peer can read it without help.)

Q3. Who is responsible for checking licences and vehicles?

Model answer: The operator must check them, and the local authority sets and enforces the rules. (Covered in module 3: Safety and licensing. To pass that module: Cover at least 12 items in your checklist, get the owner to sign the practice inspection and confirm your plan with your local authority.)

Q4. What is the cost per passenger when a trip costs S$18 and carries 10 people?

Model answer: 18 divided by 10 is S$1.80 per passenger. (Covered in module 4: Cost per trip. To pass that module: Calculate correctly, give every cost a source and show the break-even number of passengers per trip.)

Q5. What is punctuality if 82 of 100 trips are on time?

Model answer: 82 divided by 100 is 82%. (Covered in module 5: Run the trial. To pass that module: Log every trip with times and passenger counts, and calculate your punctuality figure using your original definition.)

Q6. Why count passengers instead of recording who they are?

Model answer: Counts give the information you need while protecting personal privacy. (Covered in module 6: Data and privacy. To pass that module: Cover all five points in your note, collect no more than needed and have your mentor review it.)

Q7. What is the first step in finding why trips are late?

Model answer: Find where along the route the delay happens. (Covered in module 7: Analyse delays. To pass that module: Match your table totals to your trip log, label the chart with units and support your main cause with at least 50% of late trips.)

Q8. What ranks fixes best?

Model answer: Their impact compared with their cost. (Covered in module 8: Improve. To pass that module: Base your ranking on numbers from your trial and give each chosen fix a date and a measure.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Plan and trial one small mobility service for 30 days and report safety checks, punctuality, passenger counts and cost per trip, with a ranked improvement plan. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Route and timetable design; safety and rule check; punctuality data; cost per trip; improvement plan; at least 70% on the final check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

  5. Slide 5: L5 Specialist, One narrow area to depth. Speaker note: Learner picks one area, for example Run the trial (the longest module at 36 h), and goes deeper than this course asks. Learner then teaches a peer circle of three or four. Note for the guide: check the teaching, not only the work.
  6. Slide 6: L6 Advanced, Lead a team and review evidence. Speaker note: Learner leads a small team or designs a service in this field and marks other people's evidence against the rubric. Roles this points to: Transport coordinator assistant; Dispatcher assistant; Mobility planner assistant. Note for the guide: sample three records from the team and compare marks.
  7. Slide 7: L7 Super expert, Set and audit the standard. Speaker note: Learner writes the method for Transport and Mobility Services as a short guide, audits other learners' evidence, and publishes worked examples back to learners. Note for the guide: worked examples keep currency and units on every number and are checked by a second expert.

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.
