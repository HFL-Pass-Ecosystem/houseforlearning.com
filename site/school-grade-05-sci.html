Grade 05: Science

7 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 05 Science, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 7 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

unit by unit

7 units with worked examples

Unit 1 Living things: animals and life styles Weeks 1 to 6Apply

In this unit you work on 'Living things: animals and life styles', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: group animals by diet, habitat and life cycle. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Living things: animals and life styles' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: group animals by diet, habitat and life cycle. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: A camel has wide feet for sand and stores fat in its hump; a fish has gills to breathe in water. Food chain: grass, grasshopper, frog, snake. Method: Model run for 'Living things: animals and life styles'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Group animals by diet. Herbivores eat plants (goat, camel), carnivores eat other animals (lion, falcon) and omnivores eat both (bear, human). Check the teeth: flat grinding molars suit plants, and sharp canines suit meat. Sort eight animals into three columns.
  2. Step 2, Group animals by habitat. Land habitats include desert, forest and grassland. Water habitats include rivers, ponds and the sea. Some animals use both, such as frogs. Place each of your eight animals in a habitat and write one feature that fits it, such as a camel's wide feet.
  3. Step 3, Learn complete life cycles. A butterfly goes through egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa and adult: four stages. Each stage looks very different. A frog goes through egg, tadpole, froglet and adult, and a tadpole lives in water and breathes with gills.
  4. Step 4, Learn simpler life cycles. A grasshopper has egg, nymph and adult: three stages, and a nymph looks like a small adult without wings. Birds and reptiles lay eggs, while most mammals, such as cats and humans, give birth to live young and feed them milk.
  5. Step 5, Put it in a table with columns Animal, Diet, Habitat, Life cycle. Example row: butterfly, herbivore (nectar), garden, egg → larva → pupa → adult. Another: lion, carnivore, grassland, born live and fed with milk. Complete six rows.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: classify a frog by diet, habitat and life cycle. Answer: a carnivore, because it eats insects; it lives in water and on land; its life cycle is egg, tadpole, froglet and adult.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Group animals by diet, habitat and life cycle: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 2 The human body: systems Weeks 7 to 11Understand

In this unit you work on 'The human body: systems', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: describe the skeletal, muscular, respiratory and circulatory systems. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'The human body: systems' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe the skeletal, muscular, respiratory and circulatory systems. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Digestion: mouth, food pipe, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. Incisors cut, canines tear, molars grind. The heart pumps blood round the body. Method: Model run for 'The human body: systems'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Describe the skeletal system. An adult skeleton has 206 bones. It supports the body, protects organs (the skull protects the brain and the ribs protect the heart and lungs) and gives muscles something to pull on. Joints, such as the knee and elbow, let bones move.
  2. Step 2, Describe the muscular system. Muscles pull but cannot push, so they work in pairs. When the biceps in your upper arm contracts, your elbow bends; when the triceps contracts, it straightens. Make a fist, bend your arm and feel the biceps harden.
  3. Step 3, Describe the respiratory system. Air goes in through the nose, down the windpipe, into two bronchi and then the lungs, ending in tiny air sacs called alveoli. There oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out. At rest a child breathes roughly 18 to 25 times a minute.
  4. Step 4, Describe the circulatory system. The heart pumps blood around the body in blood vessels. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins bring it back. Blood carries oxygen and nutrients to every cell. Find your pulse at your wrist: roughly 70 to 100 beats a minute is usual at rest.
  5. Step 5, Calculate heart work. At 75 beats a minute the heart beats 75 x 60 x 24 = 108,000 times a day. Then try exercise: jog on the spot for one minute and count the pulse again. Safety: stop if you feel dizzy or breathless, and sit down.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: name the system that has a biceps and triceps, and one that has alveoli. Then state the heart beats in 2 hours at 75 per minute. Answer: muscular; respiratory; 75 x 120 = 9,000 beats.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Describe the skeletal, muscular, respiratory and circulatory systems: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 3 Plants and reproduction Weeks 12 to 16Understand

In this unit you work on 'Plants and reproduction', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: describe flower parts, pollination and seed dispersal. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Plants and reproduction' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe flower parts, pollination and seed dispersal. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Step 1, Take a hibiscus flower and label: petals (attract insects), sepals (protect the bud), stamen made of anther and filament (male, makes pollen), and pistil made of stigma, style and ovary (female). Draw and label. Step 2, Describe pollination. A bee lands on the anther, pollen sticks to its body, and it carries pollen to the stigma of another flower. Pollen moves from anther to stigma: this is pollination. Wind carries pollen of grasses.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Take a hibiscus flower and label: petals (attract insects), sepals (protect the bud), stamen made of anther and filament (male, makes pollen), and pistil made of stigma, style and ovary (female). Draw and label.
  2. Step 2, Describe pollination. A bee lands on the anther, pollen sticks to its body, and it carries pollen to the stigma of another flower. Pollen moves from anther to stigma: this is pollination. Wind carries pollen of grasses.
  3. Step 3, Follow fertilisation. Pollen grows a tube down the style to the ovary and joins an ovule. The ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes the fruit. A mango's seed is inside the fruit made from the ovary.
  4. Step 4, Seed dispersal matches seed design. Maple seeds have wings and spin in wind. Coconut floats on water. Burr seeds have hooks and cling to animal fur. Pea pods burst open and shoot seeds out. Make a table of seed, method and clue.
  5. Step 5, Why dispersal matters: seeds that fall right under the parent plant compete for light and water. If a plant makes 200 seeds and only the 20 carried away find space, 10 per cent survive in new places.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a seed has a fluffy parachute like a dandelion. Say how it disperses and why. Answer: by wind, since the light fluffy hairs let it float far from the parent.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Describe flower parts, pollination and seed dispersal: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 4 Changes in materials Weeks 17 to 21Understand

In this unit you work on 'Changes in materials', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: tell reversible from irreversible changes and mixtures from solutions. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Changes in materials' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: tell reversible from irreversible changes and mixtures from solutions. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Test: does it float? A wooden spoon floats, a metal spoon sinks. Is it see-through? Glass yes, wood no. Choose a raincoat material: waterproof, light, bendy. Method: Model run for 'Changes in materials'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Separate reversible from irreversible changes. In a reversible change the material can go back to how it was, such as ice melting to water and freezing again. In an irreversible change a new material forms and cannot go back, such as burning paper, baking dough or rusting.
  2. Step 2, Test a reversible change. Put one ice cube in a cup, let it melt and note that it becomes water. Place that water in the freezer and it freezes again. Write 'melting and freezing are reversible: no new substance is made'.
  3. Step 3, Test an irreversible change, carefully. Put 2 spoons of bicarbonate of soda in a tray and add 50 mL of vinegar. It fizzes and makes a gas, so a new substance has formed. Safety: wear goggles, do it with an adult, and never taste or smell closely.
  4. Step 4, Tell mixtures from solutions. Sand and water make a mixture: the grains stay separate and can be filtered out. Salt in water makes a solution: the salt dissolves and seems to vanish, but it is still there. Stir 5 g of salt into 100 mL of water until clear.
  5. Step 5, Get the dissolved material back. Pour the salt solution into a shallow dish and leave it in the sun for a day or two, or heat it only with an adult's help. The water evaporates and white salt crystals stay behind. Dissolving is therefore reversible, and the mass is the same: 10 g sugar and 100 g water give a 110 g solution.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: classify these as reversible or irreversible, and say whether sand in water is a mixture or a solution. Egg frying, ice melting, wood burning. Answer: egg frying irreversible, ice melting reversible, wood burning irreversible; sand in water is a mixture.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Tell reversible from irreversible changes and mixtures from solutions: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 5 Forces, gravity and simple machines Weeks 22 to 26Apply

In this unit you work on 'Forces, gravity and simple machines', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: show levers, pulleys and the effect of gravity. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Forces, gravity and simple machines' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: show levers, pulleys and the effect of gravity. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Force = mass x acceleration. A 5 kg trolley accelerating at 2 m/s squared needs F = 5 x 2 = 10 N. Method: Model run for 'Forces, gravity and simple machines'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Use a lever. A 1 m plank balances on a pivot at 50 cm. Put a 2 kg load 20 cm from the pivot. To balance it place a 1 kg weight at 40 cm on the other side, because 2 x 20 = 1 x 40. Load x distance equals effort x distance.
  2. Step 2, Name the parts: pivot or fulcrum, load, effort. A see-saw has the pivot in the middle, a wheelbarrow has the load in the middle, and tweezers have the effort in the middle. Draw each with labels.
  3. Step 3, Use a pulley. A single fixed pulley on a bar changes direction only: to lift a 1 kg bag you pull down with about 10 N force, the same as the weight, but pulling down is easier than lifting up.
  4. Step 4, Gravity pulls objects toward Earth. A 1 kg mass weighs about 10 N on Earth. Drop a stone and a sheet of paper and the stone lands first because air resists the paper. Crumple the paper and both land almost together.
  5. Step 5, Explain: a machine can make work easier by changing direction or force, but not by giving free energy. A longer lever arm needs less effort but a longer push distance.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a 3 kg load is 10 cm from the pivot. Where should a 1 kg effort be placed to balance? Answer: 3 x 10 = 30, so 30 cm from the pivot on the opposite side.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Show levers, pulleys and the effect of gravity: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 6 Electricity and magnetism Weeks 27 to 31Apply

In this unit you work on 'Electricity and magnetism', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: use circuits and magnets and say how they are used. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Electricity and magnetism' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: use circuits and magnets and say how they are used. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: V = I x R. A 6 V cell across a 2 ohm resistor gives I = 6 / 2 = 3 A. Method: Model run for 'Electricity and magnetism'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Build a series circuit with two 1.5 V cells in a row (3 V total), two bulbs and a switch. Both bulbs glow equally. Unscrew one bulb and the other goes out because the loop is broken.
  2. Step 2, Build a parallel circuit with the same cells, giving each bulb its own branch. Both glow brighter than in series. Unscrew one bulb and the other stays lit, which is why house lights are wired in parallel.
  3. Step 3, Make an electromagnet. Wind 30 turns of insulated wire around an iron nail and connect it through a switch to two 1.5 V cells. Suppose it picks up 6 paper clips; 60 turns should pick up more. Switch off when not testing, as the wire warms.
  4. Step 4, Test a bar magnet on a tray of objects. It attracts iron nails, steel pins and steel paper clips but not plastic, wood, copper or aluminium foil. Two north poles repel; a north and south pole attract.
  5. Step 5, Uses: electromagnets lift scrap metal in a yard and ring doorbells, and compasses use magnets to point north. A switch in the circuit turns an electromagnet off to drop its load.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: if the nail picks up 6 clips with 30 turns, how many clips would you predict with 90 turns, and why? Answer: roughly 18, because more turns make a stronger field, assuming the same supply.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Use circuits and magnets and say how they are used: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 7 Earth, space and environment Weeks 32 to 36Understand

In this unit you work on 'Earth, space and environment', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: describe the moon's phases, planets and how to protect resources. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Observe: see, touch, measure or watch a safe demonstration linked to 'Earth, space and environment' and write what you notice.
  • Explain: use the key words of the unit to say what is happening and why, in a few lines.
  • Use it: describe the moon's phases, planets and how to protect resources. Use a diagram, table or short calculation where it fits.
  • Test and conclude: say what evidence supports your answer and what you would change.
Worked example. Solved: Eight planets orbit the Sun and Earth is third. The Moon orbits Earth in about 27 days. Method: Model run for 'Earth, space and environment'. Question: what do we want to find out? Prediction: one sentence. Method: three safe steps with the equipment named. Record: a small table with units. Conclusion: one sentence that uses the evidence and the key words of the unit. What a good answer looks like: a fair test, measured values with units, and a conclusion that matches the table.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Track moon phases for a month on a calendar. Week 1: thin crescent, then first quarter (half lit) on about day 7. Day 14 or 15: full moon. Day 22: last quarter. Day 29: new moon, almost not visible.
  2. Step 2, Explain with a model. Light a lamp in a dark room and hold a ball at arm's length. Move it around your head. You see the lit part change shape because you view different amounts of the lit half. The cycle takes about 29.5 days.
  3. Step 3, Order the planets from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. The first four are rocky and the last four are giants. Jupiter is the largest. Use a mnemonic of your own to remember.
  4. Step 4, Protect resources with numbers. Suppose a tap left running during brushing wastes 6 L; for a family of 4 twice a day that is 4 x 2 x 6 = 48 L daily. Closing the tap saves 48 L, or 336 L a week.
  5. Step 5, Other actions: reduce, reuse and recycle waste, switch off unused lights, plant trees, and use cloth bags. Each action lowers the use of water, energy or raw materials.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: how many days from full moon to the next full moon, and what phase comes about 7 days after a new moon? Answer: about 29.5 days; first quarter.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Describe the moon's phases, planets and how to protect resources: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

levels L1 to L7

Where this course sits on the ladder

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack. A level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Using my five senses

On the slide: L1 Basic · Using my five senses

Notes: Name the five senses and use them to describe a safe object. Notes: place a leaf, a smooth stone and a soft cloth in a bag; learners touch and describe each. Never taste or smell unknown things; teach children to ask first. Grades 1 and 2.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Fair tests (this course)

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Fair tests

Notes: Describe a fair test by naming what is changed, what is measured and what stays the same. Notes: plant two seed pots, one in light and one in the dark, with equal water and soil. Learners say the one changed factor, and draw both pots after a week. Grades 3 and 4.

This course: Grade 05 Science is at this level. Its units include Living things: animals and life styles; The human body: systems; Plants and reproduction; Changes in materials and more.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Measuring and recording

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Measuring and recording

Notes: Measure length, mass, time and temperature and record them in a table. Notes: a plant grows 2 cm in 4 days, which is 0.5 cm a day. Learners take three readings of 12, 14 and 13 mm, find the mean of 13, and say why repeats help. Grades 5 and 6.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Variables, graphs and density

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Variables, graphs and density

Notes: Apply a formula and draw a graph from a fair test. Notes: density equals mass over volume; a metal block of 54 g and 20 cm3 has density 2.7 g/cm3. Learners plot mass against volume for three blocks and read the gradient. Dry the benchtop before using water. Grades 7 and 8.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Evidence, anomalies and models

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Evidence, anomalies and models

Notes: Analyse data, spot an anomaly and judge how well a model fits the evidence. Notes: readings of 5.1, 5.3, 5.2 and 7.9 hold one anomaly at 7.9, so the mean of the rest is 5.2. Learners suggest a cause, repeat the trial and write a conclusion that cites the numbers. Grades 9 and 10.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Modelling and curve fitting

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Modelling and curve fitting

Notes: Apply least-squares fitting and error reasoning to link data to a model. Notes: the points (1,2.1), (2,3.9), (3,6.2), (4,7.8), (5,10.1) give slope 1.99 and intercept 0.05, with R squared 0.997. Learners explain what the intercept means and whether a straight line is right.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Study design and sample size

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Study design and sample size

Notes: Evaluate and create a study with a clear hypothesis, controls, sample size and ethics approval. Notes: to detect a 5-unit difference with spread 10 at 5 per cent significance and 80 per cent power needs about 63 per group. Learners write a pre-registered plan and list the risks and consent steps.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.
