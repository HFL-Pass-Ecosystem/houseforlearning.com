flagship course · GL-14

IT Service and Support

Run a friendly, organised help desk: take requests, find and fix common faults, write clear guides and measure how well users are served.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L3 Foundation54 h of lessons plus practiceAbout 8 to 12 weeks part-time

Run a friendly, organised help desk: take requests, find and fix common faults, write clear guides and measure how well users are served.

  • Understand Explain the support cycle: request, triage, fix, close, learn.
  • Apply Fix ten common faults on phones, computers and networks.
  • Analyse Analyse tickets to find repeated problems and their causes.
  • Create Create a help guide, a ticket form and a simple service promise.
  • Evaluate Evaluate service quality with response times and user ratings.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Run a practice help desk for 20 requests from real or simulated users and report on response times and user ratings.

Condition

A free ticket tool or spreadsheet, practice computers or phones, a list of common faults, a mentor with support experience and a peer circle. About 8 to 10 weeks part-time.

Standard

Every ticket logged with time stamps; first reply within the promised time for 90% of tickets; at least 7 of 10 faults fixed first time; guide tested by a non-technical user; average rating 4 of 5 or better.

Who it is for: Anyone starting in IT support, office staff, college learners, returners. Requirements: Basic computer skills. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Never ask users for passwords. Use remote tools only with permission. Back up before you change anything.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 The help desk cycle 4 hUnderstand

Good support follows a repeatable cycle. You will follow a seven-step support cycle and agree response targets, so every user knows what happens after they report a problem.

  • Request logged; priority set; assigned; fixed; closed; reviewed.
  • Priority depends on how many people and how much work is stopped.
  • Always tell the user what happens next and when.
  • Close only when the user agrees.
  • Learn the cycle of receive, record, classify, diagnose, fix or escalate, confirm with the user and close, and put it on one page by your desk.
  • Agree target times, such as an acknowledgement within 15 minutes and a first answer within four hours, so users know what to expect.
  • Keep a notebook of every new fault and the fix that worked.
  • Practise explaining a fault to someone who has never used a computer.
Worked example. One user cannot print (low) versus the whole office has no internet (high).

Activity. Draw the cycle and give two priority examples.

Standard to meet. Show cycle correct; examples sensible.

Common mistake. Jumping to fixes before understanding the fault.

Second worked example. A caller says 'the computer is broken'. Three questions show only the sound is muted after an update; the fix takes one minute and the ticket records the cause.

Check yourself. What three questions start every call?

Module 2 Listening and asking 6 hUnderstand

Most faults are solved by good questions. You will open each call with three diagnostic questions and repeat back key facts, which uncovers the real fault faster.

  • Ask: what happened, what did you expect, what changed lately.
  • Repeat back in your own words.
  • Use plain words, no jargon.
  • Stay calm and kind.
  • Open every call with three questions: what were you trying to do, what happened instead, and what changed recently, and write the answers word for word.
  • Use plain words, avoid jargon, and confirm by repeating the key facts back; ask the user to read any error message exactly as shown.
  • Learn the shortcuts for screenshots, which help ticket notes.
  • Set a standard greeting that is warm and short.
Worked example. User says "the computer is broken". Questions reveal that only the sound is off; fix in one minute.

Activity. Role-play three calls with a partner.

Standard to meet. Show partner rates clarity 4 of 5.

Common mistake. Using jargon with a worried user.

Second worked example. Priority: one user cannot print (low, 1 person, work continues) versus the whole floor offline (high, 20 people, work stopped).

Check yourself. Rewrite one technical sentence in plain words.

Module 3 Logging tickets 8 hApply

A good ticket lets anyone pick up the case. You will write tickets with exact error text, steps to repeat and a priority based on impact, so any colleague can continue the case.

  • Fields: who, when, what, steps already tried, priority.
  • One issue per ticket.
  • Time stamps for open, first reply and close.
  • Keep notes short and factual.
  • Record who, when, where, device, exact error text, steps to repeat, and what has been tried; keep the title short, such as 'Printer offline, floor 2'.
  • Set priority by impact and number of people affected, for example one person with a workaround is low, a whole team unable to work is high.
  • Check that every ticket has an owner and a next step.
  • Record the time you first reply to each user.
Worked example. Ticket: "Printer 2 offline since 09:10, tried restart, error E12, 4 users affected, priority medium."

Activity. Create a ticket form and log 10 practice tickets.

Standard to meet. Show that all tickets complete with times.

Common mistake. A ticket that says only "it's broken".

Second worked example. Ticket 0412: 'Laptop A12, no Wi-Fi since 09:10, other devices fine, restarted once, priority medium, owner the on-duty technician'. Another technician can continue without calling the user.

Check yourself. Is any of your tickets missing a time?

Module 4 Common faults: computers and phones 8 hApply

Learn the ten most frequent faults. You will work through an ordered checklist for slow, frozen or failing computers and phones, protecting user files before changing anything.

  • No power or screen: check cable, battery, display.
  • Slow: storage full, too many programs, updates.
  • No sound or camera: device choice and permissions.
  • Updates and backups; safe removal of unwanted apps.
  • Check power and cables first, then restart, then whether free storage has fallen under 10 percent, then recent updates or new software, then run a simple scan.
  • Before changing settings or wiping anything, ask whether the user has recent copies of important files, and note it on the ticket.
  • Practise saying 'I do not know yet, but I will find out by three o'clock'.
  • Learn how to reset a router safely and tell users what to expect.
Worked example. Storage 98% full: clear downloads, move photos to backup, free 20 GB; speed returns.

Activity. Fix five practice faults and note steps.

Standard to meet. Show five fixes documented.

Common mistake. Changing settings before backing up.

Second worked example. A phone shows 'cannot take photos' because storage is full. You move 300 photos to a backup, delete cached files, and the camera works; the ticket logs 4 GB freed.

Check yourself. What do you back up before a fix?

Module 5 Common faults: network and accounts 8 hAnalyse

Connection and sign-in problems cause many tickets. You will separate single-account faults from network-wide ones and reset sign-ins safely, resolving many of the most frequent tickets quickly.

  • Wi-Fi: forget and rejoin, restart router, check others.
  • Name lookup versus cable versus provider problem.
  • Password reset safely: verify identity first.
  • Account locked: check cause before unlocking.
  • Ask whether other devices or colleagues are affected, test with a wired connection or a phone hotspot, and check whether the problem is one account or the whole network.
  • For account problems verify identity first, check lock status and password age, and reset using a one-time method rather than reading passwords aloud.
  • Check that backups exist before any risky fix.
  • Create a list of the ten most common faults in your setting.
Worked example. Only one laptop cannot connect: others fine; forget network, rejoin; solved.

Activity. Fix five network or account scenarios.

Standard to meet. Show steps documented with reasoning.

Common mistake. Unlocking accounts without checking who asks.

Second worked example. A shop till cannot sign in to its account after a password change. You check the caps lock and keyboard language, reset through the verified owner, and the till is back in 10 minutes.

Check yourself. How do you verify identity?

Module 6 Writing guides 10 hCreate

A guide that works saves the next call. You will write numbered how-to guides and test them on a newcomer, so the next user can solve the same issue without calling.

  • Short steps with a picture each.
  • Test it on someone who is not technical.
  • Put the fix at the top, the cause below.
  • Keep a short list of frequent questions.
  • Write each guide with a title, who it is for, numbered steps of one action each, a screenshot where helpful and a line saying when to contact support.
  • Test the guide with someone who has never seen the task; every question they ask shows a step that needs rewriting.
  • Write guides in the same order every time: problem, steps, check.
  • Keep a list of trusted tools and never install unknown ones.
Worked example. Guide: "Join the office Wi-Fi in 5 steps"; three users follow it alone without calling.

Activity. Write and test one guide.

Standard to meet. Show that tester completes it without help.

Common mistake. Writing a guide without testing it.

Second worked example. A guide 'Reset your email password in 6 steps' is tested by two new staff. One stalls at step 4, so you add a screenshot of the button; the second test passes in 3 minutes.

Check yourself. Who tested your guide?

Module 7 Measure service 6 hEvaluate

What gets measured gets better. You will track volume, reply time, fix time and satisfaction, and use repeat causes to decide where a guide or training would help most.

  • First reply time; fix time; first-time fix rate; user rating.
  • Group tickets by cause to find repeats.
  • Report weekly in one table.
  • Set a service promise you can keep.
  • Track tickets opened and closed per week, average time to first reply, average time to fix, and a one-question satisfaction score out of five.
  • Review the top five repeat causes monthly, and ask whether a guide, a fix or user training would remove each one for good.
  • Review rating comments weekly and thank those who praised.
  • Hold a short monthly review of repeat problems.
Worked example. 20 tickets: 18 first-replied within 30 minutes = 90%; 14 fixed first time = 70%; average rating 4.3.

Activity. Compute the four measures for your tickets.

Standard to meet. Show all four calculated correctly.

Common mistake. Reporting only averages.

Second worked example. In one week 30 tickets arrive; 24 get a first reply inside 1 hour (80%), 21 are fixed first time (70%) and the average rating is 4.1. The weakest number is the reply time.

Check yourself. What did your lowest rating say?

Module 8 Improve and escalate 4 hCreate

Know your limits and pass on cases well. You will recognise when to escalate and hand over complete notes, then use monthly reviews to turn difficult cases into lasting improvements.

  • Escalate with full notes and what you tried.
  • Fix causes, not just symptoms.
  • Thank users and ask for feedback.
  • Plan 90 days of learning.
  • Escalate when a ticket passes its target time, needs access you do not have, or affects many people, and always hand over the notes and what has been tried.
  • Hold a 20-minute monthly review of the hardest tickets and agree one change, such as a new guide or a monitoring alert, for each.
  • Keep your own skills list and add one new fix a week.
  • Share one lesson with the peer circle each month.
Worked example. Three tickets about the same app: ask for an update rather than fix again.

Activity. Write a problem report and a 90-day plan.

Standard to meet. Report names cause, fix and owner.

Common mistake. Escalating without notes.

Second worked example. Five users report the same slow login each Monday. You record the pattern, pass it to the system owner with times and screenshots, and the cause, a morning backup job, is moved to the evening.

Check yourself. What goes in your escalation note?

case cards and glossary

Practise on cases; learn the words

Case 1. The whole floor is offline

Twenty people lose the network at 10:00.

Question: Priority and first steps?

Model answer: High priority; check shared equipment first, tell users, escalate to the provider with notes.

Case 2. The angry caller

A user shouts about a lost file.

Question: How do you respond?

Model answer: Stay calm, listen, repeat back, say what you will do and when, and check the recycle bin and backups.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Name the support cycle steps.

Model answer: Log, triage, assign, fix, close, review. (Covered in module 1: The help desk cycle. To pass that module: Cycle correct; examples sensible.)

Q2. What sets priority?

Model answer: How many people and how much work are stopped. (Covered in module 1: The help desk cycle. To pass that module: Cycle correct; examples sensible.)

Q3. Why one issue per ticket?

Model answer: It keeps tracking and closing clear. (Covered in module 3: Logging tickets. To pass that module: All tickets complete with times.)

Q4. Why never ask for a password?

Model answer: Support never needs it and asking trains users to give it away. (Covered in module 5: Common faults: network and accounts. To pass that module: Steps documented with reasoning.)

Q5. What is first-time fix rate?

Model answer: Tickets fixed at first attempt divided by all tickets. (Covered in module 7: Measure service. To pass that module: All four calculated correctly.)

Q6. How do you test a guide?

Model answer: Have a non-technical user follow it alone. (Covered in module 6: Writing guides. To pass that module: Tester completes it without help.)

Q7. What first for a slow computer?

Model answer: Check storage, startup programs and updates. (Covered in module 4: Common faults: computers and phones. To pass that module: Five fixes documented.)

Q8. What goes in a good escalation?

Model answer: Full notes and what has been tried. (Covered in module 8: Improve and escalate. To pass that module: Report names cause, fix and owner.)

Q9. Why group tickets by cause?

Model answer: To fix repeat problems at the source. (Covered in module 7: Measure service. To pass that module: All four calculated correctly.)

Q10. When can you close a ticket?

Model answer: When the user agrees it is fixed. (Covered in module 1: The help desk cycle. To pass that module: Cycle correct; examples sensible.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Check local consumer and data rules when you handle other people's devices and accounts. Check your local rules with an adviser before you act for real.

India (Bengaluru)

Currency: write INR next to every number. Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Personal data law to check: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Level frame: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework. Partner to practise with: an industrial-training institute, a polytechnic, a small-business cluster or a Bengaluru incubator.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Currency: write EUR next to every number. Language: Dutch, English. Personal data law to check: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Level frame: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a regional college (MBO), a maker space or a small firm in the region.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Currency: write AED next to every number. Language: Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Level frame: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework. Partner to practise with: a free-zone business centre, a training institute or a municipality-licensed operator.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Currency: write BRL next to every number. Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Personal data law to check: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Level frame: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies. Partner to practise with: a small-business support agency, a technical school or a local cooperative.

Canada (Toronto)

Currency: write CAD next to every number. Language: English, French. Personal data law to check: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Level frame: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance. Partner to practise with: a community college, a settlement agency or a Toronto business hub.

United States

Currency: write USD next to every number. Language: English, Spanish. Personal data law to check: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Level frame: state and community-college credit systems. Partner to practise with: a community college, a workforce board or a small-business development centre.

Singapore

Currency: write SGD next to every number. Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Personal data law to check: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Level frame: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system. Partner to practise with: a channel partner, a polytechnic or an approved training provider.

Australia

Currency: write AUD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Level frame: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels. Partner to practise with: a vocational college or registered training organisation, or a regional business chamber.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Currency: write HKD next to every number. Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Personal data law to check: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Level frame: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework. Partner to practise with: a college, training body or business chamber.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Currency: write GEL or ILS next to every number. Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Personal data law to check: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Level frame: the national qualifications framework of that country. Partner to practise with: a local college, chamber or training body.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Currency: write KYD next to every number. Language: English. Personal data law to check: the Data Protection Act. Level frame: the local education and training framework. Partner to practise with: a local training body or business chamber.

Mexico

Currency: write MXN next to every number. Language: Spanish, English. Personal data law to check: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Level frame: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels. Partner to practise with: a technical college or business chamber.

ladder L1 to L7

Slide by slide, with notes

Seven slides, one for each level. Step in at your level and move up; credits stack.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Basics of helping

On the slide: L1 Basic · Basics of helping

Notes: Describe what a help desk does and how a user should describe a problem. Notes: write a good request for a broken printer and a poor one, and compare. Standard: the good request has what, where, since when and what was tried.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Awareness

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Awareness

Notes: Name the main kinds of faults (account, device, network, software) and who should handle each. Notes: sort twenty sample requests into the four kinds. Standard: at least 17 of 20 sorted correctly.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Foundation

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Foundation

Notes: Take a request, ask the right questions, solve a simple fault and write the ticket. Notes: use sample requests such as a locked account or a slow laptop. Standard: each ticket has the problem, the fix, the time and the user's confirmation.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Practitioner

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Practitioner

Notes: Handle a queue of requests by priority, keep users informed and escalate with the right detail. Notes: run ten simulated requests with two urgent. Standard: urgent requests first, updates sent on time, escalations complete.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Specialist

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Specialist

Notes: Run a practice help desk for 20 requests from real or simulated users and report on response times and user ratings. Notes: set targets before you start, such as first reply in 30 minutes. Standard: report shows targets, results, the slowest ticket and a fix.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced: Advanced

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Advanced

Notes: Lead a help desk team with a knowledge base, a service target and a monthly review. Notes: add article templates and a repeat-ticket report. Standard: repeat tickets fall month on month, or the report says why not.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert: Super expert

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Super expert

Notes: Set the ticket-handling standard (priority, target times, hand-over) and teach the support leads who apply it. Notes: review first-response, resolution and repeat-ticket figures each year.
