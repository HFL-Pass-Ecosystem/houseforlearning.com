Education and Learning Services

Design and teach a short learning programme to a small group and measure what they learned. You learn to write checkable outcomes, find out what learners already know, plan active sessions, teach clearly, assess fairly and compare results before and after.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner8 modules · 120 h guidedPractice 70% · Share 20% · Study 10%

Design and teach a short learning programme to a small group and measure what they learned. You learn to write checkable outcomes, find out what learners already know, plan active sessions, teach clearly, assess fairly and compare results before and after.

  • Understand Explain how outcomes, activity and check fit.
  • Apply Teach a short programme.
  • Analyse Analyse learning gain.
  • Create Create session plans and checks.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Design, teach and assess a 4-session programme for a small group and report learning gain with a plan for the next version.

Condition

About 120 hours over 3 to 5 months. A small group of consenting learners, free tools, a mentor teacher and a peer circle.

Standard

Outcomes written; four session plans; assessment with rubric; learner feedback; learning-gain report with calculations; at least 70% on the final check.

Learning only. Get consent from every learner or guardian, protect learner data, follow child-protection rules if you teach minors, and check with your local authority for the rules that apply to you.

Requirements: none. Who it is for: learners 16 and over, workers changing field, small-business starters, teams. Currency is shown on every number.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples (120 h guided)

Module 1 Outcomes 10 hUnderstand

An outcome says what learners will be able to do at the end, and it is the starting point for everything else. If you cannot check an outcome, you cannot tell whether teaching worked. Good outcomes begin with an action verb.

  • Begin each outcome with an action verb such as calculate, list, compare or build, because these describe visible behaviour.
  • Write one outcome for each session so each session has a single clear aim.
  • Say how you will check each outcome, for example a short task or a question with a standard to reach.
  • Avoid vague words such as understand or know when you write the outcome, because you cannot see them.
  • A common mistake is to write what you will teach instead of what the learner will do.
Worked example. The outcome reads: calculate a 20% discount. Checking it, a learner finds that 20% of S$45 is S$9, so the new price is S$36, and the answer can be marked right or wrong.

Activity. Write four outcomes, one for each session of your programme, and next to each write the exact task that will check it.

Standard to meet. Each outcome starts with an action verb, each is paired with a checkable task, and a peer confirms they can tell when it has been met.

Module 2 Know your learners 10 hAnalyse

Learners arrive with different knowledge, languages and needs. A short pre-check shows where to start and what to skip. Without it, you may bore some learners and lose others.

  • Give a short pre-check of about five questions on the topic before teaching begins.
  • Mark the pre-check and record each learner score and the questions most often missed.
  • Note the language, reading level and any support needs that learners share with you.
  • Adjust your plan by spending more time on the weakest areas and less on what most already know.
  • A common mistake is to use the pre-check score to label a learner instead of to plan the teaching.
Worked example. Eight learners take a five-question pre-check on fractions, and only 4 get question 3 correct. That is 4 divided by 8, or 50%, so fractions get extra time in session one.

Activity. Run a pre-check with your learners, record each result in a table and write a short paragraph on how it changes your session plans.

Standard to meet. Your table shows results for every learner, you identify the most-missed question and you list at least two changes to your plans.

Module 3 Plan a session 16 hCreate

A session plan lays out what happens, when and why. Short, active sessions work better than long talks. A timed plan keeps you honest about what fits.

  • Use four parts in each session: a hook to get attention, a short teach, practice and a check.
  • Keep talking to about 10 minutes at a time and use the rest for learners to practise.
  • Give a time to each part and make sure the times add up to the length of the session.
  • List the materials and prepare them in advance so no time is lost.
  • A common mistake is to plan too much content, which leaves no time for practice or a check.
Worked example. A 50-minute session is planned as a 5-minute hook, 10 minutes of teaching, 25 minutes of practice and a 10-minute check. The parts add to 5 plus 10 plus 25 plus 10, which is 50 minutes, and half of the time is practice.

Activity. Write four session plans, each with a time for every part, the materials and the check task that matches the outcome.

Standard to meet. Every plan adds up to its stated length, has at least half the time for practice and each check task matches its outcome.

Module 4 Teaching skills 16 hApply

Teaching clearly takes plain words, checking and patience. You speak simply, ask questions and wait for answers. Practising with a peer helps you improve before you teach the group.

  • Use short, plain sentences and explain any new word the first time you use it.
  • Check understanding by asking learners to explain the idea back in their own words.
  • Wait at least three seconds after asking a question before you speak, so learners have time to think.
  • Praise effort and give one specific tip for improvement to each learner.
  • A common mistake is to ask do you understand and accept silence as yes.
Worked example. You explain a method and ask two of the 8 learners to explain it back. One gets a step wrong, which shows that 1 of 2 checked learners needs it re-taught before practice starts.

Activity. Teach one full session to a peer or the group while a mentor observes, and complete a short self-review of what went well and what to change.

Standard to meet. The observer confirms you used plain words, checked understanding at least twice and gave learners time to answer.

Module 5 Assessment 16 hCreate

An assessment shows whether the outcome was met. A fair assessment has a clear task, a condition and a standard. A rubric makes the marking fair and the feedback useful.

  • State the task, the conditions such as time and tools, and the standard such as 70% correct.
  • Write a rubric with clear descriptions for each mark band so two markers would give the same result.
  • Make the assessment match the outcome, so if the outcome says calculate, the task asks for a calculation.
  • Give written feedback within 48 hours so learners can use it.
  • A common mistake is to assess something you did not teach.
Worked example. An assessment has 20 marks and a pass standard of 70%. The pass mark is 70% of 20, which is 14 marks, so a learner who scores 13 is one mark short and receives feedback on exactly what to improve.

Activity. Build an assessment with a task, a rubric, a marking sheet and a feedback template, then have a peer mark one sample answer to test it.

Standard to meet. Your peer and you give marks within 2 marks of each other on the same sample and the rubric covers every mark band.

Module 6 Teach the programme 30 hApply

Now you teach all four sessions and keep notes. You adjust as you go, based on what you see. Learner voice helps you improve each session.

  • Take attendance at every session and write short notes on what worked and what did not straight after teaching.
  • Adjust the next session when you see that learners struggled, for example by adding an example or more practice time.
  • Ask learners for one thing that helped and one thing to change at the end of each session.
  • Keep learner data private and store names and marks safely, following the consent you were given.
  • A common mistake is to keep to the plan when the group is clearly lost.
Worked example. Over four sessions with 8 learners there are 32 possible attendances. Attendance is 29, so it is 29 divided by 32, which is about 90.6%, and the three absences are followed up with a catch-up task.

Activity. Teach all four sessions and submit the attendance table, your session notes and learner comments, with at least one documented adjustment.

Standard to meet. Attendance is recorded for every session, at least one adjustment is linked to a specific observation and learner data is stored securely.

Module 7 Learning gain 12 hAnalyse

Learning gain compares the results before and after teaching. It shows whether the programme made a difference. You also look at who did not gain, because they show what to fix.

  • Use the same or a very similar test before and after, so the comparison is fair.
  • Calculate the average score before and the average score after for the group.
  • Calculate gain as the after average minus the before average, and also look at each learner gain.
  • Look closely at learners with little or no gain and look for reasons such as absence or unclear teaching.
  • A common mistake is to report only the average, which hides learners who did not improve.
Worked example. The group average rises from 45 to 72 out of 100, so the gain is 72 minus 45, which is 27 points. The largest possible gain was 100 minus 45, which is 55, so the group achieved 27 divided by 55, or about 49%, of what was possible.

Activity. Calculate the before and after averages, the gain for the group and for each learner, and write a short report with one chart.

Standard to meet. Your calculations are correct, a table shows each learner, and you name at least one possible reason for any learner with a gain below 10 points.

Module 8 Improve 10 hEvaluate

Improving means using your evidence to make the next version better. You pick a few changes, test them and share what you learned. Learners and peers can help.

  • Study your gain table and your feedback to find the biggest weakness.
  • Choose up to three changes, such as a practice quiz, an extra example or a different order of topics.
  • For each change say what result you expect and how you will measure it.
  • Share your sample session plan and results with peers so they can learn from your work.
  • A common mistake is to change everything at once, because then you cannot tell what helped.
Worked example. Question 4 on the after test was missed by 6 of 8 learners, which is 6 divided by 8, or 75%. You choose to add a worked example and a practice quiz on that topic and aim for 75% of learners to get it right next time.

Activity. Present a plan for version two with three changes, expected results, measures and dates.

Standard to meet. Each change is linked to evidence from your results and has a measure and a date.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why should outcomes start with an action verb?

Model answer: Action verbs describe something visible, so you can tell whether the outcome was reached. (Covered in module 1: Outcomes. To pass that module: Each outcome starts with an action verb, each is paired with a checkable task, and a peer confirms they can tell when it has been met.)

Q2. Why give a pre-check before teaching?

Model answer: It shows what learners already know so you start at the right level. (Covered in module 2: Know your learners. To pass that module: Your table shows results for every learner, you identify the most-missed question and you list at least two changes to your plans.)

Q3. Why should most of a session be practice?

Model answer: People learn by doing, so practice is where the learning is made stronger. (Covered in module 3: Plan a session. To pass that module: Every plan adds up to its stated length, has at least half the time for practice and each check task matches its outcome.)

Q4. How can you check that learners understand?

Model answer: Ask them to explain it back or show it, instead of asking if they understand. (Covered in module 4: Teaching skills. To pass that module: The observer confirms you used plain words, checked understanding at least twice and gave learners time to answer.)

Q5. Why use a rubric when marking?

Model answer: It makes marking fair and clear, and it lets learners see what to improve. (Covered in module 5: Assessment. To pass that module: Your peer and you give marks within 2 marks of each other on the same sample and the rubric covers every mark band.)

Q6. Why adjust a plan while teaching?

Model answer: Because learners differ, and changing the plan to fit them improves their learning. (Covered in module 6: Teach the programme. To pass that module: Attendance is recorded for every session, at least one adjustment is linked to a specific observation and learner data is stored securely.)

Q7. What is the gain when the average goes from 45 to 72?

Model answer: 72 minus 45 is a gain of 27 points. (Covered in module 7: Learning gain. To pass that module: Your calculations are correct, a table shows each learner, and you name at least one possible reason for any learner with a gain below 10 points.)

Q8. What should drive the changes you make to a programme?

Model answer: The results and feedback from learners, not your guesses. (Covered in module 8: Improve. To pass that module: Each change is linked to evidence from your results and has a measure and a date.)

Q9. What is the task of this course, in one sentence?

Model answer: Design, teach and assess a 4-session programme for a small group and report learning gain with a plan for the next version. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

Q10. What standard must your final evidence meet?

Model answer: Outcomes written; four session plans; assessment with rubric; learner feedback; learning-gain report with calculations; at least 70% on the final check. (Covered in: Task, condition and standard, near the top of this page.)

ten locations and global

Your place

Local custom changes rules, language, currency and who you practise with. Check each rule with your local authority before you act.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the national S-system training bodies.

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: the Singapore national skills frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Language: English. Currency: KYD. Data rule for what you publish: the Data Protection Act. Skills frame to cite: the local education and training framework.

Mexico

Language: Spanish, English. Currency: MXN. Data rule for what you publish: the federal law on personal data held by private parties. Skills frame to cite: the national competency certification system and the education ministry levels.

Russian Federation

Russian Federation: HELD. A sanctions and licensing check by our lawyer must finish before any course is offered there. No localised version is published.
