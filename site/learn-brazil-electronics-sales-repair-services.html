Task, condition and standard

Task

Plan, start and run electronics sales & repair services in São Paulo: serve a household or small firm, deliver sold and repaired electronic goods, keep records, and show a working month of numbers.

Condition

About 8 weeks part-time. Own phone or computer, real customers or a real practice site, a peer and a mentor for the final review. Local rules and licences checked against official sources.

Standard

Every module standard is met; all claims are true and backed by records; peers and a mentor mark the evidence 'Meets' or better.

Who it is for: students, graduates, career changers, owners, workers and returners. Requirements: none. Materials: lesson text, templates, tracker, rubric, check with answers.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Know your customer and market 4 hAnalyse

Every sound business in electronics sales & repair services begins with one clear customer and one clear problem. In São Paulo and the wider state, demand is real but crowded, so a small, well-described customer group wins before a broad one.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Customers want honest diagnosis and fair price; give a written estimate.
  • Write the customer in one sentence: a household or small firm. Add the place, the moment of need and what they use today.
  • List five rivals or substitutes within reach. For each, record price, strength, weakness and how people reach it.
  • Interview at least eight real customers with open questions. Record exact words, not your summary.
  • Count the market in units: how many repairs could one area buy in a month? Use two public sources and your own count, then take the lower figure.
  • Choose one first segment and say why the others wait.
Worked example. Weak: 'Everyone needs sold and repaired electronic goods.' Strong: 'Our first customer is a household or small firm within 8 km of the centre, who today pays R$ 198 per repair and complains about delay. We target 120 repairs a month by month three.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: ten customers are interviewed for electronics sales & repair services. Seven say price, two say speed, one says trust. Question: which first message do you write, and which interview record backs it? Model answer: lead with the repair price and speed promise, keep trust proof as the second line, and cite the seven price answers by date.

Activity. Write the one-sentence customer, a five-row rival table and eight interview records. Share them with a peer for a challenge round.

Standard to meet. Customer is specific; rival table has prices; every claim traces to an interview or a source.

Module 2 Rules, registration and data duties in Brazil 4 hUnderstand

Brazil asks every trader to be visible and accountable. Know which register, tax regime and sector rules apply before the first sale, and check each against the current official text.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Register the business; consumer law gives warranty rights; handle e-waste properly.
  • Choose a legal form: micro-entrepreneur (MEI) only if your activity appears on the official MEI list, or a company registered with a CNPJ. Regulated activities need their sector authorisation first. Check the current limits and lists on the official portal.
  • Learn the tax regime that fits your size and write down which taxes you will collect and pay, and when.
  • Find the sector rules for electronics sales & repair services: licences, inspections, professional registers. List each with the office that issues it and the renewal date.
  • Personal data of customers falls under Brazil's data protection law (LGPD). Collect only what you need, say why, keep it safe, and name who answers questions.
  • Consumer law gives buyers clear rights on information, returns and complaints. Put your terms in plain Portuguese.
Worked example. Checklist for a new electronics sales & repair services trader: 1) legal form (CNPJ, or MEI only if your activity is on the official list); 2) city operating licence; 3) sector licence or register; 4) tax set-up; 5) privacy notice; 6) written terms. Each has an owner and a date.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a neighbour says registration can wait until the first R$ 900 of sales. Question: what do you do? Model answer: do not delay; write the compliance table first, confirm the register, tax and sector licence with the official office, and start only where the table shows no open blocker.

Activity. Build your own six-line compliance table for your first location. Mark what is done, what is pending and who confirms it.

Standard to meet. Every line cites an official source; nothing is assumed; pending items have dates. Local law always wins over this course.

Module 3 Design the offer 4 hCreate

An offer states what the customer gets, how well, by when and at what price. For sold and repaired electronic goods, the offer must make the quality visible and the next step easy.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Offer diagnosis fee, repair price and warranty.
  • Describe the core offer in three lines: what is delivered (sold and repaired electronic goods), the standard it meets, and the time it takes.
  • Add one proof for each promise: a sample, a test result, a guarantee or a customer record.
  • Price from cost upward and from customer value downward, then pick a price inside both.
  • Write a simple one-page terms sheet: what is included, what is extra, how to cancel.
  • Create two options at most, so choice stays easy.
Worked example. Offer: 'Sold and repaired electronic goods, delivered within agreed time, checked against a written standard, price R$ 180 per repair. Proof: a dated record the customer can check and sign. Changes or cancellation by written agreement before work begins.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a customer asks for a discount from R$ 180 to R$ 162 per repair. Question: decide with numbers. Model answer: contribution falls from R$ 110 to R$ 92 per repair; offer an extra of value (for example faster service) before cutting price, and accept only if the lower contribution still clears break-even.

Longer notes. List the devices you will fix, such as phones and notebooks, and the five faults you see most. For each fault, record the part price from two suppliers, the minutes of bench time and how often the repair fails. Decide whether the diagnosis is free or charged, and say so before the customer leaves the device. Set a warranty in days and write what it covers, for example the part but not water damage. Keep a small stock of fast-moving cases and cables. Print a receipt with the device condition, fault and agreed price. Check the current official text for warranty, consumer and waste disposal rules.

Extra worked example. A shop charges R$ 180 per repair. Parts, labor time and packaging cost R$ 70, leaving R$ 110, or 61,1 percent of the price. Fixed costs of R$ 8.000 a month for the counter, bench tools, the clerk and software are covered after 73 repairs, since R$ 8.000 divided by R$ 110 is 72,7. With 200 repairs, sales are R$ 36.000, contribution is R$ 22.000 and profit is R$ 14.000. The owner updates part costs with each supplier invoice.

Activity. Write the offer card, the proof list and the terms sheet. Test them with three customers and note every point where they hesitated.

Standard to meet. Promise, proof and price are consistent; terms fit one page; three customer reactions are recorded.

Module 4 Run the work step by step 5 hApply

Good operations make results repeatable. For electronics sales & repair services, write each step so a new person could follow it on their first day, and measure each step.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Check-in, diagnose, approve, repair, test, return.
  • Map the process from first contact to paid and closed. Typical stages: enquiry, quote, preparation, delivery of the repair, check, payment, follow-up.
  • Name the key resource at each stage: an anti-static bench and a job tag. Note what happens if it is missing.
  • Write a one-page standard procedure per stage: who, what, tool, time, record.
  • Choose three measures: speed, quality (errors per 100) and customer rating. Review them every week.
  • Fix the cause of a defect, not the symptom. Ask 'why' five times and record the answer.
Worked example. Stage record: 'Prepare. Owner: lead. Time: 30 min. Tool: checklist. Record: signed sheet. Defect found on 1 of 50 repairs last week; cause: missing check on an anti-static bench and a job tag; fix: add check at start, review next Friday.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: of 200 repairs last month, 12 had a defect traced to an anti-static bench and a job tag. Question: what is the defect rate and what is your first action? Model answer: about 6 defects per 100; stop the cause (add a check at the start), correct affected customers, log the cause, and re-measure next week.

Activity. Draw the process map and write procedures for the two riskiest stages. Run each once and time it.

Standard to meet. Procedures are followable by a stranger; measures have weekly numbers; one defect is traced to a root cause.

Module 5 Safety, quality and risk 4 hEvaluate

The most serious risk in this field is an electric shock or a lost customer device. A calm, written risk routine protects people first and the business second.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Static and battery fire risk; ESD mat and safe battery storage.
  • List ten things that could go wrong. Score each for how likely (1 to 5) and how serious (1 to 5). Multiply and sort.
  • For the top five, choose a control: remove the hazard, replace it, guard it, train people, or use protective gear, in that order of preference.
  • Write who does what in an emergency, and rehearse it once a quarter.
  • Keep an incident and near-miss log. Near-misses are free lessons.
  • Check that insurance and legal duties match the risk you actually carry.
Worked example. Risk row: 'Hazard: an electric shock or a lost customer device. Likelihood 3, severity 5, score 15. Control: written check before each repair, trained staff, log sheet. Owner: site lead. Review monthly.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a near-miss occurs: an electric shock or a lost customer device nearly happened but no one was hurt. Question: what do you do in the next 24 hours? Model answer: make safe, record the event, ask the five whys, add or tighten one control, brief the team, and set a review date; near-misses count as lessons, not blame.

Activity. Build a ten-row risk register and a one-page emergency plan. Run a ten-minute drill with a peer and note two improvements.

Standard to meet. Top risks have controls ranked by preference; drill done; log started. Where a regulation applies, follow the official rule.

Module 6 People, skills and service 4 hApply

People deliver the promise. Define roles, train to a standard and give feedback in the open.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Train technicians on tools and data privacy.
  • Write a role card for each person: purpose, five tasks, the standard for each, and who to ask for help.
  • Train by showing, doing with support, then doing alone with a check. Record sign-off per task.
  • Set service rules for answering customers: reply times, tone, how to apologise and fix.
  • Hold a ten-minute team check each day: yesterday's problem, today's plan, one risk.
  • Build a growth path: what a new person can do in week 1, month 3 and month 12.
Worked example. Training record: 'Task: handle a complaint. Shown 2 May; done with support 4 May; done alone 8 May, checked: apology, fix, follow-up call. Signed by lead.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: a new hire completes training but a customer complaint shows a skipped step. Question: whose gap is it? Model answer: first the system: check the role card, the training sign-off and the checklist; then coach the person and fix the document so the step cannot be skipped.

Longer notes. At the counter, train the receptionist to note the fault, passwords given or not, scratches, accessories left and the promised date, then to read the sheet back to the customer. Give technicians a bench checklist: open, photograph, label screws, replace, test sound, touch, charge and camera, then close. Tag every part with the job number. Call the customer when the device is ready and again if a new fault appears. Review each return under warranty to find out which step failed. Lock customer files and never browse them. Check the current official text for data protection and workshop rules.

Extra worked example. A shop completes 200 repairs a month and 16 return under warranty, each costing R$ 70 in new parts and time. Returns waste R$ 1.120. After a final test checklist and photos at intake, returns fall to 6. The shop saves R$ 700 a month, or R$ 8.400 in a year. The owner confirms the figure by counting warranty tickets in the receipt book at month end.

Activity. Write two role cards and a training plan for one new hire. Role-play a hard customer conversation twice and review.

Standard to meet. Role cards have measurable standards; training shows sign-off steps; service rules are written in plain words.

Module 7 Money: cost, price and break-even 5 hAnalyse

A business lives or dies on a few numbers. Know your contribution per unit, your fixed costs and the volume you need.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Parts cost and turnaround time; track return rate.
  • Contribution per repair = price minus variable cost. Fixed costs are paid whether you sell or not.
  • Break-even units = fixed cost divided by contribution. Plan to pass it by a safe margin.
  • Make a 12-month cash plan. Cash timing matters as much as profit: customers may pay late, suppliers may want money first.
  • Keep money for business and money for the owner apart.
  • Test the plan: what if sales fall by a quarter, or cost rises by a tenth?
Worked example. Worked example, all figures in reais: price R$ 180, variable cost R$ 70, contribution R$ 110 per repair. Fixed cost R$ 8.000 a month. Break-even = R$ 8.000 / R$ 110 = 73 repairs a month. At 200 repairs a month, profit = R$ 110 x 200 - R$ 8.000 = R$ 14.000. These are teaching figures that belong to this example only; later examples and the case study use their own figures. Use your own.
Second worked scenario. Scenario: sales fall to 150 repairs a month. Question: what is the monthly result? Model answer: R$ 110 x 150 - R$ 8.000 = R$ 8.500; compare with break-even of 73 repairs and decide whether to cut fixed cost or raise volume first. Teaching figures only.

Activity. Build your own table: price, variable cost, contribution, fixed cost, break-even and a downside case at 75% of sales. Show it to a peer or mentor.

Standard to meet. Arithmetic is correct; every figure has a source or is marked as an assumption; a downside case is shown.

Module 8 Grow, measure and renew 4 hEvaluate

Growth should follow proof. Measure, learn, then add; and keep returning to the customer.

  • In electronics sales & repair services: Grow with trade-ins and refurbished sales.
  • Pick one growth lever at a time: new customers, a higher repeat rate, a better price, or a new offer.
  • Set one measurable target for 90 days, such as 'raise repeat customers from 20% to 30%'.
  • Ask each customer how they found you, and record it. Spend more where the record shows results.
  • Review monthly: numbers, defects, complaints, cash. Write three decisions each time.
  • Plan the renewal of licences, equipment and skills a year ahead.
Worked example. 90-day plan: 'Lever: repeat customers. Action: follow-up call after every repair. Target: repeat rate from 20% to 30%. Check: weekly count. Review date: day 45 and day 90.'
Second worked scenario. Scenario: you can add 40 repairs a month by one of two levers: a follow-up call to past customers or a price rise of R$ 18 to R$ 198. Question: how do you choose? Model answer: test the cheaper reversible lever first (the call), measure for 30 days, and only then try the price change, so you can tell which one worked.

Longer notes. Save every customer's device model and repair date, then send a message six months later offering a free check. Offer a case and a charger together with each repair, and track how many accept. Count repairs per week, average ticket, returns and days spent waiting for parts. Keep only the parts that sell often and order the rest on demand. Compare suppliers on price and failure rate, not price alone. Rank accessories by margin per shelf space and move the winners to the counter. Ask satisfied customers for a review. Check the current official text for warranty, imports and tax rules.

Extra worked example. A shop does 200 repairs a month at R$ 180, with R$ 70 of variable cost and R$ 8.000 of fixed cost, so profit is R$ 14.000. Offering a case or charger with each repair and a follow-up message brings 20 more repairs from referrals, reaching 220. Sales rise to R$ 39.600 and profit to R$ 16.200, a gain of R$ 2.200 a month. Fixed costs stay unchanged. Over twelve months the gain is R$ 26.400. The owner checks the receipt book.

Activity. Write a 90-day plan with one target, three actions and a review date. Present it in five minutes and take questions.

Standard to meet. Target is measurable and dated; actions are small enough to start this week; review date is in the calendar.

case study

A case to work through

Situation. Ricardo runs a small phone repair counter in a market area of central Sao Paulo. His average repair is R$ 180, and parts, labor time and packaging cost him about R$ 70. His fixed costs are R$ 8.000 a month for the counter, bench tools, a clerk and software. He does about 200 repairs a month. Last month 16 customers came back within the warranty period with the new screen showing lines or dead touch. He redid each of them at his own cost, with another part and more time. He buys the parts from two suppliers and does not note which part came from which supplier. He also sometimes forgets to test the speaker and camera before returning the phone, and one customer said the camera was already broken before the repair. He wants to reduce returns and settle disputes fairly.

Task. Say what you would do first, next and last, and what record you would keep.

Model answer. First, find the margin: R$ 180 minus R$ 70 leaves R$ 110 per repair. Next, break-even: R$ 8.000 divided by R$ 110 is 72,7, so he needs 73 repairs a month. At 200, sales are R$ 36.000, contribution is R$ 22.000 and profit is R$ 14.000. Each redo costs another R$ 70, so 16 redos cost R$ 1.120 and profit falls to R$ 12.880. The return rate is 8 percent. Then act. Fill a form when the phone arrives, with model, visible damage, a photo and a checklist of screen, camera, speaker and charging, signed by the customer. Test the same list before delivery. Mark each part with supplier and purchase date, and count returns by supplier. State the warranty terms in plain words on the receipt; check the current official text of the consumer rules. Standard to meet: every phone has an arrival record, a final test and a traceable part, and returns stay below 5 percent.

levels L1 to L7

Where this sits on the ladder

Seven levels, one slide each. Read the slide, then read the speaker notes under it. Credits stack and you can step in and out; this course is L4.

Slide 1 of 7: L1 Basic: electronics sales and repair

  • Name common device parts and basic tools
  • List safety steps for batteries and static
  • Recognise simple faults such as dead battery or cracked screen
Notes for slide 1

Beginners open a dead phone on a mat and name battery, board, screen and connector, then learn why batteries swell and how static damages chips. They spot simple faults such as a cracked glass or a drained cell. Six to ten hours with a parts quiz; pass is correct names and safe handling.

Slide 2 of 7: L2 Awareness: electronics sales and repair

  • Describe how a repair job moves from intake to return
  • Explain warranty, spare parts and used-device grading
  • Identify sales and repair demand in Sao Paulo
Notes for slide 2

Ten to fifteen hours follow a repair from intake tag to test to return, explain warranty, spare parts and used-device grading, and look at sales and repair demand in Sao Paulo, from shopping-mall kiosks to neighbourhood benches. A written job-flow summary is the evidence. Tidy tags prevent mix-ups at the counter.

Slide 3 of 7: L3 Foundation: electronics sales and repair

  • Apply testing steps to find a simple fault
  • Demonstrate a safe screen or battery swap on a practice device
  • Calculate a repair quote from parts, time and margin
Notes for slide 3

Supervised work, 20 to 30 hours: test a device with a multimeter to find a simple fault, swap a screen or battery on a practice phone without damage, and write a quote from parts, minutes and margin. Portfolio: photos, a test log and a costed quote.

Slide 4 of 7: L4 Practitioner: electronics sales and repair

  • Plan an electronics sales or repair shop with services, stock and customers
  • Start it by setting up a safe bench and checking the current official text for business, waste and consumer rules
  • Run intake logs, parts stock, quotes and payments each week
Notes for slide 4

The core plan sets out services, parts stock and customer flow, with a check of the current official text on bench safety, waste and consumer rules. Sixty to eighty hours across 8 modules end in a launch checklist and a month of intake logs, parts counts, quotes and receipts.

Slide 5 of 7: L5 Specialist: electronics sales and repair

  • Analyse repeat repairs and parts returns to cut waste
  • Improve diagnostic speed and supplier quality
  • Handle data privacy and warranty disputes correctly
Notes for slide 5

Repeat repairs and returned parts are charted by model and supplier. The specialist speeds diagnosis with a fixed test order, drops weak suppliers and handles customer data and warranty disputes with signed forms. Forty to sixty hours; the case study shows returns falling. Evidence is a month of tickets.

Slide 6 of 7: L6 Advanced: electronics sales and repair

  • Evaluate new device lines and training needs
  • Design bench standards, tracking and pricing rules
  • Manage risk from fake parts, stock value and fire safety
Notes for slide 6

Managers evaluate new device lines and technician training, write bench standards, tracking rules and pricing, and assess risks from fake parts, locked-up stock value and fire. Sixty to eighty hours of work, followed by a review of the effect on returns and margin. Every purchase gets a reason.

Slide 7 of 7: L7 Super expert: electronics sales and repair

  • Create repair quality and safety standards
  • Mentor technicians and shop owners
  • Lead recycling and training projects and share results
Notes for slide 7

Experts set repair quality and safety standards, coach technicians and owners, and lead recycling and training projects. Over 100 hours of mentoring, writing and community work, judged by peers. Waste and battery rules change, so every item is rechecked against current official text. Safe benches are the test.

Your place

Local rules change licences, taxes, data duties and customer rights. This course is written for São Paulo. Check the current official text for your own place.

India (Bengaluru)

Language: English, Hindi, Kannada. Currency: INR. Registration: Udyam (small business) or company registration with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Data rule for what you publish: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Skills frame to cite: National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF) and the national credit framework.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Language: Dutch, English. Currency: EUR. Registration: registration with the Chamber of Commerce (KvK). Data rule for what you publish: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens. Skills frame to cite: the Dutch qualification framework (NLQF) linked to the European Qualifications Framework.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Language: Arabic, English. Currency: AED. Registration: a trade licence from the emirate economic department or a free-zone licence. Data rule for what you publish: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection). Skills frame to cite: the emirate education and training regulators (for example KHDA in Dubai) and the national qualifications framework.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Language: Portuguese (Spanish and English support). Currency: BRL. Registration: CNPJ registration, or MEI for micro-entrepreneurs. Data rule for what you publish: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados). Skills frame to cite: the national technical-education catalogue and the S-system training bodies (SENAI, SENAC).

Canada (Toronto)

Language: English, French. Currency: CAD. Registration: federal or Ontario business registration. Data rule for what you publish: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply. Skills frame to cite: the provincial colleges framework and the national credential-recognition guidance.

United States

Language: English, Spanish. Currency: USD. Registration: state registration (Secretary of State) and a federal tax ID. Data rule for what you publish: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13. Skills frame to cite: state and community-college credit systems.

Singapore

Language: English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil. Currency: SGD. Registration: registration with ACRA. Data rule for what you publish: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA). Skills frame to cite: SkillsFuture Singapore frameworks and the national credit system.

Australia

Language: English. Currency: AUD. Registration: an ABN and, for companies, ASIC registration. Data rule for what you publish: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles. Skills frame to cite: the Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) levels.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Language: Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English. Currency: HKD. Registration: a business registration and, for companies, the Companies Registry. Data rule for what you publish: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance. Skills frame to cite: the Hong Kong Qualifications Framework.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Language: Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English. Currency: GEL or ILS. Registration: local business registration in the country where you work. Data rule for what you publish: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives. Skills frame to cite: the national qualifications framework of that country.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)
