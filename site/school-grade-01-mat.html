Grade 01: Mathematics

9 units across the school year. Each has a worked example, an activity and a standard to meet.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L1Primary9 units36 teaching weeks36-week school yearBloom levels: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse
  • Remember Count, read, write and order numbers to 20.
  • Remember Count in ones and tens, read and write numbers to 100, tens and ones.
  • Apply Add using objects, number lines and number bonds to 10.
  • Apply Subtract using objects and number lines, and link adding with taking away.
  • Remember Name and sort flat and solid shapes by their features.
  • Analyse Compare and order objects by length and weight using everyday words.
  • Understand Tell time to the hour and half hour and order the days of the week.
  • Remember Name coins and notes and make small amounts.
  • Apply Continue and make patterns and sort by one rule.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Meet the outcome of every unit in Grade 01 Mathematics, and keep one piece of evidence for each.

Condition

A 36-week school year, 9 units, a teacher or guide, a learning book or device, and local examples in the local language first with English support.

Standard

Each unit: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school. Course pass: no unit at 'Not yet' after one retry, and a pass in SA1 and SA2.

Assessment plan

Formative checks FA1 to FA4 around weeks 6, 12, 24 and 30; summative checks SA1 in week 18 and SA2 in week 36.

Unit 1 Numbers to 20 Weeks 1 to 4Remember

In this unit you work on 'Numbers to 20', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: count, read, write and order numbers to 20. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Numbers to 20' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: count, read, write and order numbers to 20. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: Show 47 as 4 tens and 7 ones. Compare 47 and 74: 7 tens is more than 4 tens, so 74 is greater. Count on in tens from 47: 57, 67, 77. Method: Model run for 'Numbers to 20'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Count a group: draw 14 stars in two rows, 10 in the top row and 4 in the bottom. Touch each star once and say 1, 2, 3 up to 14, moving each touched star aside so none is counted twice.
  2. Step 2, Write the number: 14 is one ten and four ones, so write 1 then 4. Say it aloud as fourteen. Compare with 41, which has the digits swapped but means four tens and one one, a much bigger number.
  3. Step 3, Order numbers: put 9, 17, 12 and 5 in order from smallest to biggest. Look at each on a number line from 0 to 20. The order is 5, 9, 12, 17, because each one sits further right.
  4. Step 4, Find the missing number: in the pattern 11, 12, __, 14, 15 count on from 12. The next count word is thirteen, so the gap is 13. Check by counting the whole row again from 11 to 15.
  5. Step 5, Compare two numbers: which is more, 16 or 19? Count to 16 first, then keep going 17, 18, 19. Since 19 comes later in the count, 19 is more than 16, and 16 is less than 19.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: draw 18 dots and write the number. Then put 7, 20, 13 and 2 in order from smallest to biggest. Answer: 18 is one ten and eight ones, and the order is 2, 7, 13, 20.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Count, read, write and order numbers to 20: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 2 Numbers to 100 Weeks 5 to 9Remember

In this unit you work on 'Numbers to 100', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: count in ones and tens, read and write numbers to 100, tens and ones. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Numbers to 100' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: count in ones and tens, read and write numbers to 100, tens and ones. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: Show 47 as 4 tens and 7 ones. Compare 47 and 74: 7 tens is more than 4 tens, so 74 is greater. Count on in tens from 47: 57, 67, 77. Method: Model run for 'Numbers to 100'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Count in tens: touch ten-sticks one at a time and say 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. Five sticks of ten make 50. Each jump adds one whole ten, so the ones digit stays 0 every time.
  2. Step 2, Tens and ones: show 3 ten-sticks and 7 loose cubes. Three tens are 30, and 7 more makes 37. Write 3 in the tens place and 7 in the ones place, and say thirty-seven.
  3. Step 3, Read a number: 64 has a 6 in the tens place and a 4 in the ones place. So it is 6 tens and 4 ones, which is 60 and 4. Draw 6 sticks and 4 cubes to prove it.
  4. Step 4, Count on from a number: start at 47 and count on ones: 48, 49, 50. After 49 the ones run out, so we make a new ten and the number becomes 50. Then 51, 52 follow.
  5. Step 5, Check on a hundred square: find 58 in the row that runs from 51 to 60. One step right gives 59, and one step down gives 68, which is ten more. This confirms that moving down a row adds a ten.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: show 52 with sticks and cubes, then say how many tens and ones. Count on from 96 to 100. Answer: 52 is 5 tens and 2 ones, and the count is 97, 98, 99, 100.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Count in ones and tens, read and write numbers to 100, tens and ones: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 3 Adding within 20 Weeks 10 to 14Apply

In this unit you work on 'Adding within 20', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: add using objects, number lines and number bonds to 10. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Adding within 20' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: add using objects, number lines and number bonds to 10. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: 47 + 28. Tens: 40 + 20 = 60. Ones: 7 + 8 = 15. Add: 60 + 15 = 75. Check by taking away: 75 - 28 = 47. Method: Model run for 'Adding within 20'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Add with objects: 6 red counters and 5 blue counters. Push them together and count all: 6, then 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. So 6 + 5 = 11. Writing it as a sentence helps: six and five make eleven.
  2. Step 2, Count on with a number line: for 8 + 4 start on 8 and hop 4 times: 9, 10, 11, 12. Land on 12, so 8 + 4 = 12. Keep the bigger number in your head and count only the small one.
  3. Step 3, Use number bonds to 10: 7 + 5. Split 5 into 3 and 2. Since 7 + 3 = 10, add the remaining 2 to get 12. So 7 + 5 = 12. Bonds to 10 make big sums easy.
  4. Step 4, Check with doubles: 6 + 6 = 12 is a double. Then 6 + 7 is one more than the double, which is 13. A different route lets you confirm the earlier answers are sensible.
  5. Step 5, Word problem: Amal has 9 marbles and wins 6 more. 9 + 6: make 10 with 1 from the 6, leaving 5, so 10 + 5 = 15. Write the answer with words: Amal has 15 marbles.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: find 8 + 7 using a bond to 10, and 9 + 3 on a number line. Answer: 8 + 2 = 10, then 5 more gives 15; and 9 hop 3 lands on 12.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Add using objects, number lines and number bonds to 10: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 4 Taking away within 20 Weeks 15 to 19Apply

In this unit you work on 'Taking away within 20', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: subtract using objects and number lines, and link adding with taking away. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Taking away within 20' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: subtract using objects and number lines, and link adding with taking away. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: 83 - 47. Count up: 47 to 50 is 3, 50 to 80 is 30, 80 to 83 is 3. Total 3 + 30 + 3 = 36. Check: 36 + 47 = 83. Method: Model run for 'Taking away within 20'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Take away objects: 12 cubes, remove 5. Cross out five cubes and count the rest: 7 are left. So 12 - 5 = 7. Say it as: twelve take away five leaves seven.
  2. Step 2, Count back on a number line: for 15 - 4 start on 15 and hop back 4: 14, 13, 12, 11. You land on 11, so 15 - 4 = 11. Count the hops, not the start number.
  3. Step 3, Link to adding: since 11 + 4 = 15, then 15 - 4 = 11 must be true. Write the fact family: 4 + 11 = 15, 11 + 4 = 15, 15 - 4 = 11, 15 - 11 = 4. These four go together.
  4. Step 4, Subtract using ten: 14 - 6. Take away 4 to reach 10, then take away the other 2 to get 8. So 14 - 6 = 8. Check by adding: 8 + 6 = 14, which matches the start.
  5. Step 5, Word problem: there are 13 birds on a wall and 7 fly off. Work 13 - 7: 13 - 3 = 10, then 10 - 4 = 6. Answer: 6 birds are left. Check: 6 + 7 = 13.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: solve 16 - 9 and write its fact family. Answer: 16 - 6 = 10, then 10 - 3 = 7, so 16 - 9 = 7. The family is 9 + 7 = 16, 7 + 9 = 16, 16 - 9 = 7, 16 - 7 = 9.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Subtract using objects and number lines, and link adding with taking away: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 5 Shapes around us Weeks 20 to 23Remember

In this unit you work on 'Shapes around us', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: name and sort flat and solid shapes by their features. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Shapes around us' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: name and sort flat and solid shapes by their features. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: A rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry; a square has 4; a cube has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices. Method: Model run for 'Shapes around us'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Name flat shapes: a clock face is a circle with one curved side and no corners. A book cover is a rectangle with 4 sides and 4 corners. A sandwich cut corner to corner makes a triangle with 3 sides.
  2. Step 2, Count sides and corners: a square has 4 equal sides and 4 corners. A rectangle also has 4 sides, but two are long and two are short. Tap each side and say the count to be sure.
  3. Step 3, Name solid shapes: a ball is a sphere, a tin of beans is a cylinder, a dice is a cube, and a party hat is a cone. Solids can be held and have faces you can feel.
  4. Step 4, Sort by a feature: put a ball, a dice, a tin and a box in two groups, can roll and cannot roll. Ball and tin roll. Dice and box have flat faces only, so they do not roll.
  5. Step 5, Find shapes in a face: a cube has 6 flat faces and every face is a square. Trace around one face of a dice on paper and you draw a square with 4 equal sides.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: name the shape of a coin, a cereal box and a football, and say which of them roll. Answer: circle (flat face of a cylinder), cuboid, sphere. The coin and football roll; the box does not.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Name and sort flat and solid shapes by their features: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 6 Length and weight Weeks 24 to 27Analyse

In this unit you work on 'Length and weight', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: compare and order objects by length and weight using everyday words. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Length and weight' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: compare and order objects by length and weight using everyday words. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: 2 m 35 cm = 200 + 35 = 235 cm. 1.5 kg = 1.5 x 1000 = 1500 g. Method: Model run for 'Length and weight'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Compare length: lay a pencil and a crayon with their left ends on the same line. The pencil sticks out further, so the pencil is longer and the crayon is shorter. Starting at the same line is the key.
  2. Step 2, Order three objects: a ribbon, a straw and a toothpick. Line them up from one edge. The order from shortest to longest is toothpick, straw, ribbon. Say: the ribbon is the longest of the three.
  3. Step 3, Measure with cubes: a book is 9 cubes long and a cup is 4 cubes tall. Because 9 is more than 4, the book is longer than the cup is tall. Count with no gaps and no overlaps.
  4. Step 4, Compare weight: hold a bag of rice in one hand and a feather in the other. The rice feels heavier and the feather lighter. A balance tips down on the heavy side, which confirms it.
  5. Step 5, Order by weight: a pumpkin, an apple and a grape. Put them on a balance two at a time. The order from lightest to heaviest is grape, apple, pumpkin. Use words heavier and lighter.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: a rope is 12 cubes long and a stick is 8 cubes long. Which is longer, and by how many cubes? Answer: the rope is longer by 4 cubes, since 12 - 8 = 4.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Compare and order objects by length and weight using everyday words: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 7 Time Weeks 28 to 31Understand

In this unit you work on 'Time', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: tell time to the hour and half hour and order the days of the week. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Time' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: tell time to the hour and half hour and order the days of the week. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: A train leaves at 9:45 and the journey takes 2 hours 30 minutes. 9:45 + 2:00 = 11:45, then + 30 minutes = 12:15. Method: Model run for 'Time'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Read o'clock: the short hour hand points to 3 and the long minute hand points to 12. That is 3 o'clock. When the minute hand is on 12, we say o'clock and read only the hour hand.
  2. Step 2, Read half past: the minute hand points to 6 and the hour hand is between 4 and 5. That is half past 4, written 4:30. Half past means the long hand has gone half way round.
  3. Step 3, Show a time: to show half past 7 draw the minute hand on 6. Draw the hour hand halfway between 7 and 8. Check that the long hand points down, since half past always points down.
  4. Step 4, Order the days: Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday. Seven days in a week. If today is Tuesday, tomorrow is Wednesday and yesterday was Monday.
  5. Step 5, Day after problem: today is Thursday. Two days later is Saturday, since Friday is one and Saturday is two. Check by counting on the fingers: Friday, Saturday. Answer: Saturday.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: what time is it when the hour hand is on 9 and the minute hand on 12? And what day comes after Friday? Answer: 9 o'clock, and the day after Friday is Saturday.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Tell time to the hour and half hour and order the days of the week: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 8 Money Weeks 32 to 34Remember

In this unit you work on 'Money', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: name coins and notes and make small amounts. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Money' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: name coins and notes and make small amounts. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: 3 books at 45 each: 3 x 45 = 135. Paid 200, so change = 200 - 135 = 65. Method: Model run for 'Money'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Name the coins: use a coin with the number 1, one with 5 and one with 10. Say the value of each: one, five, ten. Larger number does not always mean bigger coin, so read the number.
  2. Step 2, Make 7 with coins: use one 5 coin and two 1 coins. Count: 5, then 6, 7. So 5 + 1 + 1 = 7. Start with the largest coin and count on with the smaller ones.
  3. Step 3, Make 10 two ways: one 10 coin, or two 5 coins. Both give 10 because 5 + 5 = 10. Different coins can make the same amount, and you can use fewer coins with larger values.
  4. Step 4, Count a handful: a 10 coin, a 5 coin and three 1 coins. Count 10, 15, 16, 17, 18. The total is 18. Counting the biggest first keeps your count quick and correct.
  5. Step 5, Shop problem: a pencil costs 6 and you pay with a 10 coin. Count up from 6 to 10: that is 4 more. So the change is 4. Check: 6 + 4 = 10.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: make 12 using only 5 and 1 coins, then find change from 10 for a sticker costing 3. Answer: two 5 coins and two 1 coins make 12; change is 7 since 3 + 7 = 10.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Name coins and notes and make small amounts: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Unit 9 Patterns and sorting Weeks 35 to 36Apply

In this unit you work on 'Patterns and sorting', taught in a way that is short, playful, with objects, pictures and talk. The target is clear: continue and make patterns and sort by one rule. You move from seeing it, to saying it, to doing it alone.

  • Show it with objects or a picture: build or draw the idea in 'Patterns and sorting' before using symbols.
  • Say the rule in your own words, then write one worked line, naming each step.
  • Use it: continue and make patterns and sort by one rule. Start with one easy question, then one with a real-life story.
  • Check it: use a second method, estimate, or work backwards to test your answer.
Worked example. Solved: 3, 7, 11, 15: add 4 each time, so next is 19. The nth term is 4n - 1; check n = 4: 15. Method: Model run for 'Patterns and sorting'. Read the question and underline what is asked. Draw or write what is known. Choose the method named in the unit. Work it line by line, one idea per line, with units where they apply. Estimate first so the answer can be tested for sense. Check by working backwards. What a good answer looks like: correct method, every step visible, units stated, and a one-line check.

Step by step.

  1. Step 1, Continue a colour pattern: red, blue, red, blue, red, __. The unit that repeats is red, blue. After red comes blue, so the next one is blue. Read the whole pattern aloud twice.
  2. Step 2, Find the repeating unit: circle, circle, square, circle, circle, square. The unit is circle, circle, square, three shapes long. Next you draw circle, circle, square again to extend it.
  3. Step 3, Number pattern: 2, 4, 6, 8, __. Each number is 2 more than the one before. So the next is 8 + 2 = 10. Say the rule in a sentence: add 2 each time.
  4. Step 4, Make a pattern: use claps and stamps. Clap, clap, stamp, clap, clap, stamp is an AAB pattern. Show it with blocks: yellow, yellow, green, yellow, yellow, green.
  5. Step 5, Sort by one rule: buttons, 5 red and 3 blue. Sort by colour into two groups. Count each group: 5 and 3. There are 2 more red than blue, as 5 - 3 = 2.
  6. Step 6, Your turn: continue star, heart, heart, star, heart, heart, __, __ and sort 4 big and 6 small balls by size. Answer: star, heart; and the groups have 4 big and 6 small, so 2 more small.

Activity. About 20 to 30 minutes of class work, then one short practice task for home. Keep evidence: one page in your learning book.

Standard to meet. Continue and make patterns and sort by one rule: at least 4 of 5 attempts correct, said or written, with one real example from home or school.

Slide 1. L1 Basic: Count and name shapes (this course)

On the slide: L1 Basic · Count and name shapes

Notes: Count objects up to 20 and name a circle, square and triangle. Notes: give ten buttons and two trays; learners count aloud, move three across and say how many are left (7), then hunt the room for one shape of each kind.

This course: Grade 01 Mathematics is at this level. Its units include Numbers to 20; Numbers to 100; Adding within 20; Taking away within 20 and more.

Slide 2. L2 Awareness: Add, subtract and measure

On the slide: L2 Awareness · Add, subtract and measure

Notes: Add and subtract within 100 and measure a length with a ruler. Notes: work 46 + 27 = 73 on a number line, jumping 20 then 7; learners measure a pencil in centimetres and explain how they checked their answer a second way.

Slide 3. L3 Foundation: Fractions and decimals

On the slide: L3 Foundation · Fractions and decimals

Notes: Compare fractions and decimals and use the four operations with whole numbers. Notes: shade 3/4 and 6/8 on equal strips to show they match, then add 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75 and write it as 75 per cent of one whole.

Slide 4. L4 Practitioner: Equations and percentages

On the slide: L4 Practitioner · Equations and percentages

Notes: Solve one-step and two-step equations and percentage problems, showing each step. Notes: practice case is 3x + 5 = 20, so x = 5, checked by substituting back; then a 15 per cent discount on 400 leaves 340, because 400 x 0.85 = 340.

Slide 5. L5 Specialist: Geometry, data and graphs

On the slide: L5 Specialist · Geometry, data and graphs

Notes: Use the right-angled triangle rule, the mean and a straight-line graph to solve a problem. Notes: legs of 6 and 8 give a longest side of 10; the data 4, 6, 6, 8, 11 have mean 7; plot y = 2x + 1 and read y = 7 at x = 3.

Slide 6. L6 Advanced (degree level): Proof, calculus and algebra

On the slide: L6 Advanced · Proof, calculus and algebra

Notes: Prove results and use calculus and matrices to model change. Notes: differentiate f(x) = x^3 - 3x to get 3x^2 - 3, so turning points sit at x = 1 and x = -1; the second derivative 6x shows a minimum then a maximum. Learners prove the square root of 2 is irrational.

Slide 7. L7 Super expert (postgraduate/expert): Research-level modelling

On the slide: L7 Super expert · Research-level modelling

Notes: Formulate and defend a proof or statistical model, stating assumptions and limits. Notes: for 25 values with mean 50 and standard deviation 10, the standard error is 2 and a 95 per cent interval is about 45.9 to 54.1 (t = 2.064). Learners check residuals and write a referee-style critique.

Notes: a level is reached when you can meet its standard, not by age. Learners may move up or down a level by subject.

ten locations and global

Localised for your place

Teach in the local language first, with English support. Use local currency, units and examples in every worksheet. Local curriculum and ministry rules are checked before use in a school; Arabic, Islamic, Social and Moral content needs licensed educators and ministry review. Child-safety rules apply for every learner under 18.

India (Bengaluru)

Use INR in money problems; deliver in English, Hindi, Kannada; personal data rule: Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Netherlands (Eindhoven area)

Use EUR in money problems; deliver in Dutch, English; personal data rule: EU GDPR (Dutch: AVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

UAE (Dubai and Abu Dhabi)

Use AED in money problems; deliver in Arabic, English; personal data rule: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (personal data protection); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Brazil (Sao Paulo)

Use BRL in money problems; deliver in Portuguese (Spanish and English support); personal data rule: LGPD (Lei Geral de Protecao de Dados); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Canada (Toronto)

Use CAD in money problems; deliver in English, French; personal data rule: PIPEDA, with provincial rules where they apply; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

United States

Use USD in money problems; deliver in English, Spanish; personal data rule: state privacy laws (for example California), and COPPA for children under 13; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Singapore

Use SGD in money problems; deliver in English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil; personal data rule: Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA); guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Australia

Use AUD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Hong Kong (Eastern Asia)

Use HKD in money problems; deliver in Chinese (Cantonese, Mandarin), English; personal data rule: the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Georgia and Israel (Western Asia)

Use GEL or ILS in money problems; deliver in Georgian, Hebrew, Arabic, English; personal data rule: the personal data law of the country where the learner lives; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Cayman Islands (Caribbean)

Use KYD in money problems; deliver in English; personal data rule: the Data Protection Act; guardian contact for every learner under 18.

Mexico

Use MXN in money problems; deliver in Spanish, English; personal data rule: the federal law on personal data held by private parties; guardian contact for every learner under 18.
