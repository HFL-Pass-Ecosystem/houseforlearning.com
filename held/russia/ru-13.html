HELD · RU-13

Condiments Business

Make, cost, label, store and sell sauces, spice blends and similar condiments safely: recipes, batch costing, hygiene, shelf life, labelling and small-scale selling.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner28 h of lessons plus practiceHELD

Make, cost, label, store and sell sauces, spice blends and similar condiments safely: recipes, batch costing, hygiene, shelf life, labelling and small-scale selling.

  • Understand Explain how acidity, salt, sugar, heat and packaging control spoilage in condiments.
  • Apply Scale a recipe by weight and calculate yield, cost per jar and selling price.
  • Analyse Compare hazards in a recipe and identify the control points that must be monitored.
  • Evaluate Judge whether a product and kitchen meet hygiene, labelling and registration needs.
  • Create Build a batch record, label draft and launch plan for one condiment.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Scale and cost one condiment recipe, write a hazard and control sheet, draft a label and plan a small launch.

Condition

Desk exercise with a recipe, price list and spreadsheet; 4 hours; no production claims.

Standard

Yield and cost correct to 2 percent, every control point has a limit and a record, label has all required elements, and at least 90 percent of rubric points.

Who it is for: Home cooks becoming small producers, market sellers, small food shops and cafe owners adding a house sauce. Requirements: Kitchen basics and arithmetic. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Food production is regulated. Registration, premises approval, hygiene training, product safety checks, labelling, allergen information, traceability and shelf-life claims are set by the relevant federal rules and the competent authorities. Low-acid, oil-based or home-canned products can carry serious risks such as botulism; do not sell them without proper process validation. Check with the competent Russian authority and a local adviser before you sell food. This course does not replace laboratory testing or official approval.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Food safety basics for condiments 4 hUnderstand

Condiments last because of acid, salt, sugar, low water or heat treatment. Remove or weaken one of those and spoilage or illness can follow. Safe condiments are designed, measured and recorded, never judged by taste or smell, because dangerous bacteria and their toxins give no warning.

  • Explain pH, water activity and why they matter.
  • List hazards: biological, chemical, physical, allergens.
  • Know the danger of low-acid and oil-based products without control.
  • Wash hands, separate raw and ready foods, clean and sanitise surfaces.
Worked example. A sauce at pH 3.8 is acid enough to limit many bacteria, while the same sauce with added garlic in oil at pH 5.5 is not. The second needs expert process control or must not be sold. Check: 5.5 - 3.8 = 1.7 pH units, a fifty-fold change in acidity; the pH must be measured with a calibrated meter on each batch, and a result above the confirmed limit means the batch is held back.

Activity. Classify five condiments as high or low risk with a reason.

Standard to meet. Reasons refer to acid, water activity or process.

Module 2 Recipes by weight and scaling 3 hApply

Weigh, do not measure by volume. A recipe in percentages scales to any batch. Weigh every ingredient on a scale checked against a known weight, and write the real figure on the batch sheet, not the planned one.

  • Convert the recipe to grams and percent of total.
  • Allow for cooking loss.
  • Compute yield by weighing before and after.
  • Record each batch with lot numbers.
Worked example. Recipe: 4.0 kg tomato, 0.6 kg sugar, 0.2 kg salt, 0.4 kg vinegar = 5.2 kg raw. After cooking the batch weighs 4.4 kg: loss 15.4 percent. Jars of 250 g: 17 jars (4.25 kg) with 150 g left over. Check: 4.0 + 0.6 + 0.2 + 0.4 = 5.2 kg; 5.2 - 4.4 = 0.8 kg lost, 0.8 / 5.2 = 15.4 percent; 17 x 250 g = 4,250 g and 4,400 - 4,250 = 150 g. For a 10 kg batch multiply each weight by 10 / 5.2 = 1.92.

Activity. Scale a recipe to a 10 kg batch and compute yield.

Standard to meet. Weights and yield correct to 2 percent.

Module 3 Costing a batch 4 hApply

Know cost per jar before setting a price. Include ingredients, packaging, labour, energy and waste. Many small producers underprice because they forget their own time; a price that pays for ingredients only will end the business when the first big cost rises.

  • Cost each ingredient per kg.
  • Add jars, lids, labels and energy.
  • Add labour time at a fixed hourly rate.
  • Add a waste allowance of 3 to 5 percent.
Worked example. Batch 17 jars. Ingredients 1,150 RUB, jars and lids 17 x 22 = 374 RUB, labels 51 RUB, energy 90 RUB, labour 3 h x 300 = 900 RUB. Total 2,565 RUB; per jar 150.9 RUB. With a 35 percent margin: price = 150.9 / 0.65 = 232 RUB. Check: 1,150 + 374 + 51 + 90 + 900 = 2,565; 2,565 / 17 = 150.9; a 4 percent waste allowance adds about 103 RUB to the batch, giving 2,668 RUB or 157 RUB per jar and a price of 241 RUB.

Activity. Cost a 10 kg batch and set a price.

Standard to meet. Cost per jar within 2 percent.

Module 4 Hazards and control points 4 hAnalyse

Find where things can go wrong and set a measurable limit and a record at each point. A limit must be a number you can measure, such as a temperature or a time, and the monitor must be a tool, not an opinion.

  • Draw the process: receive, store, prepare, cook, fill, seal, cool, store.
  • Mark the steps where a hazard is controlled.
  • Set a limit, how to monitor, and what to do if it fails.
  • Keep monitoring records.
Worked example. Control point: hot fill at not less than 85 C, held 2 minutes, checked with a calibrated thermometer each batch. If 82 C is recorded: re-heat, and record the correction. Check: 85 - 82 = 3 C below the limit, so the batch is not filled until it reaches 85 C again and holds for the full 2 minutes; the thermometer is checked against ice water at 0 C before the shift.

Activity. Build a hazard and control table for one recipe.

Standard to meet. Each control point has limit, monitor and corrective action.

Module 5 Packaging, storage and shelf life 3 hEvaluate

Shelf life is shown by testing, not guessing. Packaging protects but can fail. A poor seal lets air and microbes in, so test seals on every batch and keep a few jars from each lot as retained samples to check later.

  • Choose containers and seals suited to hot fill.
  • Check seals and cool as planned.
  • Store cool, dry and dark; use oldest first.
  • Set the date mark only after expert advice or testing.
Worked example. Trial: 3 jars held at 25 C for 12 weeks, checked every 2 weeks. All passed on appearance, smell and pH; a lab confirms. A conservative label of 6 months is proposed, pending the competent authority rules. Check: 12 weeks at 25 C does not prove 6 months, so the label stays provisional; checks every 2 weeks give 6 readings per jar, 18 readings for 3 jars, and any failure resets the proposed date.

Activity. Write a shelf-life test plan for one product.

Standard to meet. Plan has sample number, conditions, checks and a decision rule.

Module 6 Labels and allergen information 3 hCreate

A label must be truthful and carry the information required by the rules. Allergens must be clear. A buyer with an allergy relies on the label alone, so an error here can cause real harm; have a second person read every label against the recipe before printing.

  • List name, ingredients by weight order, net quantity, date mark, storage, producer details.
  • Highlight allergens as the rules require.
  • Avoid unproven health claims.
  • Add the lot number for traceability.
Worked example. Label for 250 g jar: name, ingredients (tomato 77 percent, sugar, vinegar, salt), net 250 g, lot A0412, storage after opening. Check with the competent authority for the full list. Check: tomato 4.0 kg of 5.2 kg raw is 76.9 percent, shown as 77 percent, and ingredients are listed in descending weight order: tomato, sugar, vinegar, salt.

Activity. Draft a label and mark what you must confirm with the authority.

Standard to meet. No false claims and allergens clear.

Module 7 Registration, premises and compliance 3 hEvaluate

Selling food needs approval and a suitable place. A home kitchen may not qualify. Ask before you buy equipment or print labels, since approval may require a separate room, safe water, waste handling and trained staff that change your costs.

  • Ask which registration your production needs.
  • Check premises, water, waste and pest control.
  • Train staff in food hygiene.
  • Keep supplier and batch records for recall.
Worked example. Recall drill: given lot A0412, find the sold jars within 60 minutes using the sales log: 17 jars, 14 sold to 5 buyers, 3 in stock. Check: 14 + 3 = 17 jars accounted for, with 0 missing; the drill passes only if all 17 are found, the 5 buyers are contacted and the 3 jars are held, all within the 60 minutes.

Activity. Write a compliance checklist and run a recall drill on sample data.

Standard to meet. Checklist names an authority question for each item; drill under 60 minutes.

Module 8 Launch and selling 4 hCreate

Start small, test with real buyers and track the numbers. A small launch limits waste and gives honest feedback on taste, price and jar size before you commit more cash and time.

  • Choose a channel: stall, shop, online, local cafe.
  • Plan batch size to demand.
  • Track sales, waste and feedback.
  • Change one thing at a time.
Worked example. Week 1: 34 jars made, 28 sold at 232 RUB = 6,496 RUB, 6 unsold. Gross profit per jar 81 RUB x 28 = 2,268 RUB. Next batch: 28 jars. Check: 28 x 232 = 6,496; 232 - 151 = 81 and 81 x 28 = 2,268; the 6 unsold jars cost about 906 RUB, so the net gain is 1,362 RUB, and the sell-through rate is 28 / 34 = 82 percent.

Activity. Write a 4-week launch plan with batch sizes and review dates.

Standard to meet. Plan links batch size to sales data.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: a scaled recipe, a batch costing sheet, a hazard and control table, a label draft and a launch plan. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. Why does acid help preserve a sauce?

Model answer: A low pH limits the growth of many bacteria, so acid is a measured control, and the pH of each batch must be checked and recorded.

Q2. What is the risk with garlic in oil?

Model answer: Low acid and no oxygen can allow botulism; do not sell such a product without expert process control and approval.

Q3. Why weigh, not measure by volume?

Model answer: Weight is accurate and scalable, while volume changes with packing and temperature; percentages by weight scale to any batch size.

Q4. Batch 17 jars cost 2,565 RUB. Cost per jar?

Model answer: About 150.9 RUB, found by dividing the batch cost by the number of jars, before waste allowance and margin are added.

Q5. What is a critical control point?

Model answer: A step where a hazard is controlled, with a measurable limit, a way to monitor it, a corrective action and a record.

Q6. Who decides your date mark?

Model answer: Testing or expert advice under the rules of the competent authority; you do not choose it by guessing or by copying another product.

Q7. What must a label show for allergens?

Model answer: They must be clearly identified as the rules require, and a second person should check the label against the recipe.

Q8. Why keep lot numbers?

Model answer: For traceability and recall, so you can find every jar from a faulty batch and the buyers quickly, within the 60 minute drill target.

Q9. Can a home kitchen always be used for sales?

Model answer: No; check premises, water, waste and registration rules with the competent authority before you buy equipment or sell.

Q10. What if a hot fill reads below the limit?

Model answer: Re-heat to the limit, hold the full time, record the correction and review why it happened so it does not repeat.
