Cosmetology and Pharmaceutics

Learn the safe basics of cosmetic products and pharmacy practice: skin and hair science, ingredients, simple formulation arithmetic, hygiene and batch records, labelling, storage, dosing arithmetic, and the duty to refer and to stay inside licence.

about this course

What you will be able to do

L4 Practitioner36 h of lessons plus practiceHELD

Learn the safe basics of cosmetic products and pharmacy practice: skin and hair science, ingredients, simple formulation arithmetic, hygiene and batch records, labelling, storage, dosing arithmetic, and the duty to refer and to stay inside licence.

  • Understand Explain the legal and practical difference between a cosmetic, a medicine and a medical device.
  • Apply Calculate a batch formula by percent and weight, and fill in a batch and hygiene record.
  • Analyse Read a product label and ingredient list and flag allergen, preservative and claim issues.
  • Evaluate Judge a client or customer request and decide whether to proceed, refer to a qualified professional or decline.
  • Create Design a hygiene, storage and complaint system for a small cosmetic service or retail point.

Task, condition and standard

Task

Produce a batch formula and record for a simple 5 kg cosmetic cream (written), a label check, a storage plan, a dose-arithmetic worksheet and a referral and complaints sheet.

Condition

Open-book; 6 hours; calculator, templates and sample labels; written case only; no products made for sale and no advice given to real patients.

Standard

Formula sums to 100 percent and the weights add up within 1 percent; batch record complete; label check finds 6 planted issues; referral decision correct for 4 of 5 cases; pass mark 80 percent.

Who it is for: Beauty and personal care workers, small cosmetic makers, pharmacy assistants and learners who wish to understand safety, quality and limits. Requirements: Adult learner; basic chemistry and arithmetic; clean hands and good hygiene habits. Materials: lesson text, worked examples, templates, rubric, 10-question check.

Safety and limits: Cosmetic products, medicines and medical procedures are regulated. Making or selling cosmetics needs compliance with technical rules, safety assessment, labelling and sometimes registration; pharmacy work needs a qualified pharmacist, licensed premises and prescription rules; invasive treatments (injections, deep peels, lasers) need medical qualifications and licences. Check with the competent Russian authority and a local adviser. Nothing here is medical advice, and no product is claimed to cure disease. Refer skin conditions, infections and reactions to a doctor.

lesson by lesson

8 modules with worked examples

Module 1 Scope, law and boundaries 4 hUnderstand

What a product claims to do decides how it is regulated. A cosmetic cleans, perfumes, protects or changes appearance; a medicine treats or prevents disease.

  • Cosmetic: applied to skin, hair, nails or lips for cleaning, care or appearance. Medicine: claims to treat or prevent disease. Medical device: acts by physical means for a medical purpose.
  • Claims like "cures acne" or "removes scars" turn a cosmetic into an unlawful medicine claim. Keep claims modest and true.
  • Roles: a beautician works within cosmetic care; a pharmacist dispenses; a doctor diagnoses and prescribes. Know where your role stops.
  • Keep records of product safety files, supplier certificates and staff training.
Worked example. Claim check on five labels: "moisturises dry skin" OK; "heals eczema" not OK; "reduces the look of fine lines" OK; "cures fungal infection" not OK; "cleanses oily skin" OK. Result: 2 labels fail and must be rewritten.

Activity. Sort 12 claims and tasks into cosmetic, medicine, device or refer. Explain 4 choices.

Standard to meet. At least 11 of 12 correct; each not-OK claim has a lawful rewrite.

Module 2 Skin, hair and common conditions 4 hUnderstand

Skin is a barrier and an organ. Knowing normal structure and warning signs helps you care safely and refer in time.

  • The skin has an epidermis, dermis and subcutaneous layer; the surface is slightly acidic, around pH 4.5 to 5.5.
  • Skin types: normal, dry, oily, combination and sensitive. Type can change with season and age.
  • Warning signs that need a doctor: a changing or bleeding mole, spreading redness, pus, fever, severe pain, sudden hair loss or a rash with swelling.
  • Sun protection and gentle cleansing are the base of care; avoid claims beyond that.
Worked example. Client with a mole that changed colour over 2 months and has an uneven edge. Decision: do not treat or advise products; recommend a doctor visit soon; record the date and the referral.

Activity. Describe five skin types, one care step each, and five warning signs with the action to take.

Standard to meet. All five types and warning signs correct; action is referral where needed.

Module 3 Ingredients, labels and safety 5 hAnalyse

The label is a legal document and a safety tool. Learn to read it and judge it.

  • The ingredient list uses standard international names in descending order of amount.
  • Common groups: water, emollients, emulsifiers, humectants, thickeners, preservatives, fragrance, colour. Each has a role and limits.
  • Allergens and restricted substances must be declared or limited under the relevant rules; check each against the official lists.
  • Check the label: product name and function, net content, ingredients, batch, best-before or period-after-opening, maker's name and address, warnings.
Worked example. Label check: net content missing, batch missing, "cures acne" claim, fragrance allergen not listed, maker address absent, open-jar symbol missing. 6 issues found; fix before sale.

Activity. Check three sample labels and list issues with a lawful fix for each.

Standard to meet. At least 6 planted issues found; fixes are specific.

Module 4 Formulation arithmetic 5 hApply

Cosmetic formulas are written in percent of the total. Weights follow the percent exactly.

  • Weight = percent x batch weight. Parts must total 100 percent.
  • Water phase and oil phase are heated and mixed with an emulsifier to make an emulsion; follow safe temperature and hygiene steps.
  • Preservative level is set by the supplier data and legal limit. Never skip preservation in a water-based product.
  • Check pH with a calibrated meter or strips; record it, and adjust only by a written method.
Worked example. Batch 5.000 kg: water 70 percent = 3.500 kg; oils 25 percent = 1.250 kg; emulsifier 4 percent = 0.200 kg; preservative 1 percent = 0.050 kg. Total 5.000 kg. Tolerance for weighing: 1 percent of 0.050 kg = 0.0005 kg, so weigh to 0.1 g.

Activity. Scale a formula to 2 kg, 5 kg and 12 kg and check the totals.

Standard to meet. Totals are 100 percent; weights within 1 percent; unit labels correct.

Module 5 Hygiene, GMP and batch records 5 hApply

Good manufacturing practice means clean people, clean rooms, clean equipment and written records so any batch can be traced.

  • Wash hands, wear clean clothing and a hair cover; no food or drink in the work area. Clean and sanitise surfaces and tools before and after.
  • Keep a batch record: product, formula version, batch number, date, raw material lots, weights, temperatures, pH, operator, checks and release.
  • Label raw materials with the date received and opened; use oldest first; quarantine rejected material.
  • Keep a retained sample of each batch and test for appearance, smell, pH and microbial quality as required.
Worked example. Batch 24-017: planned 5.000 kg, produced 4.920 kg; yield 98.4 percent. Raw lots recorded; pH 5.2 within 5.0 to 5.6; release signed by a second person.

Activity. Complete a batch record for the 5 kg cream, including a deviation note for one planted error.

Standard to meet. Record complete; yield calculated; deviation recorded with action.

Module 6 Storage, shelf life and traceability 4 hApply

Storage and dating keep products safe. Traceability lets you recall a bad batch quickly.

  • Store cool, dry and away from sun. Medicines have stated storage ranges, such as 2 to 8 degrees Celsius for cold-chain products; follow the label.
  • Keep a temperature log with checks at least twice daily for cold storage and act on any excursion.
  • Period after opening (months) and minimum shelf life must be on the label; use first-expiry, first-out.
  • Keep records to trace each batch to supplier and customer so a recall can reach everyone in 24 hours.
Worked example. Fridge for cold-chain items: reading 9.5 degrees Celsius at 14:00, limit 8. Action: move stock to a working fridge, quarantine, record the time and duration, ask the maker or pharmacist whether the stock can be used.

Activity. Make a storage plan with ranges, a log sheet and a mock recall sheet.

Standard to meet. Ranges stated; excursion action written; recall sheet traces 3 batches.

Module 7 Pharmacy basics: dispensing arithmetic and limits 5 hEvaluate

Pharmacy work is precise and regulated. The arithmetic can be learned here, but dosing decisions belong to the prescriber and pharmacist.

  • Only a qualified pharmacist, with the licensed premises and a valid prescription where required, dispenses prescription medicines.
  • Dose by weight: dose = mg per kg x weight in kg; volume = dose divided by concentration. Always use the figures on the prescription or the official instructions.
  • Check the five rights: right person, medicine, dose, time and route; check allergies and interactions.
  • Refer any doubt, error or reaction to the pharmacist or doctor at once.
Worked example. Teaching arithmetic only: prescribed 15 mg per kg per dose, child 12 kg: 180 mg. Syrup is 120 mg per 5 mL: volume = 180 / 120 x 5 = 7.5 mL. Check by the pharmacist before any real use.

Activity. Calculate five teaching doses and mark where a pharmacist check is required.

Standard to meet. All calculations correct; each case has a named checker; no real advice given.

Module 8 Reactions, complaints and referral system 4 hCreate

Good systems protect people when something goes wrong. Keep a clear path from complaint to learning.

  • Do a patch test as the product maker advises (for example, small area, 24 to 48 hours) before using a new product on a client, and record the result.
  • If a reaction occurs, stop the product, rinse, advise medical help for swelling, breathing difficulty or blistering, and call emergency services if breathing is affected.
  • Log each complaint with date, product, batch, symptoms, action and result; report serious events to the competent authority.
  • Review the log monthly to find patterns by batch or supplier.
Worked example. Month log: 1,200 uses, 6 mild reactions = 0.5 percent; 4 of 6 from batch 24-021. Action: quarantine the batch, contact the supplier, inform clients, record.

Activity. Design a one-page referral, reaction and complaints system and apply it to five cases.

Standard to meet. Escalation path clear; batch pattern found; records complete.

assessment

How you are checked

Evidence: batch formula and record, label check sheet, storage and hygiene plan, dose arithmetic worksheet, referral and complaints sheet and a short compliance note. A mentor and one peer mark it with this rubric. Pass: no criterion at 'Not yet', and at least 8 of 10 check answers right.

Ten-question check, with model answers

Q1. What makes a product a medicine rather than a cosmetic?

Model answer: A claim or purpose to treat or prevent disease.

Q2. Name two warning signs that need a doctor.

Model answer: A changing or bleeding mole, spreading redness, pus, fever or severe pain.

Q3. How are ingredients listed?

Model answer: Using standard names in descending order of amount.

Q4. A 5 kg batch has 4 percent emulsifier. What weight?

Model answer: 0.200 kg.

Q5. Why is preservative needed in a water-based product?

Model answer: To stop microbes growing, which could harm users.

Q6. What must a batch record let you do?

Model answer: Trace the product to raw material lots, people, steps and checks.

Q7. Cold-chain stock reads above its limit. What do you do?

Model answer: Move and quarantine it, record the excursion and ask the maker or pharmacist about use.

Q8. Compute a dose: 15 mg per kg, 12 kg child.

Model answer: 180 mg, subject to a pharmacist's check.

Q9. Who may dispense prescription medicines?

Model answer: A qualified pharmacist in licensed premises, within prescription rules.

Q10. What do you do if a client has swelling and breathing difficulty?

Model answer: Call emergency services at once and stop the product.
